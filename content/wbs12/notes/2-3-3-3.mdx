## Internal causes

Within the business's control, which means they are also preventable. That matters, because a question asking whether failure "could have been avoided" is really asking you to sort the causes into these two lists.

<Chunk title="The six internal causes" open>

**Poor management of cash flow.** The most common single cause. Profitable businesses fail because they cannot pay this month's bills. No forecast, no overdraft arranged, no reserve.

**Overestimation of sales.** The optimistic forecast again. Staff, stock and premises are committed on the basis of sales that never arrive, so the cost base is too large for the actual revenue.

**Overtrading.** Growing faster than the working capital allows. The business wins large orders, buys stock and hires staff to fulfil them, pays for all of that now, and waits 60 days to be paid. Sales and profit both rise, and it runs out of cash. **It is failure caused by success**, which is why it catches good businesses.

**Poor inventory control.** Too much stock ties up cash and risks obsolescence. Too little means lost sales and idle production. Either way it is cash mismanaged.

**Poor marketing.** The product may be good and nobody knows, or it is aimed at the wrong segment, or priced wrongly. Revenue never reaches the level the cost base needs.

**Poor quality.** Returns, refunds, warranty costs, lost repeat custom and reputational damage. In a service business, where reviews decide demand, this is fatal fast.

</Chunk>

## External causes

Outside the business's control, though some can be planned for.

- **Market conditions** — demand falls, or tastes move away permanently.
- **Competition** — a new entrant, a price war, a larger rival with deeper pockets.
- **Economic conditions** — recession, falling incomes, weak confidence.
- **Exchange rates** — a weaker currency raises the cost of imported inputs; a stronger one makes exports dearer.
- **Interest rates** — higher rates raise the cost of servicing debt and reduce customer borrowing.
- **Government regulation** — new licensing, safety or tax requirements that raise costs.
- **Supplier problems** — a key supplier fails or raises prices sharply.
- **Natural phenomena** — storms, floods, disease, disruption to travel.

## The distinction that earns marks

External causes are not an excuse. The examinable insight is that **a well-run business is more likely to survive an external shock than a badly-run one**, because it has cash reserves, lower fixed commitments, a spread of customers and no over-reliance on one supplier.

So when a business fails during a recession, the honest analysis is usually: the recession was the trigger, and poor cash management and a narrow customer base were the reasons it could not absorb it. Saying that is a high-level answer.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

The 2020 halt to international travel was as pure an external cause as exists: no forecast could have predicted it and no marketing could have offset it.

The businesses that survived it were mostly the ones that had done the internal things well: held cash from the peak season rather than spending it, kept fixed commitments and debt low, and had good enough relationships with suppliers and staff to renegotiate rather than default. The trigger was external; survival was internal.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on overtrading in particular; Explain (4) on one cause; Assess (10) or Evaluate (20) on why a business failed or whether the failure was avoidable.

**Structure for "why did this business fail?"**

1. **Sort the causes** in the source into internal and external. Say which you think was the trigger.
2. **Develop the main one** as a chain, not a label.
3. **Then the key judgement:** was the business exposed because of its own decisions? Cash reserves, fixed costs, customer concentration, single supplier.
4. Conclude on whether it was avoidable, and what would have had to be different.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Most failures are cash failures**, not profit failures.
- **External triggers meet internal vulnerabilities.** That sentence, applied to the source, is the strongest point available.
- **Overtrading is caused by growth**, which makes it counter-intuitive and worth naming.
- **Some causes interact.** A recession plus high gearing is far worse than either alone.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Blaming only external factors.** It reads as an excuse and misses the analysis.
- **Confusing overtrading with trading at a loss.** Overtrading businesses are usually profitable and growing.
- **Saying the business "ran out of money" with no mechanism.** Say why: credit sales, stock, an asset purchase, debt repayments.
- **Listing causes without identifying the main one.**
- **Assuming failure means the product was bad.** Often the product was fine and the cash was not.
- **Forgetting that poor quality destroys demand fastest in a service business** where reviews drive bookings.

</Mistakes>
