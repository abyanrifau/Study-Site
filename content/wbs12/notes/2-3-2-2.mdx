## Why forecast at all

The sales forecast is the first number in the plan and every other number depends on it:

- **Cash flow.** When money comes in.
- **Staffing.** How many people, and when.
- **Stock and purchasing.** How much to buy and when.
- **Capacity.** Whether the premises and equipment can cope.
- **Finance.** How much is needed, and the evidence a lender needs to see.

That dependency is also the weakness. Get the sales forecast 30% wrong and the cash flow forecast, the staffing plan and the break-even analysis are all wrong too.

## What affects a forecast

<Chunk title="Consumer trends" open>

Tastes, habits and expectations change: health awareness, sustainability, online buying, convenience, demographics.

Some are slow and predictable, such as an ageing population. Some are fast and hard to read, such as a shift in fashion. A forecast built on a trend continuing is only as good as that assumption.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Economic variables">

- **Incomes** — rising incomes raise demand for normal goods, and by more for luxuries. This is YED from WBS11 **1.3.2.5**.
- **Interest rates** — higher rates reduce borrowing and spending, and hit big-ticket and credit-funded purchases hardest.
- **Inflation** — raises costs and squeezes real incomes.
- **Unemployment** — reduces confidence and spending.
- **The business cycle** — boom, downturn, recession, recovery.

<Chunk title="Actions of competitors">

The hardest to forecast, because it depends on other people's decisions. A new entrant, a rival's price cut, a competitor's advertising campaign or a closure can all move your sales sharply, and none of it is visible in your own past data.

</Chunk>

## Why forecasting is difficult

The specification asks for the difficulties, so learn them as a list.

- **No historical data** for a new business or a new product, so the forecast is essentially judgement.
- **The past may not predict the future.** Extrapolation assumes conditions continue.
- **Competitor actions are unknowable.**
- **External shocks** cannot be forecast by definition.
- **Optimism bias.** Founders and managers systematically forecast too high, because they believe in what they are doing.
- **The longer the horizon, the wider the error.** A forecast for next month is far better than one for year three.
- **Fast-changing markets** date a forecast quickly.

## Working with an unreliable forecast

Good practice, and good evaluation material:

- **Forecast a range**, not a single number: worst, likely, best case.
- **Test the plan against the worst case.** Can the business survive it?
- **Forecast by segment or season** rather than an annual average, which hides the difficult months.
- **Update it regularly** against actual sales and investigate the difference.
- **Keep fixed commitments low** where the forecast is unreliable, so a shortfall is survivable.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A new guesthouse has no history, so its forecast has to be built from what is observable: published national arrival statistics, the visible occupancy of neighbouring properties, and the rates and availability shown on booking platforms.

The three specification factors all bite. Consumer trends decide whether budget island tourism keeps growing. Economic variables in the *source markets*, not at home, decide whether people book long-haul holidays at all, because a long-haul holiday is close to a pure luxury in YED terms. And competitor actions matter enormously, because a handful of new guesthouses on the same small island can take a large share of a limited market.

The practical answer is a seasonal forecast with a low case that still services the loan. A forecast that only works at 70% occupancy is not a forecast, it is a hope.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one factor affecting a forecast or one difficulty; Assess (10) on how reliable a given forecast is.

**Answering "how reliable is this forecast?"** Work through:

1. **What is it based on?** Historical data, research, or judgement? No history means low reliability.
2. **How far ahead?** Longer horizon, wider error.
3. **How stable is the market?** Fast-changing or competitive means less reliable.
4. **Is there optimism bias?** Whose forecast is it, and what do they want from it?
5. **Then the consequence:** what happens to the cash flow if it is 20% out? Quantify it if you have the figures.

That last step is what separates a good answer from a list of difficulties.

**Getting evaluation marks:** recommend a range rather than a single figure, and test the plan against the low case. Also note that an unreliable forecast is not a reason to skip forecasting; it is a reason to keep fixed commitments low.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Treating a forecast as a fact.** It is an estimate, and the exam rewards saying so.
- **Naming factors without applying them** to the business in the source.
- **Forgetting competitor actions**, which is the factor most often left out.
- **Ignoring which incomes matter.** For a tourism or export business it is incomes in the source market.
- **Using an annual average** for a seasonal business.
- **Saying the forecast is unreliable and stopping.** Say what follows: what should the business do about it?
- **Missing that everything else in the plan depends on this number.**

</Mistakes>
