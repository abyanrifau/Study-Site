## Inflation

Prices rising across the economy.

**Effects on a business:** input costs rise, wage demands follow, real incomes fall so demand for non-essentials weakens, and menu costs arise from repricing. It also makes planning harder because forecasts date quickly.

**Responses:** pass costs on if demand is inelastic, absorb them and accept a thinner margin, negotiate fixed-price supply contracts, hedge or buy forward, improve productivity to offset the cost, or reduce package sizes rather than raise prices.

**The judgement:** whether you can pass it on depends on price elasticity of demand and on what competitors do. If everyone's costs have risen, passing it on is safer.

## Exchange rates

</Figure>

**A stronger currency:** imports cheaper, so input costs fall for an importer. Exports dearer in foreign markets, so an exporter loses competitiveness.

**A weaker currency:** imports dearer, so input costs rise. Exports cheaper abroad, so an exporter gains.

**Who wins depends entirely on which side of the trade the business is on**, and questions test whether you identify that correctly before you start.

**Responses:** buy forward or hedge, source domestically to reduce exposure, price in your own currency, spread across several markets, or hold foreign currency reserves.

## Interest rates

**A rise means:**

- Existing variable-rate borrowing costs more, which directly reduces profit for a geared business.
- New investment looks less attractive, because the return has to beat a higher cost of finance.
- Customers with mortgages and loans have less to spend, so demand falls, most sharply for big-ticket and credit-funded purchases.
- Saving becomes more attractive, so spending falls further.
- The currency tends to strengthen, which feeds back into the exchange rate effects above.

**Who is hit hardest:** highly geared businesses, and those selling expensive items bought on credit. **Who is barely affected:** debt-free businesses selling cheap essentials.

**Responses:** fix the interest rate, reduce gearing, delay investment, or shift towards products less sensitive to credit.

## Taxation and government spending

**Higher direct taxes** (income tax, corporation tax) reduce disposable income and post-tax profit, so demand and reinvestment both fall.

**Higher indirect taxes** (VAT, duties) raise the price customers pay, which shifts supply left and reduces quantity demanded. Who bears it depends on elasticity, as in WBS11 **1.3.2.2**.

**Government spending** works both ways: it can be a direct source of demand for businesses that supply the public sector, and infrastructure spending lowers costs for everyone. Cuts do the reverse.

## The business cycle

<Figure caption="Output moves around a long-term trend through boom, downturn, recession and recovery. Where a business thinks it is in the cycle should shape its decisions.">
  <BusinessCycleDiagram />
</Figure>

| Stage | What happens | What a business should do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Boom** | high demand, rising prices, labour shortages, confidence | invest, build capacity, and keep cash back for the downturn |
| **Downturn** | growth slows, confidence falls | control costs, avoid new fixed commitments |
| **Recession** | falling output, unemployment, weak demand | protect cash, cut discretionary spending, consider cheaper product lines |
| **Recovery** | demand returns, confidence rebuilds | rebuild capacity and stock, invest ahead of the curve |

**The good evaluation point:** businesses with a high income elasticity of demand (luxuries) swing hardest through the cycle, and those selling necessities barely move. Which one this business is determines how exposed it is.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

An import-dependent, tourism-led economy is unusually exposed to two of these at once.

**Exchange rates** hit from the input side. Almost everything a guesthouse uses is imported, so a weaker local currency raises the cost of food, fuel and building materials with nothing about the product having changed.

**The business cycle abroad, not at home,** drives demand. A long-haul holiday is close to a pure luxury, so a recession in the main source markets cuts bookings sharply even while the domestic economy is fine. The practical response is to spread across more source markets, which is the same risk-reduction logic as holding products with different income elasticities.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) and Analyse (6) very often, and Assess (10) on how a business should respond to a named change.

**The chain that earns analysis marks.** Always run it in three steps:

> The change → the effect on this business's costs or demand → what the business should do about it.

For example: interest rates rise 2% → the business has $400,000 of variable-rate debt, so annual interest rises by around $8,000, and customers buying on credit reduce spending → so it should consider fixing the rate and shifting promotion towards cash buyers.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Identify which side of the trade the business is on** before discussing exchange rates.
- **Size the effect.** Use the figures. A 2% rate rise on $400,000 is $8,000; on $40,000 it is $800.
- **Some businesses are barely affected.** Debt-free sellers of essentials are largely insulated.
- **Effects interact.** Higher interest rates strengthen the currency, which helps importers and hurts exporters.
- **Timing.** A temporary change may not justify a permanent response.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Getting SPICED backwards.** Strong currency: imports **cheaper**, exports **dearer**.
- **Assuming a weaker currency is bad.** It is good for an exporter.
- **Saying higher interest rates "are bad for business"** without saying for which business and by how much.
- **Confusing direct and indirect taxes.**
- **Treating inflation as only a cost problem.** It also reduces customers' real incomes.
- **Naming the change without the response.** The specification asks how businesses can best respond.
- **Forgetting that for a tourism or export business, the relevant economy is the customer's.**

</Mistakes>
