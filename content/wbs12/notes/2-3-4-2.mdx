## The calculation

<Formula name="Capacity utilisation" note="Always a percentage. Current output goes on top.">
Capacity utilisation = (Current output ÷ Maximum possible output) × 100
</Formula>

<Figure caption="Below about 60% fixed costs per unit are high and resources sit idle. Around 90% is usually the target. At 100% there is no slack for breakdowns, maintenance or an unexpected order.">

</Figure>

<Worked>

A factory can produce **12,000** units a month and is currently producing **9,000**. Fixed costs are **$36,000** a month.

`Capacity utilisation = (9,000 ÷ 12,000) × 100 = 75%`

`Fixed cost per unit = $36,000 ÷ 9,000 = $4.00`

**If output rises to 11,000 units** with no change in capacity or fixed costs:

`Utilisation = (11,000 ÷ 12,000) × 100 = 91.7%`

`Fixed cost per unit = $36,000 ÷ 11,000 = $3.27`

**Reading it:** raising utilisation from 75% to 91.7% cut the fixed cost per unit by 73 cents, about 18%, with no new investment at all. The machinery and the rent were already being paid for; they are simply being used.

**The condition, which is the evaluation point:** this only helps if the extra 2,000 units can actually be *sold*. Producing them into a warehouse raises costs and raises nothing else.

</Worked>

## Why high utilisation is good

- **Lower fixed cost per unit**, which means lower average cost and a better margin or a more competitive price.
- **Assets are earning.** Equipment and premises paid for and unused are pure cost.
- **Staff are busy**, which is usually better for morale than standing idle.

## Why 100% is not the target

This is the part students miss.

- **No slack for breakdowns or maintenance.** Machines running flat out get no servicing, so they fail, and then output stops entirely.
- **No capacity for a new order.** A profitable large order has to be turned down, or a customer is let down.
- **Staff strain.** No time for training, holidays, absence cover or improvement, and errors and turnover rise.
- **Quality falls** when there is no time to check work.
- **No room to grow** without capital investment and the delay that brings.

So the sensible target is **high but not maximum**, usually quoted as around 90%.

## Dealing with under-utilisation

**Raise demand:** promotion, price cuts if demand is elastic, new markets or segments, new products using the same capacity.

**Use the spare capacity:** subcontract for other businesses, produce own-label for a retailer, rent out unused space or equipment.

**Reduce capacity (rationalisation):** close a site, sell equipment, reduce staff. It cuts fixed costs, and it is hard to reverse, damages morale, and carries redundancy costs.

The judgement is about **whether the low demand is temporary or permanent**. Temporary means keep the capacity and bridge the gap. Permanent means rationalise. Getting that wrong is expensive in either direction.

## Dealing with over-utilisation

**Increase capacity:** invest in equipment or premises, take on staff, add a shift. Slow and capital-intensive.

**Get output elsewhere:** subcontract, outsource.

**Manage demand:** raise prices to reduce it and improve the margin, lengthen lead times, prioritise the most profitable customers.

**Improve productivity:** get more from existing capacity through better methods, which is the cheapest route if it is available.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Occupancy *is* capacity utilisation, and it makes the theory visible. A ten-room guesthouse selling 210 room nights in a 30-day month is at 70%.

Both problems occur in the same year. In the wet season, utilisation might be 30%, so the fixed costs, loan, salaried staff and electricity, are spread across very few guests and the fixed cost per room night is crippling. In the peak, it is at or near 100%, so a late enquiry has to be turned away and there is no room to take a group booking.

The responses are the standard ones: discount and market the low season to lift utilisation, and raise rates in the peak to manage demand and improve the margin on the rooms available. That is exactly why rates vary so widely by season, and it is a capacity decision as much as a pricing one.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) very often, Explain (4) on one implication, Assess (10) on how a business should respond to under or over-utilisation.

**On the calculation:** output on top, multiply by 100, and give a percentage. If asked for maximum capacity from a percentage, divide rather than multiply.

**Then always take the second step:** work out the fixed cost per unit at both levels. That converts a percentage into a business consequence, which is where the marks are.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Higher utilisation only helps if the output can be sold.**
- **100% is not the target**, and saying why is a reliable mark.
- **Temporary or permanent?** That decides whether to bridge the gap or rationalise.
- **Rationalisation is hard to reverse** and damages morale.
- **Seasonal businesses cannot be judged on an annual figure.** Look at the peak and the trough separately.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing maximum by current output.** Current goes on top.
- **Forgetting to multiply by 100**, or giving a decimal rather than a percentage.
- **Saying 100% utilisation is ideal.** It leaves no slack at all.
- **Assuming extra output is automatically sold.**
- **Confusing capacity utilisation with productivity.** Utilisation is about how much of your capacity you use; productivity is output per worker.
- **Recommending rationalisation for a seasonal dip.** The capacity will be needed again in three months.
- **Ignoring the fixed cost per unit effect**, which is the whole reason utilisation matters.

</Mistakes>
