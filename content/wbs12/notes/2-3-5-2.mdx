## The six areas

<Chunk title="Consumer protection" open>

Goods must be as described, fit for purpose and of satisfactory quality, and selling must not mislead.

**Costs:** refunds and replacements, accurate labelling, staff training, returns handling.

**Benefits:** consumers trust the market and are more willing to buy, and honest businesses are not undercut by dishonest ones.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Employee protection">

Minimum wage, contracts, maximum hours, holiday and sick entitlement, protection from discrimination, and fair dismissal procedures.

**Costs:** a higher wage bill, more administration, less flexibility to dismiss, and the risk of a tribunal claim.

**Benefits:** better motivated staff, lower turnover, a wider pool of applicants, and reputational protection. This connects directly to staff as an asset in WBS11 **1.3.4.1**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Environmental protection">

Limits on emissions, waste disposal, packaging and resource use.

**Costs:** cleaner equipment, waste handling, permits, monitoring and reporting.

**Benefits:** it can force efficiencies that save money, because using less material is cheaper, and it protects businesses that depend on the environment. For a tourism business the environment *is* the product.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Competition policy">

Prevents price fixing, cartels, abuse of a dominant position, predatory pricing, and mergers that would reduce competition too far.

**Costs:** restricts pricing freedom, can block an acquisition, and carries heavy fines.

**Benefits:** a small business is protected from being destroyed by a dominant rival, so this is legislation that helps the smaller firm far more than it hinders it.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Health and safety">

Risk assessments, safe equipment, training, protective equipment, accident reporting.

**Costs:** equipment, training time, documentation, and inspection.

**Benefits:** fewer accidents means less lost time, lower insurance and no compensation claims. Staff who feel safe are more productive, which is a Maslow safety need met.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Intellectual property">

- **Copyright** — original written, musical, artistic and software work.
- **Patent** — a new invention or process, exclusive for a fixed period.
- **Trademark** — a name, logo or slogan.

This is the area where legislation is almost entirely a **benefit** to the business. It is what makes a USP defensible: without a patent, a competitor copies the invention immediately, and without a trademark, anyone can trade under your name. Innovation is only worth funding if the result can be protected.

The cost is registration fees and, more significantly, the cost of enforcing the right, which can mean litigation a small business cannot afford.

</Chunk>

## The balanced view

Students almost always argue that legislation is a cost. The stronger answer recognises both sides:

- It **raises costs** and creates compliance work, and the burden is proportionately heaviest on small firms with no legal or HR department.
- It also **protects the business**: from unfair competition, from having its ideas copied, and from the far larger cost of an accident, a tribunal claim or a consumer scandal.
- **Compliance is usually cheaper than breach.** Fines, legal costs, compensation and reputational damage generally exceed the cost of doing it properly.
- Some businesses go **beyond** the legal minimum deliberately, as an ethical stance or a marketing position, which links to WBS11 **1.3.5.2**.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Environmental regulation matters unusually directly here. Rules on waste disposal, sewage, reef protection and construction impose real costs on a guesthouse or resort.

They also protect the asset the entire industry sells. A damaged reef or a polluted lagoon destroys the product permanently, and no marketing recovers it. So environmental legislation in a tourism-dependent economy is closer to protecting the business's own long-term revenue than to being a burden on it, which makes it a good example for the balanced argument.

Employment law is the other visible one, because staff frequently live on site, so accommodation standards, hours and rest days are regulated in ways that would not arise in a city business.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on the effect of one area of legislation; Assess (10) on the impact of legislation on a business.

**Answering an impact question:**

1. **Name the specific area**, not "legislation" generally.
2. **Cost side:** what does compliance actually require, and roughly what does it cost? Use figures if given.
3. **Benefit side:** what does it protect the business from, or make possible?
4. **Then compare with the cost of breach:** fines, claims, reputation.
5. Judge, usually that compliance is worth it, and note who it burdens most.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Legislation is not only a cost.** Intellectual property and competition policy are largely protective.
- **The burden is proportionately heavier on small firms.**
- **Compliance is cheaper than breach.**
- **It can force useful efficiencies**, particularly in waste and energy.
- **Where the environment is the product**, protecting it is protecting revenue.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Arguing only that legislation is a burden.** One-sided answers cap the mark.
- **Confusing copyright, patents and trademarks.** Copyright: creative work. Patent: invention. Trademark: name or logo.
- **Vagueness.** "There are laws they must follow" earns nothing. Name the area and the requirement.
- **Forgetting enforcement costs money** as well as registration.
- **Missing that competition policy protects small businesses** rather than restricting them.
- **Ignoring the reputational cost of breach**, which often exceeds the fine.
- **Not applying to the business in the source.** A restaurant, a factory and a software firm face different laws.

</Mistakes>
