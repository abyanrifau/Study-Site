## Control against assurance

The distinction examiners test most.

**Quality control** — inspect at the end. A specialist checker rejects or reworks anything below standard.

- Simple, needs no change to how anyone else works, and it catches defects before the customer sees them.
- The defect has already been made, so the materials and labour in it are wasted. It also makes quality somebody else's problem, so the people producing the work have no ownership of it.

**Quality assurance** — build it in. Each stage checks its own work against an agreed standard before passing it on.

- Prevents defects rather than finding them, so waste falls. Staff own the quality of their own work, which is motivating.
- It needs training, agreed standards and a genuine culture change, and it takes time before the benefits appear.

**The one-line version:** control finds defects, assurance prevents them. Prevention is cheaper.

## Quality circles

Small groups of employees who meet regularly to identify quality problems in their own area and propose solutions.

They work because the people doing the job know where the problems are, and being asked is motivating in Mayo's and Herzberg's terms. They fail when management asks for suggestions and then ignores them, which is worse than never asking.

## Total Quality Management

TQM makes quality the responsibility of **every** employee, not a department.

Its central idea is the **internal customer**: the next stage in the process is your customer, and you must meet their requirements. If everyone does that, the external customer receives something correct by construction.

**For:** defects fall sharply, waste and rework fall, staff are engaged, and quality becomes a genuine point of difference.

**Against:** it is a cultural change, not a procedure, so it takes years. It needs extensive training and visible commitment from management. Introduced half-heartedly it becomes a slogan that staff rightly ignore, and some businesses have spent heavily on it and got very little.

## Kaizen

Continuous improvement: many small changes, suggested largely by the people doing the work, rather than one transformational project.

**For:** cheap, incremental, low-risk, and the gains compound. It engages staff because their ideas are used.

**Against:** slow, and it will not deliver a step change when one is needed. If the whole process is wrong, improving it by 2% a year does not fix it.

## Competitive advantage from quality

Chain it properly:

> Better quality → fewer defects, returns and complaints → lower waste and warranty costs → *and* higher customer satisfaction → more repeat purchase and better reviews → either a price premium or higher volume → higher profit.

Note it runs through **both** cost and revenue. Saying both is stronger than saying one.

**The cost side of the argument:** quality is not free. Training, better materials, inspection and systems all cost money. The justification is that **the cost of getting it wrong is higher**: wasted materials, rework, refunds, lost customers, reputational damage. In a business where reviews drive demand, one visible quality failure can cost more than a year of prevention.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

For a guesthouse, quality *is* the product, and it is measured publicly. The review score on booking platforms directly determines ranking, which determines bookings, which determines revenue. There is no gap between quality and demand.

That makes quality assurance the only sensible approach. Quality control, inspecting at the end, is impossible: you cannot check a guest's stay after they have had it and replace the defective one. The standard has to be built into how every room is prepared and every guest greeted.

It also makes the cost argument easy to win. A run of poor reviews drops the ranking, and the lost bookings dwarf the cost of the training that would have prevented them.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on any of the terms, especially control against assurance; Explain (4) on one benefit; Assess (10) on whether a business should introduce TQM or invest in quality.

**Answering "should they invest in quality?"**

1. **Establish the cost of poor quality here.** Use the source: returns, complaints, review scores, rework.
2. **Name the method that fits**, and say why. Assurance for a service business; control where inspection is cheap and defects are rare.
3. **Chain the benefit** through both cost and revenue.
4. **Weigh the cost and the time.** TQM takes years and needs cultural commitment.
5. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Quality costs money, and poor quality costs more.** Compare the two.
- **In a service business you cannot inspect at the end**, so assurance is the only option.
- **TQM is a culture change, not a procedure**, and half-hearted introduction achieves nothing.
- **Kaizen is slow.** It cannot deliver a step change.
- **Quality standards must match the market.** A budget product does not need a premium specification, and over-engineering is itself waste.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing quality control with quality assurance.** Control inspects at the end; assurance prevents throughout.
- **Saying quality improvement is free.** It has real costs, which is why the comparison matters.
- **Treating TQM as a system to install.** It is a cultural change over years.
- **Describing quality circles without the motivation link** to Mayo and Herzberg.
- **Confusing Kaizen with a single big improvement project.** It is many small ones.
- **Assuming higher quality is always worth it.** Over-engineering a budget product wastes money.
- **Only arguing the cost side.** Quality affects revenue through reputation and repeat purchase too.

</Mistakes>
