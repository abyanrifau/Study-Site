## Number of competitors

More competitors means:

- **Lower prices**, as firms undercut each other.
- **Thinner margins** and pressure to cut costs.
- **More choice and better quality**, as firms differentiate to avoid competing on price alone.
- **More innovation**, because standing still loses share.
- **More price elastic demand** for any one firm, because customers have alternatives. That connects directly to WBS11 **1.3.2.4**.

Fewer competitors means the reverse: pricing power, fatter margins, and less pressure to improve.

**What determines the number:** barriers to entry. Where capital requirements are low and no licence is needed, competitors arrive quickly, which is why online markets are so crowded.

## Size of competitors

A large competitor brings advantages a small firm cannot match:

- **Economies of scale**, so a lower unit cost and the ability to undercut.
- **Brand recognition** and a marketing budget.
- **Buying power** over suppliers.
- **Financial reserves**, so it can sustain losses through a price war that would bankrupt a smaller rival.
- **Distribution reach**, including shelf space a small firm cannot obtain.

A small business should therefore **not** compete head-on on price with a much larger rival. It loses, because the larger firm can absorb losses for longer.

## Behaviour of competitors

The dimension students forget, and often the most important.

- **Aggressive** — price cutting, heavy advertising, matching every move, poaching staff and customers.
- **Passive** — content with its position, slow to respond.
- **Innovative** — competing on new products rather than price.
- **Collusive** — informally keeping prices high, which is usually illegal under competition policy (**2.3.5.2**).

**A small aggressive competitor can do more damage than a large passive one**, which is why size alone does not tell you how hard a market is. Saying that is a strong exam point.

## How a small business competes

The specification asks for this directly, so have the list ready. Each of these is something a large firm is structurally bad at.

- **Serve a niche** too small for a large firm to bother with.
- **Differentiate** on quality, design, or expertise.
- **Give better service.** Personal, flexible, and responsive. A large firm's processes prevent this.
- **Be faster.** No committees, so decisions and adaptations happen in days.
- **Build relationships.** Customers who know the owner by name do not switch for a small saving.
- **Compete locally**, on knowledge of a specific place or community.
- **Use online channels** to reach customers directly without needing shelf space.

**What it should not do:** compete on price against a firm with lower costs and deeper reserves.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

The guesthouse market shows all three dimensions clearly. **Number:** low barriers to entry mean many small properties on the same island, so demand for any one of them is highly price elastic and rates are visibly competitive on booking platforms.

**Size:** the resorts are far larger, and they do not compete directly, because they serve a different segment at a different price. The relevant competitors are the guesthouses next door, not the resort across the water.

**Behaviour:** on a small island, one operator discounting aggressively pulls everyone's rates down, because the platform shows all of them side by side. That is why the sensible strategy is differentiation, dive packages, food, hosting, local knowledge, rather than matching the cheapest rate, which is a race nobody wins.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) and Analyse (6) on the effect of increased competition, and Assess (10) on how a small business should compete.

**Answering "how should this small business compete?"**

1. **Describe the competitive environment from the source.** Number, size, behaviour, using any figures given.
2. **Rule out price competition explicitly**, with the reason: the larger rival has lower costs and deeper reserves.
3. **Recommend two routes** the large firm cannot easily copy, and chain each to why it works.
4. **Weigh them:** cost, how long the advantage lasts, whether this business can actually deliver it.
5. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Behaviour matters as much as size.** A small aggressive rival can be the bigger threat.
- **A price war is usually won by whoever has the deepest reserves.**
- **Differentiation can be copied**, so say how long the advantage might last.
- **Low barriers to entry mean today's competitive position is temporary.**
- **Some rivals are not really competitors.** Check they serve the same segment before treating them as a threat.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Only discussing the number of competitors.** The specification names three dimensions.
- **Recommending that a small business undercut a large one.** It cannot win that.
- **Treating every larger firm as a direct competitor** when it serves a different segment.
- **Saying "they should differentiate" with no detail.** Say how, and why it is hard to copy.
- **Forgetting the elasticity link.** More competitors makes demand more price elastic.
- **Ignoring barriers to entry**, which determine whether more competitors are coming.
- **Not applying to the source.** The number and type of rivals given should shape the whole answer.

</Mistakes>
