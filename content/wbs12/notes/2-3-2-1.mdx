## The four calculations

<Formula name="Everything in this topic" note="Read the question carefully: 'variable costs' may mean per unit or in total. Multiply by output to get the total.">
Sales revenue = Sales volume × Selling price

Total variable costs = Variable cost per unit × Output

Total costs = Fixed costs + Total variable costs

Average cost = Total costs ÷ Output
</Formula>

## Fixed and variable

**Fixed costs** stay the same whatever you produce, in the short run: rent, business rates, insurance, salaried staff, loan interest, depreciation. Produce nothing and you still pay them.

**Variable costs** move directly with output: raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages, delivery per order, payment processing fees.

**Semi-variable costs** have both parts: a phone contract with a standing charge plus usage, or staff on a basic wage plus overtime. Exam questions usually split them for you; if not, say you have treated the standing element as fixed.

Two cautions:

- "Fixed" means fixed **with respect to output**, not fixed forever. Rent can rise; it just does not rise because you made more units.
- Fixed costs are only fixed **in the short run**. Double output permanently and you need a bigger factory.

## Why average cost falls with output

This is the most examined idea in the topic.

Fixed costs are spread over more units, so the fixed cost *per unit* falls while the variable cost per unit stays the same. So average cost falls.

<Worked>

A bakery has **fixed costs of $3,000 a month** and **variable costs of $0.80 a loaf**. It sells loaves at **$2.50**.

**At 2,000 loaves a month:**

`Revenue        = 2,000 × $2.50 = $5,000`

`Variable costs = 2,000 × $0.80 = $1,600`

`Total costs    = $3,000 + $1,600 = $4,600`

`Profit         = $5,000 − $4,600 = $400`

`Average cost   = $4,600 ÷ 2,000 = $2.30`

**At 4,000 loaves a month:**

`Revenue        = 4,000 × $2.50 = $10,000`

`Variable costs = 4,000 × $0.80 = $3,200`

`Total costs    = $3,000 + $3,200 = $6,200`

`Profit         = $10,000 − $6,200 = $3,800`

`Average cost   = $6,200 ÷ 4,000 = $1.55`

**Reading the answer:** doubling output multiplied profit by more than nine, because the $3,000 of fixed costs was already being paid. Average cost fell from $2.30 to $1.55, entirely because fixed cost per loaf fell from $1.50 to $0.75. The variable cost per loaf never changed.

That is the mechanism behind economies of scale, and it is why capacity utilisation (**2.3.4.2**) matters so much.

</Worked>

## Ways to improve sales volume and revenue

The specification asks for this, so have a short list ready and remember revenue is volume × price, so you can act on either.

**Raise volume:** advertise and promote, widen distribution, cut price if demand is elastic, improve the product, enter a new segment or market, add sales channels.

**Raise revenue:** raise price if demand is inelastic, sell a better mix towards higher-margin products, bundle, add optional extras, reduce discounting.

The link back to WBS11 **1.3.2.4** is essential: whether raising price raises revenue depends entirely on price elasticity of demand. Never recommend a price rise without saying which way elasticity points.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse has almost entirely fixed costs. The building, the loan, the salaried staff and the electricity to keep the place open barely change whether two rooms or ten are occupied. The variable cost of one more guest is breakfast, laundry and water, perhaps $12 a night on a $95 rate.

That cost structure explains the whole pricing behaviour of the sector. With variable costs that low, an empty room earns nothing and costs almost nothing to fill, so a heavily discounted late booking still adds nearly $80 of contribution. It is why last-minute rates fall so far in the low season and why occupancy matters more than rate.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Almost always as a Calculate (4), often as the first step of a longer question that then asks about break-even or profit.

**Method that never loses marks:**

1. Write the formula down.
2. Substitute the numbers, with the units.
3. Show the intermediate steps, especially total variable costs.
4. State the answer with its unit: dollars, or units of output.

**Watch for:** a question giving variable costs *in total* when you assumed per unit, or the reverse. Read it twice.

**Getting evaluation marks** when asked whether to increase output: average cost falls, and only if the extra output can actually be sold. Producing more and storing it raises costs without raising revenue. Capacity limits matter too.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing sales volume with sales revenue.** Volume is units, revenue is money.
- **Forgetting to multiply variable cost per unit by output.**
- **Treating fixed costs as fixed per unit.** Total fixed costs stay the same; fixed cost per unit falls as output rises.
- **Calling something fixed because it does not change month to month.** The test is whether it changes with output.
- **Dividing by the wrong figure for average cost.** It is total costs divided by output, not by revenue.
- **Recommending a price rise without considering elasticity.**
- **Omitting units** from the final answer.

</Mistakes>
