## Where the difference comes from

Liability follows from **legal identity**, and that is the sentence to lead with.

A **sole trader or partnership has no separate legal identity**. The business and the owner are the same person in law, so the business's debts are the owner's debts. If the business owes $80,000 and has $20,000, the remaining $60,000 is claimed from the owner: savings, car, and potentially the house.

A **company has its own legal identity**. It owns its assets, signs its own contracts and owes its own debts. If it fails owing $80,000, the shareholders lose the value of their shares and nothing more. The unpaid creditors bear the loss.

## What each one means in practice

<Chunk title="Unlimited liability" open>

**The cost:** personal assets are exposed. A single bad year, an unpaid supplier or a legal claim can reach the owner's home. This is the single strongest argument for incorporating.

**The benefits, which students often miss:**

- **Lenders prefer it.** There is more to claim against, so borrowing can be easier and cheaper for a small business than the same borrowing by a company with no assets.
- **Suppliers trust it.** Trade credit is easier to obtain when the owner is personally on the hook.
- **It concentrates the mind.** An owner risking their own house takes fewer reckless decisions.
- **Privacy.** No accounts to file publicly.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Limited liability">

**The benefit:** risk is capped at the amount invested, which is what makes outside investment possible at all. Nobody would buy shares in a company if they could be pursued for its debts.

**The costs and limits:**

- **Registration, filing and public accounts.** More cost, less privacy.
- **Lenders respond by asking for security.** A bank lending to a small company will very often require a **personal guarantee** from the director, which reinstates unlimited liability for that debt. So incorporation does not automatically protect the owner in practice, and saying this is a strong evaluation point.
- **Directors still have legal duties**, and can be personally liable for wrongful trading or failing to act properly.
- **Suppliers may demand payment upfront** from a new company with no assets, which worsens cash flow.

</Chunk>

## Finance and liability

The specification links liability to the finance appropriate for each form, so make the connection.

**Unlimited liability businesses** (sole trader, most partnerships) rely on owner's capital, retained profit, family and friends, bank loans secured on personal assets, trade credit and leasing. They **cannot sell shares**, which caps how much external equity they can raise.

**Limited liability businesses** can do all of that *and* issue share capital, take venture capital, and eventually float. That is the main financial reason to incorporate: it opens equity finance.

So the chain is: **incorporation → limited liability → outside investors are willing to invest → equity finance becomes available → larger sums can be raised → growth.** That chain is worth learning, because it answers most questions on this topic.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A single guesthouse run by its owner is normally a sole trader. Liability is unlimited, and with no borrowing and a modest scale, the exposure is manageable.

The calculation changes with a loan. Borrowing $150,000 to add rooms means that if occupancy disappoints, the bank can pursue the owner personally, and in a small community the family home may be the main asset. That is when incorporating becomes worth the cost and the filing.

And the evaluation point applies directly: the bank lending that $150,000 to a newly formed company with no track record will almost certainly want a personal guarantee on the property anyway. The owner then has the cost of incorporation without, for that loan, the protection. The protection applies to *other* creditors, which still has value, but it is not the clean shield people assume.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2), very commonly; Explain (4) on one implication of limited or unlimited liability; Assess (10) on whether a business should incorporate.

**The three-step answer:**

1. **State the exposure with numbers.** If the source gives debts or personal assets, use them. "Debts of $80,000 against business assets of $20,000 leaves $60,000 claimable from the owner personally."
2. **Weigh the protection against the cost.** Registration, filing, public accounts, director duties.
3. **Then the evaluation:** would a lender require a personal guarantee anyway, and how much is actually at risk?

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **A personal guarantee undoes the protection** for that particular debt. This is the highest-value point on the topic.
- **The value of limited liability rises with the amount borrowed** and with how much the owner personally owns.
- **Unlimited liability has genuine advantages** in borrowing, trust and privacy. Arguing only one side caps the mark.
- **It depends on the risk of the business.** A high-risk venture justifies incorporation far more than a steady one.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying limited liability means the business has limited debts.** It limits the *owners'* liability, not the debts.
- **Thinking incorporation makes the owner untouchable.** Personal guarantees and director duties both cut through it.
- **Forgetting that unlimited liability can make borrowing easier**, because the lender has more to claim against.
- **Confusing liability with limited company status as a whole.** Liability is one consequence of separate legal identity.
- **Assuming partnerships always have unlimited liability.** Usually, but limited liability partnerships exist.
- **Not quantifying the exposure** when the source gives you the figures.
- **Missing the finance link.** Only companies can raise share capital, and that is the main financial reason to incorporate.

</Mistakes>
