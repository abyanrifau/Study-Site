## The three sources

<Chunk title="Owner's capital" open>

The owner's own savings, put into the business.

**For:** no interest, no repayment schedule, no loss of control, available immediately, and it shows a lender the owner has faith in the idea, which makes borrowing easier.

**Against:** limited to what the owner has, and the owner carries the whole loss if it fails. The opportunity cost is whatever those savings were doing before, and for most people that is their financial safety net.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Retained profit">

Profit kept in the business rather than taken out.

**For:** free in the sense of no interest, no dilution of ownership, entirely at the business's discretion, and it signals financial strength.

**Against:** only available if the business is profitable, so a start-up has none. The opportunity cost is real: profit retained is profit not paid to the owner or shareholders, and in a company that means smaller dividends, which shareholders may resent.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Sale of assets">

Selling equipment, vehicles, property, or surplus stock.

**For:** turns an idle asset into cash, and improves liquidity without taking on debt.

**Against:** you can only do it once with each asset, the asset may be needed, a forced sale gets a poor price, and selling productive equipment reduces capacity. **Sale and leaseback** is the usual compromise: sell the building, rent it back, keep using it, and accept an ongoing rent instead of ownership.

</Chunk>

## Internal against external

The case for internal finance in one line: **it costs nothing in interest and nothing in control.**

The case against: **there is not much of it.** A business needing $200,000 cannot usually find it internally, and waiting until retained profit accumulates means missing the opportunity.

Most businesses use both, and the judgement in an exam is usually about which is appropriate for the *amount* and the *purpose*. Small, short-term needs suit internal; large, long-term investment usually needs external.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse owner wanting to add four rooms has three routes. Retained profit is the cheapest but might take three years to accumulate, by which time a competitor may have taken the market. Owner's capital is faster and puts their personal savings at risk. A bank loan is fastest and largest, and now the debt has to be serviced through the wet season when there is little revenue.

That last point is the key application: the *timing* of repayments against seasonal cash flow often matters more than the interest rate. A business with highly seasonal income is safer with internal finance even when a loan looks cheaper on paper.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one advantage of a source; Assess (10) on which source of finance a business should use, usually comparing internal and external.

**Answering a source-of-finance question:**

1. **How much is needed?** Internal finance may simply not be enough. Say so with the figures.
2. **What is it for?** Long-term assets suit long-term finance; a short-term cash gap suits an overdraft.
3. **What does the business already have?** A start-up has no retained profit.
4. **What is the cost and the risk?** Interest and repayments against dilution of control.
5. Judge, and name the opportunity cost of your choice.

**Getting evaluation marks:** opportunity cost is the most reliable point here. Retained profit spent on expansion is retained profit not available as a buffer, and owner's capital used is the owner's safety net gone.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying retained profit is "free".** It has no interest, and it has a real opportunity cost.
- **Suggesting retained profit for a start-up.** A new business has none.
- **Treating sale of assets as repeatable.** Each asset can be sold once.
- **Forgetting that selling productive assets reduces capacity.**
- **Ignoring how much is needed.** Internal finance is often simply too small.
- **Confusing owner's capital with a loan from the owner.** Capital is invested; a loan has to be repaid.
- **Not mentioning that internal finance avoids losing control**, which is often the owner's main concern.

</Mistakes>
