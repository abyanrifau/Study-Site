## Sources: where the money comes from

- **Family and friends** — flexible, cheap or free, and quick. The risk is personal: if it goes wrong you lose the relationship as well as the money. Amounts are usually small.
- **Banks** — the main source. Large sums, structured terms, and they want collateral, a track record and a business plan. A start-up often has none of those.
- **Peer-to-peer funding** — individuals lending through an online platform. Often faster and more willing than a bank, usually at a higher interest rate.
- **Business angels** — a wealthy individual investing their own money. They bring experience, contacts and credibility as well as cash, and they take equity and expect involvement.
- **Crowd funding** — many small contributions online. Doubles as marketing and market testing, and it needs a compelling story and a public campaign that may fail visibly.
- **Other businesses** — a supplier, customer or larger firm investing, often to secure a supply chain or a distribution route. Comes with strategic strings.

## Methods: the form it takes

<Chunk title="Debt methods" open>

**Loan** — fixed sum, fixed period, interest. Predictable repayments make planning easy, and control is retained. The business must pay whether or not it is doing well, and security is usually required.

**Overdraft** — short-term flexibility up to a limit. You pay interest only on what you use, which makes it ideal for bridging a cash flow gap. Interest rates are high and it is repayable on demand, so it is dangerous as long-term finance.

**Trade credit** — pay the supplier 30 to 90 days after delivery. Effectively an interest-free short-term loan, which is why working capital management leans on it. Abusing it damages supplier relationships and forfeits early-payment discounts.

**Leasing** — rent the asset instead of buying. No large upfront payment, maintenance is often included, and it is easier to upgrade. Over the asset's life it costs more than buying, and you never own it.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Equity and other methods">

**Share capital** — sell part of the company. No interest, no repayment, and the money is permanent. The cost is ownership and control: new shareholders own part of the business, expect dividends and can outvote the founder. Only companies can do this.

**Venture capital** — a specialist investor takes a substantial equity stake in a high-growth business. Large sums plus expertise, and they will want board influence and a clear exit within a few years, which shapes strategy whether the founder likes it or not.

**Grant** — usually government, and does not have to be repaid. Effectively free money. Competitive, restricted to specific purposes or regions, with conditions and paperwork attached.

</Chunk>

## Debt or equity: the core trade-off

| | Debt (loans, overdrafts) | Equity (shares, venture capital) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repayment | yes, with interest | no |
| Control | retained | diluted |
| Cost if the business does badly | still payable, so the risk is yours | investors share the loss |
| Cost if the business does well | fixed interest only, so you keep the upside | investors take a share of the upside forever |
| Suits | predictable cash flows, retained ownership | high risk, high growth, no cash for repayments yet |

## Matching finance to the need

This is the single most useful judgement in the topic:

- **Long-term asset** (premises, machinery) → long-term finance: a loan, a lease, or share capital. Never an overdraft.
- **Short-term cash gap** (paying wages before a customer pays) → short-term finance: overdraft or trade credit.
- **High-risk growth with no cash yet** → equity, because debt repayments would sink it.

Using an overdraft to buy a building is the classic error: expensive, repayable on demand, and mismatched to an asset that pays back over twenty years.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse expanding by four rooms is buying a long-term asset, so it needs long-term finance: a bank loan secured on the property, or the owner's capital.

The seasonality changes the analysis. Loan repayments fall due every month including the wet season, when revenue may barely cover wages. So the business either needs a repayment holiday or seasonal schedule, or an overdraft facility alongside the loan to bridge the quiet months.

Trade credit also matters more than it looks in an import-dependent economy. Suppliers offering 60 days on imported food and materials are financing the guesthouse's working capital for free, and losing that facility by paying late would hurt more than a change in the loan rate.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Heavily. Define (2); Explain (4) on one method; Assess (10) or Evaluate (20) on which source or method a business should use.

**The structure that scores:**

1. **What is the money for, and for how long?** Match the term of the finance to the life of the asset. Say this explicitly; many candidates never do.
2. **How much is needed?** Rule out sources that cannot supply it.
3. **What can this business actually get?** A start-up with no collateral and no trading history will not get a large bank loan whatever the plan says.
4. **Debt or equity?** Use the table: does the owner value control, and can the cash flow service the repayments?
5. **Judge**, and name what is given up.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Availability, not just suitability.** The best option is worthless if no lender will provide it.
- **Cash flow timing.** Seasonal businesses struggle with fixed monthly repayments.
- **Control.** Equity finance is cheap in cash terms and expensive in ownership.
- **Gearing.** More debt means more risk if trading worsens or interest rates rise.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Mixing up sources and methods.** A bank is a source; a loan is a method. The specification separates them.
- **Recommending share capital for a sole trader.** Only companies can issue shares.
- **Using an overdraft for a long-term asset.** Expensive and repayable on demand.
- **Forgetting that equity costs control**, which is often the owner's main concern.
- **Treating a grant as easy money.** They are competitive and conditional.
- **Ignoring whether the business could actually obtain the finance.**
- **Saying leasing is cheaper than buying.** It avoids the upfront cost and usually costs more over the asset's life.
- **Not linking to cash flow.** Repayments have to be affordable in the worst month, not the average one.

</Mistakes>
