## The three main forms

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owners | one | two or more | one or more shareholders |
| Liability | unlimited | usually unlimited | limited |
| Separate legal identity | no | no | yes |
| Set-up | simplest, cheapest | simple, needs an agreement | registration and legal formalities |
| Raising finance | hardest | easier than sole trader | easiest of the three |
| Control | total | shared | depends on shareholdings |
| Privacy of accounts | private | private | accounts must be filed publicly |
| Continuity | ends with the owner | disrupted if a partner leaves | continues regardless of owners |

<Chunk title="Sole trader" open>

**For:** fast and cheap to start, complete control, all the profit, privacy, and decisions can be made immediately.

**Against:** unlimited liability, so personal assets are at risk. Finance is hard to raise. No one to share the workload or cover illness. Limited skills, because one person cannot be good at everything. The business ends when the owner does.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Partnership">

**For:** more capital than one person can provide, shared skills and workload, shared decisions, and cover for absence.

**Against:** usually unlimited liability, and crucially **each partner is liable for the others' business decisions**. Profits are shared. Disagreements are common and can paralyse the business. A partnership agreement setting out shares, responsibilities and what happens if someone leaves is essential; without one, statutory default rules apply.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Private limited company">

**For:** limited liability protects personal assets. Separate legal identity means the business can own property, sue and be sued in its own name, and it continues when owners change. Easier to raise finance, including by issuing shares to chosen investors. Often perceived as more credible by suppliers and customers.

**Against:** more expensive and complex to set up and run, accounts must be filed publicly so rivals can see them, more legal duties for directors, and shareholders can outvote the founder. Profit distributed as dividends is taxed differently, which can help or hurt depending on the jurisdiction.

</Chunk>

## Other forms in the specification

- **Franchising.** The franchisee pays a fee and ongoing royalties to trade under an established brand with a proven system, training and marketing support. Lower risk than starting from scratch, and far less freedom, ongoing cost, and a reputation you do not control. From the **franchisor's** side it is a way to expand fast using other people's capital.
- **Social enterprise.** Trades to achieve a social objective, reinvesting most profit into the purpose. Can attract grants, volunteers and loyal customers, and the social mission constrains commercial decisions.
- **Lifestyle business.** Run to fit the owner's chosen life rather than to grow. Deliberately limited, and completely rational: see satisficing in WBS11 **1.3.5.2**.
- **Online business.** Low start-up cost, no premises needed, national or global reach from day one. Also very low barriers to entry, so intense competition, and it depends entirely on being found.

## Growth to a plc

**Flotation** means offering shares to the public, usually on a stock exchange.

**For:** access to very large sums of capital, a higher profile, shares become easier to trade so early investors can exit, and the business can use shares to acquire other companies.

**Against:** enormous cost and regulatory burden, ownership is dispersed so control is lost, accounts and strategy become public, there is constant pressure to deliver short-term results for shareholders, and the company becomes vulnerable to a hostile takeover.

The standard evaluation point: flotation solves a finance problem and creates a control problem. A founder who floats can be voted out of their own company.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Most guesthouses start as sole traders: one owner, minimal formality, complete control. It is the right form for a single small property.

Incorporating as a private limited company makes sense at the point where the owner is borrowing significant sums against the business or operating more than one property. Limited liability then genuinely matters, because a failed second property would otherwise put the family home at risk.

Franchising also appears in the sector as international hotel brands operating properties they do not own. The owner gets the brand, the booking system and the standards; the brand gets expansion without capital. Both sides are buying something they lack, which is the essence of franchising.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one advantage of a form; Assess (10) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business should change its legal form, usually sole trader to Ltd, or Ltd to plc.

**Answering "should they become a limited company?"**

1. **Liability.** How much is at risk, and does the owner have personal assets to protect? Use the figures.
2. **Finance.** Will incorporation actually make borrowing or investment easier for this business?
3. **Cost and complexity.** Set-up, accounting, filing and director duties all cost money and time.
4. **Control and privacy.** Public accounts and possible outside shareholders.
5. Judge, and say what it depends on most.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Limited liability matters more as borrowing rises.** For a business with no debt and no assets, it protects little.
- **Incorporation does not guarantee finance.** A lender may still require a personal guarantee, which reinstates the liability.
- **It depends on the owner's objectives.** A lifestyle business may have no reason to incorporate.
- **Flotation trades finance for control.** Name that trade-off explicitly.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying partnerships always have unlimited liability.** Usually, and limited liability partnerships exist in many jurisdictions.
- **Thinking incorporation removes all personal risk.** Lenders often require personal guarantees, and directors have legal duties.
- **Confusing Ltd and plc.** A plc can sell shares to the public; an Ltd cannot.
- **Forgetting a partnership agreement.** Without one, disputes fall back on default legal rules.
- **Treating franchising as easy money.** There are fees, royalties and strict rules.
- **Recommending flotation for a small business.** The cost and regulation are far beyond most firms.
- **Ignoring the loss of privacy.** Filed accounts are visible to competitors.
- **Missing that each partner is liable for the others' decisions**, which is the sharpest risk in a partnership.

</Mistakes>
