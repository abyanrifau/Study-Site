## The statement of comprehensive income

Read it as a waterfall. Start with revenue and take costs off in three stages.

| | $000 |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | 840 |
| Cost of sales | (504) |
| **Gross profit** | **336** |
| Operating expenses | (210) |
| **Operating profit** | **126** |
| Interest | (26) |
| Tax | (20) |
| **Profit for the year** | **80** |

**Why three levels, not one?** Because each answers a different question.

- **Gross profit** — does the product itself make money? It compares the selling price with what it directly cost to make or buy.
- **Operating profit** — does the *business* make money once you include running it? Rent, salaries, marketing, utilities.
- **Profit for the year** — what is actually left for the owners, after the cost of borrowing and the taxman.

A business can have a healthy gross profit and a loss for the year, and the three levels tell you exactly where the money went.

## Margins

<Formula name="The three margins" note="All three divide by revenue. Margins are percentages, so they allow comparison between a small business and a large one, or between this year and last.">
Gross profit margin = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Operating profit margin = (Operating profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Profit for the year margin = (Profit for the year ÷ Revenue) × 100
</Formula>

<Worked>

Using the figures above:

`Gross profit margin = (336 ÷ 840) × 100 = 40%`

`Operating profit margin = (126 ÷ 840) × 100 = 15%`

`Profit for the year margin = (80 ÷ 840) × 100 = 9.5%`

**Now interpret, because that is where the marks are.** The gap between the 40% gross margin and the 15% operating margin is 25 percentage points, which is $210,000 of operating expenses. The gap between 15% and 9.5% is interest and tax, of which $26,000 is interest.

**Suppose last year the margins were 40%, 19% and 13%.** The gross margin is unchanged, so the product and its pricing are fine. The whole deterioration is between gross and operating profit, which means **operating expenses have risen**. That immediately tells the business where to look, and saying so is worth far more than recalculating the numbers.

</Worked>

**The diagnostic habit:** compare the three margins over time. Whichever gap widened is where the problem is.

## Ways to increase profit

<Chunk title="Raise revenue" open>

Raise price (only if demand is inelastic), raise volume, improve the sales mix towards higher-margin products, reduce discounting, add higher-value services.

Note the elasticity condition. A price rise with elastic demand lowers revenue, as WBS11 **1.3.2.4** shows, though it can still raise profit because fewer units also means fewer costs.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Reduce cost of sales, which improves gross margin">

Negotiate with suppliers, buy in bulk, change supplier, reduce waste, redesign the product to use less material, improve productivity so labour per unit falls.

The risk is quality. Cheaper inputs can reduce the price customers will pay by more than the cost saved.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Reduce operating expenses, which improves operating margin">

Cut overheads, renegotiate rent, reduce energy use, cut marketing, delayer management, outsource functions.

The risk is capability. Cutting marketing raises this year's profit and lowers next year's revenue, and cutting maintenance raises profit now and breaks something later. That short-run against long-run tension is reliable evaluation material.

</Chunk>

## Profit is not cash

Say this whenever profit is discussed. A business can report a healthy profit and be unable to pay its wages, because revenue includes credit sales not yet collected and excludes cash spent on assets. See **2.3.2.4**.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse typically has a very high gross margin, because the direct cost of a room night, breakfast, laundry and water, is perhaps $15 against a $95 rate, giving a gross margin above 80%.

Almost all of its costs sit below the gross profit line: the salaried staff, the electricity, the loan interest. So the operating margin can be a fraction of the gross margin, and the profit-for-the-year margin smaller again once interest is taken out.

The practical consequence is that **occupancy drives everything**. With a gross margin that high, extra bookings flow almost straight to operating profit, and a fall in occupancy has nothing to cushion it, because the costs below the line do not fall with it.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) for any profit figure or margin, Explain (4) on a way to improve profitability, Assess (10) on a set of accounts or a proposed action.

**Calculating without losing marks:** write the formula, substitute, show the working, and give the answer as a percentage with a % sign. Round consistently, usually to one decimal place.

**Interpreting a set of accounts.** Do this and you will outscore most candidates:

1. Calculate all three margins for both years.
2. Identify which margin moved.
3. Name the cause that follows from it: gross margin fell means cost of sales or pricing; operating margin fell alone means overheads.
4. Recommend action aimed at that specific cause.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Short run against long run.** Cost cuts that raise profit now can reduce it later.
- **Margins against absolute profit.** A high margin on tiny revenue may be worth less than a thin margin on large revenue.
- **Compare with what?** A 15% margin means nothing alone. Compare with last year or with a competitor.
- **Profit is not cash.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing a margin by cost instead of revenue.** Every margin divides by revenue.
- **Confusing the three profits.** Gross deducts only cost of sales; operating also deducts overheads; profit for the year also deducts interest and tax.
- **Putting operating expenses in cost of sales.** Cost of sales is direct costs only.
- **Confusing profit with cash.**
- **Quoting a margin with no comparison.** Compare across years or against a rival.
- **Recommending a price rise without checking elasticity.**
- **Ignoring which margin actually moved** when a question gives two years of data. That is where the answer is.
- **Forgetting the % sign**, or giving the margin as a decimal.

</Mistakes>
