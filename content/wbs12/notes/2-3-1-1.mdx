## What goes in a business plan

The specification asks for the content of a plan. The standard sections:

- **Executive summary** — the whole plan in a page, written last and read first.
- **The business and its objectives** — what it does and what it is aiming at.
- **The business opportunity** — the unmet need being served.
- **The owner's background** — skills, experience, why them.
- **The market** — size, growth, segments, competitors, evidence from research.
- **Marketing plan** — the four Ps and the strategy.
- **Personnel** — who is needed, what they cost.
- **Premises and equipment** — what is needed and how it will be obtained.
- **Costings and finance** — start-up costs, running costs, how much finance is needed and from where.
- **Financial forecasts** — sales forecast, cash flow forecast, break-even, projected profit.
- **SWOT analysis** — an honest assessment.

## Why plans matter

<Chunk title="The four real uses" open>

**To raise finance.** This is the main one. No bank lends and no investor invests without a plan, because the plan is the evidence that the founder has thought it through and can service the debt.

**To test the idea.** Writing down a sales forecast and a cost base forces the optimism out into the open. Many plans kill their own business idea, which is a cheap way to find out.

**To give direction.** Clear objectives mean the owner and any staff know what they are aiming at and can measure whether they got there.

**To coordinate.** Marketing, staffing, premises and finance all have to be consistent with the same sales forecast. The plan is where that consistency is checked.

</Chunk>

## The limitations

This is where the evaluation marks are, and the specification specifically asks for the *relevance and uses* of a plan, so you need both sides.

- **A plan does not make it happen.** Writing a good plan and running a good business are different skills.
- **The forecasts are guesses.** Sales forecasts in particular are usually optimistic, and since costs, staffing and cash flow are all built on the sales figure, one wrong assumption at the top invalidates everything below it.
- **It can be too rigid.** A founder who sticks to the plan when the market has changed is worse off than one who never wrote it.
- **Unforeseen events.** A plan cannot include what nobody could predict. This is the risk and uncertainty distinction again: a plan handles risk and cannot handle uncertainty.
- **It takes time.** Time the founder could have spent selling.

The balanced position: the plan is valuable **as a process and as a document for lenders**, and it must be treated as a living document rather than a commitment.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse plan taken to a bank succeeds or fails on one number: projected occupancy. Everything else follows from it, and it is the number a first-time operator is most likely to get wrong, because they have no history to base it on.

A cautious plan would forecast occupancy separately for the dry and wet seasons rather than using an annual average, because an average hides the months when the loan still has to be serviced on almost no revenue. A plan that shows the owner has understood that seasonality is far more persuasive to a lender than one with a higher headline profit.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on business plan or cash flow forecast; Explain (4) on one use of a plan; Assess (10) on how useful a plan is to this business.

**Answering "how useful is a business plan?"** Split it:

1. **Useful for raising finance** — develop why a lender needs it.
2. **Useful as a process** — it tests the assumptions.
3. **But the forecasts are only estimates**, and the sales forecast is the weak link everything else rests on.
4. **And it must stay flexible.**
5. Judge: valuable, and not a guarantee.

**Getting evaluation marks:** attack the sales forecast specifically. If the source gives a forecast, ask what it is based on and what happens to the cash flow if it is 20% out. That is quantified evaluation.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying a plan guarantees success.** It reduces risk; plenty of well-planned businesses fail.
- **Listing the sections with no purpose.** Say what each is *for*.
- **Forgetting that raising finance is the main practical use.**
- **Treating forecasts as facts.** They are estimates, and the sales forecast is the most fragile one.
- **Ignoring the rigidity problem.** A plan followed blindly in a changed market is a liability.
- **Confusing a cash flow forecast with a profit forecast.** They are different statements answering different questions.

</Mistakes>
