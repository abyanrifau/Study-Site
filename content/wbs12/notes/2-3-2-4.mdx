## Cash is not profit

The most important sentence in WBS12: **a profitable business can fail by running out of cash.**

Why they differ:

- **Credit sales.** A sale counts as revenue when the goods go out, and the cash arrives 60 days later. Profit rises immediately; cash does not.
- **Credit purchases.** You receive stock now and pay in 30 days. The cost hits profit when the goods are sold, and the cash goes later.
- **Buying assets.** A $50,000 machine is a $50,000 cash outflow now, and appears in profit only as depreciation spread over years.
- **Loan repayments.** The capital repayment is a cash outflow and is not a cost in the income statement.
- **Depreciation.** It reduces profit and involves no cash at all.

So the two statements answer different questions: profit asks whether the business model works, cash asks whether it can pay this month's bills.

## Constructing a forecast

| | Jan | Feb | Mar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash from sales | 9,000 | 6,000 | 12,000 |
| **Total inflows** | **9,000** | **6,000** | **12,000** |
| Supplier payments | 3,000 | 3,000 | 4,500 |
| Wages | 4,000 | 4,000 | 4,000 |
| Rent and other | 2,500 | 2,500 | 2,500 |
| **Total outflows** | **9,500** | **9,500** | **11,000** |
| **Net cash flow** | **(500)** | **(3,500)** | **1,000** |
| Opening balance | 4,000 | 3,500 | 0 |
| **Closing balance** | **3,500** | **0** | **1,000** |

<Worked>

Read the table above as an examiner would.

**January:** inflows $9,000 − outflows $9,500 = net cash flow of **−$500**. Opening $4,000 + (−$500) = closing **$3,500**.

**February:** net cash flow **−$3,500**. Opening $3,500 (January's closing) + (−$3,500) = closing **$0**.

**March:** net cash flow **+$1,000**. Opening $0 + $1,000 = closing **$1,000**.

**What it tells the business:** February is the problem. The forecast identifies it in advance, so the owner can act now, by arranging an overdraft, delaying a purchase, chasing customers, or negotiating longer credit with suppliers. Spotting it in February would be too late.

**The crucial point for an exam:** notice the business is not unprofitable. Over the quarter, inflows of $27,000 exceed outflows of $30,000 only because of timing. Fix the timing and the business is fine.

</Worked>

## Uses of a cash flow forecast

- **It identifies shortages in advance**, which is the main point. Finance arranged before a crisis is cheaper and easier than finance arranged during one.
- **Lenders require it.** A bank will not lend without seeing when and why the money is needed and how it will be repaid.
- **It supports decisions.** Can the business afford this equipment this month, or should it wait?
- **It sets a benchmark.** Compare actual cash against forecast and investigate the difference, which is variance analysis in **2.3.2.5**.

## Limitations

- **It is based on estimates**, especially of sales, and one optimistic sales assumption invalidates the rest.
- **Customers pay late.** A forecast assuming 30-day payment collapses if customers take 60.
- **Unexpected costs.** Repairs, a lost customer, a price rise from a supplier.
- **External shocks** cannot be forecast at all.
- **It can create false confidence.** A tidy spreadsheet feels like certainty and is not.

## Improving cash flow

**Speed up inflows:** chase debtors, offer early-payment discounts, take deposits, shorten credit terms, use factoring to get cash for invoices now.

**Slow down outflows:** negotiate longer supplier credit, lease rather than buy, delay non-essential spending, reduce stock so cash is not tied up in inventory.

**Bring in cash:** overdraft, short-term loan, sell unused assets, owner injects capital.

Note the trade-offs: factoring costs a fee, longer supplier credit annoys suppliers, cutting stock risks running out, and an overdraft costs interest. There is always a price.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Seasonality makes this the most important topic in WBS12 for a Maldivian business. A guesthouse earns most of its cash in a handful of dry-season months and pays wages, electricity and loan instalments all twelve.

The forecast has to show the wet-season trough, and the business has to enter it with a cash reserve built in the peak or with an agreed overdraft. Operators who spend the peak-season cash on refurbishment and then meet the wet season with nothing in the bank are the ones that fail, and many of them are profitable across the year.

That is the whole lesson: **annual profit does not pay August's wages.**

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Very heavily. Calculate (4) to complete a forecast, Construct (4) to build one, Assess (10) on the cash flow position or on how to improve it.

**Completing a forecast without losing marks:**

1. Total the inflows, total the outflows.
2. Net cash flow = inflows − outflows. Show negatives in brackets.
3. Closing balance = opening + net cash flow.
4. **Carry the closing balance forward** as the next month's opening. This is where most marks are lost.
5. Label everything.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Distinguish a timing problem from a viability problem.** A business short of cash in one month because a customer pays late is very different from one whose costs exceed its revenue. Say which this is.
- **Every remedy has a cost.** Factoring charges a fee, extended supplier credit damages relationships.
- **The forecast is only as good as the sales estimate.**
- **In a seasonal business, the annual figure hides the problem.** Look at the worst month.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing cash flow with profit.** The most heavily penalised error in this unit.
- **Not carrying the closing balance forward** to the next month's opening balance.
- **Forgetting that buying an asset is a cash outflow now** but only a small cost in the income statement.
- **Including depreciation in a cash flow forecast.** It involves no cash.
- **Treating a negative closing balance as automatically fatal.** It means finance is needed, and an agreed overdraft may cover it.
- **Recommending remedies with no cost attached.**
- **Assuming customers pay on time.**
- **Looking only at the annual total** in a seasonal business.

</Mistakes>
