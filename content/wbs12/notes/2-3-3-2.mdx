## Liquidity is not profitability

Two different questions:

- **Profitability:** over a period, did revenue exceed costs? Does the business model work?
- **Liquidity:** right now, can it pay what it owes this month?

A profitable business with all its money tied up in unsold stock and unpaid customer invoices is illiquid, and illiquidity is what actually closes businesses.

## The statement of financial position

A snapshot at one date of what the business owns and owes.

| | $000 |
| --- | --- |
| Non-current assets | 420 |
| **Current assets** | |
| Inventory | 90 |
| Trade receivables | 70 |
| Cash | 20 |
| Total current assets | **180** |
| **Current liabilities** | |
| Trade payables | 75 |
| Overdraft | 25 |
| Total current liabilities | **100** |

Liquidity is entirely about the relationship between those last two totals.

## The two ratios

<Formula name="Liquidity measures" note="The acid test strips out inventory because stock is the hardest current asset to turn into cash quickly, and in a downturn it may not sell at all.">
Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities

Acid test ratio = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities
</Formula>

<Worked>

Using the figures above:

`Working capital = 180 − 100 = $80,000`

`Current ratio = 180 ÷ 100 = 1.8:1`

`Acid test = (180 − 90) ÷ 100 = 90 ÷ 100 = 0.9:1`

**Interpreting it.** A current ratio of 1.8:1 looks comfortable: short-term assets cover short-term debts nearly twice over.

The acid test of 0.9:1 is the more revealing figure. Strip out the $90,000 of inventory and the business has only $90,000 of quick assets against $100,000 of debts due within a year. So **it depends on selling its stock to pay its bills.**

Whether that matters depends on what the stock is. Fast-moving food stock will convert in days, so 0.9:1 is fine. Slow-moving specialist equipment might sit for months, in which case the business has a real problem hiding behind a healthy-looking current ratio.

**That is the whole skill:** the current ratio flatters a business with a lot of inventory, and the acid test shows what is really there.

</Worked>

## What the numbers mean

| Current ratio | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Below 1:1 | current liabilities exceed current assets, so there is a real risk of being unable to pay |
| 1.5 to 2:1 | usually comfortable |
| Well above 2:1 | possibly too much cash or stock sitting idle, earning nothing |

The rule of thumb matters less than the **direction of travel** and the **type of business**. A supermarket with daily cash sales operates safely on ratios that would be alarming for a manufacturer with 90-day customers.

## Improving liquidity

The specification names four routes, so use its own vocabulary.

- **Selling assets.** Sell an unused non-current asset for cash, or use sale and leaseback. One-off, and it may reduce capacity.
- **Supplier credit terms.** Negotiate longer to pay, which keeps cash in the business. Free if agreed, and it damages relationships if simply taken.
- **Factoring.** Sell receivables for immediate cash. Fast and reliable, and you receive less than the invoice value, so it costs margin.
- **Inventory and JIT.** Hold less stock so less cash is tied up. Directly improves the acid test, and it risks stockouts and loses bulk-buying discounts.

Others worth naming: chase debtors, take deposits, arrange an overdraft, inject owner's capital, delay non-essential spending.

**Every one has a cost.** Naming the cost is how you turn a list into an evaluation.

## Too much liquidity

A genuinely overlooked point. Cash sitting in the bank earns almost nothing, and excess stock costs storage and risks obsolescence. A business with a current ratio of 4:1 is very safe and is not working its assets, which shows up as a poor return on capital.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse has almost no inventory and no trade receivables, because guests pay at or before checkout. So its current assets are essentially cash, and its current ratio and acid test are nearly identical. That is unusually clean, and it means the ratios actually tell you something.

The seasonal risk is the real issue. Entering the wet season with a current ratio of 1.2:1 is dangerous in a way the same ratio would not be for a business with steady monthly income, because there are four months of wages and loan instalments ahead with little revenue coming in. Liquidity has to be judged against the *cash flow profile*, not against a textbook rule of thumb.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) for either ratio or for working capital, Explain (4) on one way to improve liquidity, Assess (10) on a business's liquidity position.

**Answering "assess its liquidity":**

1. Calculate **both** ratios. Using only the current ratio misses the point of the topic.
2. Compare them. A large gap means the business is inventory-heavy.
3. Compare with last year if given, and say which direction it is moving.
4. Relate to the type of business: how fast does its stock sell, and how quickly do customers pay?
5. Recommend, and name the cost of your recommendation.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The acid test is the sharper measure**, and saying why shows understanding.
- **Rules of thumb depend on the sector.** A supermarket and a shipbuilder are not comparable.
- **Trend matters more than level.** Falling from 2.0 to 1.2 is worse news than a steady 1.2.
- **Too much liquidity is also a problem.** Very few candidates say this.
- **Every remedy costs something:** factoring fees, supplier goodwill, stockout risk.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing liquidity with profitability.** Different questions entirely.
- **Forgetting to remove inventory** in the acid test.
- **Dividing liabilities by assets.** Assets go on top.
- **Quoting the answer without the ratio format.** Write 1.8:1, not just 1.8.
- **Applying the 1.5 to 2:1 rule of thumb blindly** without considering the sector.
- **Treating a high ratio as automatically good.** Idle cash and excess stock earn nothing.
- **Listing remedies with no cost attached.**
- **Including non-current assets** in the current ratio. Only current assets count.

</Mistakes>
