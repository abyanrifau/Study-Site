## What budgets are for

The specification asks for the purposes, so have them ready:

- **Control.** Spending is authorised in advance and monitored against plan.
- **Planning.** It forces managers to think about next year rather than reacting to it.
- **Delegation.** A manager given a budget can be trusted to run their area within it, which links to decentralisation in WBS11 **1.3.4.3**.
- **Motivation.** A budget is a target, and a realistic target motivates.
- **Coordination.** Departmental budgets have to add up to something the business can afford.
- **Monitoring and correction.** Variance analysis shows where the plan is going wrong, in time to act.

## The two types

<Chunk title="Historical budgeting" open>

Start from last year and adjust. "Marketing had $40,000, inflation is 4%, so give them $41,600."

**For:** quick, cheap, easy to produce, and stable, so managers can plan.

**Against:** it embeds last year's inefficiency. Money wasted last year is still budgeted this year. It also encourages managers to spend everything before year end so their budget is not cut, which is a real and well-documented behaviour.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Zero-based budgeting">

Start from zero. Every item of expenditure must be justified from scratch before it is allowed.

**For:** eliminates waste, forces managers to think about value, and allocates resources to what actually matters now rather than what mattered before.

**Against:** very time-consuming and expensive to produce, needs skilled managers who can build a case, and it can be demoralising to justify your whole department every year. It also risks favouring the manager who argues best rather than the department that matters most.

Most businesses use historical budgeting routinely and zero-based occasionally, when costs need a hard look.

</Chunk>

## Variance analysis

<Formula name="Variance" note="The number alone earns little. You must say whether it is favourable or adverse, and the sign convention differs between revenue and costs.">
Variance = Actual − Budget

Revenue: actual ABOVE budget is favourable
Costs:   actual ABOVE budget is adverse
</Formula>

<Worked>

A guesthouse budgeted for a month and compares it with the actual result.

| | Budget | Actual | Variance | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | 24,000 | 21,500 | 2,500 | adverse |
| Wages | 8,000 | 8,400 | 400 | adverse |
| Food and supplies | 4,000 | 3,600 | 400 | favourable |
| Utilities | 2,500 | 2,900 | 400 | adverse |
| **Profit** | **9,500** | **6,600** | **2,900** | **adverse** |

**Working the revenue variance:** $21,500 − $24,000 = −$2,500. Revenue below budget, so **$2,500 adverse**.

**Working the wages variance:** $8,400 − $8,000 = +$400. Costs above budget, so **$400 adverse**. Note the sign is positive but the variance is adverse, which is exactly why the label matters more than the number.

**Working the food variance:** $3,600 − $4,000 = −$400. Costs below budget, so **$400 favourable**.

**Now interpret, which is where the marks are.** Revenue is $2,500 down and food costs are $400 down. Those are almost certainly the same event: fewer guests means less revenue *and* less food used. So the favourable food variance is not good management, it is a symptom of the revenue problem. Reporting it as a success would be wrong.

Utilities up $400 with fewer guests is the genuinely worrying figure, because it should have fallen too. That is the variance to investigate.

</Worked>

**That last paragraph is the skill.** Variances are linked, and a favourable one can be the shadow of an adverse one. Examiners reward candidates who notice.

## Difficulties with budgeting

- **It depends on the forecast.** An optimistic sales budget makes every other budget wrong.
- **It takes time**, particularly zero-based.
- **It can be set by people who do not know the operation**, producing targets nobody believes.
- **Gaming.** Managers pad their requests, spend up to the limit before year end, or negotiate soft targets they can beat.
- **Rigidity.** Sticking to a budget when circumstances have changed can be worse than abandoning it.
- **Short-termism.** Cutting training or maintenance to hit this year's budget damages next year.
- **Demotivation.** An unrealistic budget nobody can hit demotivates rather than motivates.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Monthly budgets are essential in a seasonal business and an annual budget is nearly useless. A guesthouse budgeting $24,000 of revenue as a monthly average would show large adverse variances every wet-season month and large favourable ones in the peak, telling the owner nothing they did not already know.

Budgeting month by month against expected seasonal occupancy makes the variances meaningful: a $2,500 adverse variance in a month that was *expected* to be quiet is a genuine signal, and one in the peak season is an emergency.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) for variances, Explain (4) on a purpose or difficulty, Assess (10) on whether a business should adopt zero-based budgeting or on what a set of variances shows.

**Calculating variances without losing marks:**

1. Variance = actual − budget.
2. **Label every one favourable or adverse.** A bare number usually loses the mark.
3. Watch the cost convention: costs above budget are adverse even though the number is positive.
4. Then **interpret**: which variance matters, and are any of them linked?

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Link the variances.** A favourable cost variance caused by lower sales is not good news.
- **Ask what caused it.** Controllable causes such as overspending need action; uncontrollable ones such as a supplier price rise need a revised budget.
- **Size matters.** A $400 variance on $8,000 is 5%; a $2,500 variance on $24,000 is over 10%. Say which is material.
- **On zero-based budgeting:** the saving has to exceed the cost of the process, and it is usually worth doing occasionally rather than every year.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Giving a variance without saying favourable or adverse.**
- **Calling a cost overspend favourable** because the number is positive.
- **Calculating budget − actual** instead of actual − budget. Be consistent and label clearly.
- **Treating every favourable variance as good.** Lower costs from lower sales is a warning, not a win.
- **Confusing a budget with a cash flow forecast.** A budget plans income and expenditure; a forecast tracks cash movements.
- **Recommending zero-based budgeting without costing the process.**
- **Using an annual budget for a seasonal business.**
- **Forgetting the human side.** Budgets are gamed, and unrealistic ones demotivate.

</Mistakes>
