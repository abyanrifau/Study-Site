## Cultural differences

The dimensions that most often affect a marketing decision:

- **Religion.** Dietary rules, prohibited ingredients, fasting periods, days of rest, modesty in imagery, and the timing of festivals that drive seasonal demand.
- **Family and household structure.** Extended or nuclear households change pack sizes, car sizes, housing and who makes the purchase decision.
- **Attitudes to gender.** How men and women are portrayed, and who is assumed to make which purchase.
- **Attitudes to hierarchy and authority.** This affects business-to-business selling and negotiation as much as consumer advertising.
- **Individualism against collectivism.** Whether an appeal to standing out or to belonging is more persuasive.
- **Attitudes to time and punctuality**, which affect delivery promises and negotiation.
- **Attitudes to debt and credit**, which determine whether finance and instalment offers work.
- **Colour and symbol associations.** White, red and the number four carry very different meanings in different places, and this reaches packaging, logos and store design.

## Tastes and preferences

Not culture in the deep sense, and just as commercially decisive: flavour and sweetness, spice tolerance, portion size, preferred textures, strength of fragrance, styling and colour preferences, and how much sugar or salt is expected. These differences are usually measurable through research, which makes them the easiest category to get right and the most embarrassing to get wrong.

**The related dimension is income-driven rather than cultural:** pack size, price point and whether a product is bought weekly or daily. Do not label as cultural what is actually about income and retail structure.

## Language and unintended meanings

- **Brand names** can mean something unfortunate, or something rude, in another language.
- **Slogans** translated literally may be meaningless, absurd or offensive; the wordplay that made the original memorable rarely survives.
- **Pronounceability** matters: a name customers cannot say does not get recommended.
- **Existing associations.** A name may already be a common word, a competitor's product or a place.
- **Script and direction.** Text expands and contracts in translation, which breaks packaging layouts, and right-to-left scripts change design entirely.

**The professional response** is not literal translation but **transcreation**: recreating the intended effect in the target language, checked by native speakers in that market. **The reliable rule:** never sign off a name or slogan for a market without someone from that market checking it.

## Inappropriate branding and promotion

- **Imagery** that shows too much skin, alcohol, or gestures that are offensive locally.
- **Humour**, which travels worse than any other element of advertising because it depends on shared reference points.
- **Comparative advertising**, which is normal in some markets and unlawful or regarded as rude in others.
- **Gender portrayal** that appears normal at home and dated or insulting elsewhere.
- **Religious or national references** used casually, which is the fastest way to cause offence.
- **Claims** that breach local advertising regulation, which is far stricter in some markets, especially for food, medicines and products marketed to children.
- **Labelling** that is inaccurate or misleading, which overlaps with the ethics in **4.3.4.2**.

**Why this now matters more than it did.** A campaign made for one market is visible everywhere within hours, so an advertisement that offends in one country becomes a global reputational problem. There is no longer any such thing as a purely local campaign, and that is the single most useful modern point to make here.

## How a business reduces the risk

- **Primary research in the market**, not desk research from home.
- **Employ local staff and local agencies**, and give them authority to say no.
- **Test before launch**, with local consumers.
- **Check names and slogans with native speakers** who live in the market.
- **Use a geocentric approach:** a global brand with locally adapted execution, from **4.3.3.1**.
- **Train expatriate managers** in the culture they are working in.
- **Monitor social media** so a mistake is caught in hours rather than weeks.

**The honest limitation:** a business cannot see its own assumptions, which is precisely why local people must be involved and listened to. A firm that hires local staff and overrules them has bought the information and thrown it away.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Product adaptation for religious dietary rules.** International food brands routinely reformulate for markets where particular ingredients are prohibited, and obtain local certification. The cost is real; the alternative is exclusion from the market altogether.

**Advertising withdrawn after local objection.** Campaigns are repeatedly pulled after criticism of how they portray a country, a gender or a religious practice. The pattern is consistent: the campaign was approved in the home market by people who did not know what it would mean elsewhere, and it was visible worldwide before anyone in the target market had been asked. The cost is the wasted production spend plus the reputational damage, which lasts considerably longer.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define culture or cultural sensitivity (2 marks); Explain (4) on one consideration; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on the importance of cultural factors, or on how a business should adapt its marketing for a specific country.

**Structuring "how should this business adapt?":**

1. **Identify the specific cultural or social features** the source gives. Use the source rather than generalising about the region.
2. **Take the 4Ps in turn** and say what must change and why. Product for dietary or regulatory reasons, place for retail structure, promotion for imagery and media, price for income.
3. **Give the strongest single risk** the business faces, usually an unintended meaning or inappropriate imagery.
4. **Say how it should be avoided:** local research, local staff, pre-testing.
5. **Judge how important these factors are relative to the others** in the unit. For a food or media product they may be decisive; for industrial components they matter far less.
6. **State the condition.**

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Cultural sensitivity matters more for some products than others**, which is the most useful comparative point.
- **No campaign is local any more**, because everything is visible everywhere.
- **A business cannot see its own assumptions**, so local staff are a necessity rather than a courtesy.
- **Do not confuse cultural difference with income difference.** Pack size is often about affordability.
- **Adaptation costs money** and must be weighed against the extra revenue.
- **Regulation forces some adaptation** whether the business considers it culturally necessary or not.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Treating culture as a minor detail.** It can make a market inaccessible.
- **Generalising about whole regions.** Cultures vary within countries as well as between them.
- **Confusing taste differences with deep cultural differences**, and both with income differences.
- **Assuming translation is enough.** Meaning, imagery and humour matter more than words.
- **Listing famous marketing mistakes without analysis.** Explain the mechanism, not the anecdote.
- **Forgetting local advertising regulation**, which is a hard constraint rather than a cultural preference.
- **Ignoring the global visibility of local campaigns.**
- **Recommending local research without saying what it would investigate.**

</Mistakes>
