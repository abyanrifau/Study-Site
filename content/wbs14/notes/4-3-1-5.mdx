## The four levels of integration

<Figure caption="Each level adds to the one below it">
<TradingBlocsDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Free trade area.** No tariffs between members; each member keeps its own external tariffs. The complication is **rules of origin**, needed to stop goods entering through the member with the lowest external tariff, and the paperwork is a real cost.
- **Customs union.** Adds a **common external tariff**, which removes the rules-of-origin problem and removes members' freedom to set their own trade policy.
- **Single market.** Adds free movement of **goods, services, capital and labour**, with harmonised standards so a product approved in one member is accepted in all.
- **Monetary union.** Adds a single currency, removing exchange rate risk and transaction costs between members, and removing each member's ability to set its own interest rate or let its currency adjust.

## The three named examples

**The EU and the single market.** The deepest integration of the three: a customs union plus free movement, harmonised product standards, common competition rules, and a monetary union covering most though not all members. **What it means for a business:** approval once for the whole market, no customs formalities between members, free recruitment across members, and a large enough market to justify a single production site serving all of it.

**ASEAN.** A regional grouping in South East Asia which has progressively reduced tariffs between members. Integration is looser than the EU's: it is closer to a free trade area with cooperation, without the harmonised regulation or free movement of labour. **For a business** it lowers tariffs across a large and fast-growing region, and it does not remove the need to meet each member's own standards.

**NAFTA.** The North American agreement, replaced in 2020 by the USMCA. It was a free trade area rather than a customs union, so **rules of origin** were central: to qualify for tariff-free treatment, a stated proportion of a product's value had to originate within the bloc. That drove manufacturers to source and assemble within North America, which is the clearest example of a bloc reshaping supply chains rather than simply cutting prices.

## Impact on businesses

**Benefits for a business inside the bloc:**

- **A larger tariff-free market**, and therefore higher volume and economies of scale.
- **Lower administrative costs:** one set of standards, fewer customs procedures.
- **Simpler location decisions.** Production can be concentrated where it is most efficient and shipped throughout the bloc.
- **Access to labour and capital** across members, in a single market.
- **No exchange rate risk** within a monetary union.
- **Stronger negotiating position**, because the bloc bargains collectively with outside countries.

**Costs and threats:**

- **More competition** from other members' firms in the home market, which is the same removal of barriers working in the other direction.
- **Loss of protection** for businesses that depended on it.
- **Compliance with bloc-wide regulation**, which may be stricter than the national rules it replaced.
- **Trade diversion.** The common external tariff can force a business to buy from a less efficient member instead of a cheaper non-member supplier, raising its costs.
- **Rules of origin** paperwork in a free trade area.
- **Exclusion.** A business **outside** the bloc faces the external tariff and may lose market share to insiders, which is a common reason for FDI inside the bloc.
- **Policy dependence.** Membership can change, and a business whose model depends on tariff-free access carries that risk.

**The central analytical distinction** is **trade creation against trade diversion**. Creation is the genuine gain: barriers fall, so more trade happens with efficient producers. Diversion is the loss: the external tariff makes an efficient non-member more expensive than a less efficient member, so trade shifts to the wrong producer. Whether a bloc is beneficial overall depends on which effect is larger, and saying so is the strongest evaluative point available here.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Locating inside the bloc.** Manufacturers from outside a customs union routinely build plants inside it, because a product assembled inside the bloc circulates tariff-free while an import faces the common external tariff. The bloc therefore attracts FDI from non-members, which is a benefit to the bloc and a cost to the business that has to make the investment.

**Small economies in a bloc.** For a very small economy, membership provides access to a market far larger than its own and reduces its ability to protect any domestic industry. The trade-off is sharper the smaller the member, because there is little domestic industry to protect and a great deal of market to gain.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define a trading bloc or a customs union (2 marks); Explain (4) on one impact; Analyse (6); Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business benefits from its country being in a bloc, or on how a business outside a bloc should respond.

**Structuring "how should a business outside the bloc respond?":**

1. **Identify the barrier:** the common external tariff and any regulatory requirements.
2. **Quantify the disadvantage** against an insider, using any figures given.
3. **Set out the options:** export and absorb the tariff, raise prices, invest inside the bloc, license a producer inside it, or focus elsewhere.
4. **Weigh cost against risk.** FDI removes the tariff and commits a large, largely irreversible sum.
5. **Recommend**, with the condition: it depends on the size of the tariff, the expected volume, and how permanent the bloc's arrangements look.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Trade creation against trade diversion** is the key distinction.
- **Removing barriers works both ways:** a larger market also means stronger competitors at home.
- **Blocs attract FDI from non-members**, which is how outsiders respond.
- **Deeper integration means less national policy freedom**, which matters when a member needs a different interest rate or exchange rate.
- **Rules of origin are a real cost** in a free trade area, and are removed by a customs union.
- **It depends on where the business is:** inside the bloc, outside it, or dependent on non-member suppliers.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing a free trade area with a customs union.** The common external tariff is the difference.
- **Describing the EU as only a free trade area.** It is a single market with a monetary union among most members.
- **Forgetting that NAFTA has been replaced by the USMCA**, while the specification still names NAFTA.
- **Listing benefits with no costs**, particularly the increase in competition at home.
- **Omitting trade diversion**, which is the main economic criticism of blocs.
- **Ignoring businesses outside the bloc**, which is often what the question is really about.
- **Not applying to the source.** Whether a bloc helps depends entirely on the business's position relative to it.

</Mistakes>
