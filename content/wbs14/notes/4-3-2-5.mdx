## Exchange rate movements

<Figure caption="How a change in the exchange rate affects importers and exporters">
<ExchangeRateDiagram />
</Figure>

**The rule is SPICED.** Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. It works for any currency: substitute the one in the question.

| | Currency appreciates | Currency depreciates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exporter** | products dearer abroad, volume falls, or margin is cut to hold the price | products cheaper abroad, volume rises, or margin improves |
| **Importer of inputs** | inputs cheaper, costs fall, margin improves | inputs dearer, costs rise, margin squeezed |
| **Business with overseas assets** | foreign profits worth less when converted | foreign profits worth more when converted |
| **Business with foreign currency debt** | repayments cost less in home currency | repayments cost more in home currency |

<Worked title="Worked example: the size of the effect">

A business exports at **$60** per unit and its home currency is the euro. The rate moves from **€1 = $1.10** to **€1 = $1.25**, an appreciation of the euro.

`Revenue per unit at the old rate = 60 ÷ 1.10 = €54.55`

`Revenue per unit at the new rate = 60 ÷ 1.25 = €48.00`

**Holding the dollar price** costs the business **€6.55 per unit**, a fall of **12%** in revenue per unit. On 40,000 units that is **€262,000** a year.

**Alternatively, protecting the euro revenue** means raising the dollar price to 54.55 × 1.25 = **$68.19**, a **13.6% price increase** to the customer. Whether volume survives that depends on price elasticity of demand: if demand is elastic, the volume loss exceeds the price gain and total revenue falls anyway.

**The judgement:** the business is choosing between a certain loss of margin and an uncertain loss of volume, and which is worse depends on elasticity and on what competitors do. If the competitors are also euro-based, all of them face the same pressure and the price can be raised. If they are dollar-based, they face none of it and a price rise loses the business the order. **That comparison is the strongest point available in an exchange rate question.**

</Worked>

## Volatility, and the natural hedge

**Level and volatility are different problems.** A permanently strong currency is a competitive disadvantage the business must adapt to. A volatile currency is a planning problem: a contract priced today and settled in six months can lose its entire margin to a movement nobody controls.

**Managing the risk:**

- **Price in your own currency**, which passes the risk to the customer, if the business has enough bargaining power.
- **Forward contracts**, which fix the rate and remove the uncertainty. They have a cost, and they remove any benefit from a favourable movement too.
- **Match costs and revenues in the same currency** by producing in the market where you sell. This is the **natural hedge**, and it is the most robust answer because it removes the exposure rather than insuring against it.
- **Operate in several currencies**, so movements partly offset one another.
- **Keep a margin for currency movement** in the price.
- **Accept it**, which is sometimes right for small exposures, because hedging costs money too.

**The point worth making:** a business that both imports inputs and exports output is **partly hedged already**, because a depreciation raises its input costs and its export revenue at the same time. Working out the **net** exposure is what a good answer does, rather than treating the business as purely an exporter or purely an importer.

## Skill shortages

**Where they come from:** rapid growth creating demand faster than education can supply it, an ageing workforce, emigration of skilled workers, mismatch between what is taught and what employers need, and restrictions on the movement of labour.

**The impact on a business:**

- **Higher wage costs**, as employers bid against each other for the same people.
- **Higher recruitment and training costs**, and higher turnover as staff are poached.
- **Output constrained** by the inability to staff the operation, regardless of demand.
- **Expansion delayed or abandoned**, which is the most serious effect: the market opportunity exists and cannot be taken.
- **Quality and productivity fall** where roles are filled by under-qualified staff.
- **Expatriate staff** used instead, at several times the local cost, with its own problems of retention and local resentment.

**The impact on international competitiveness:** a country that cannot supply skills loses inward investment to one that can, and its own businesses cannot compete on quality or innovation. This is why education and training appear so consistently in the location factors in **4.3.2.3**.

**How a business responds:** train internally, which is slow, expensive and risks the trained staff leaving; recruit internationally, subject to immigration rules; raise pay and improve retention, which addresses the symptom at a cost; automate, which removes the need for the skill; partner with local colleges to build the supply; or relocate the activity to where the skills exist.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Both pressures appear together in the tourism sector, which makes it a compact example.

**Currency.** Resorts earn revenue largely in foreign currency and pay for almost all their inputs in foreign currency too, since nearly everything is imported. The natural hedge is therefore unusually strong on the cost side, and the exposure sits mainly in the local-currency costs, chiefly wages.

**Skills.** Specialist roles such as chefs, dive instructors, spa therapists and senior management are in short supply relative to demand from a fast-growing number of properties. The consequences are the textbook ones: wages bid up, staff poached between resorts, and expatriate staff employed at a premium. A resort's expansion plans can be constrained by the inability to staff a new property rather than by finance or by demand, which is exactly the effect worth naming in an answer.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate the effect of an exchange rate change (2–4 marks); Explain (4) on one impact; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on the impact of exchange rate movements or skill shortages on a business.

**Method for an exchange rate calculation:**

1. **Write down the direction.** Which currency has strengthened, and by how much?
2. **Apply SPICED** and state whether the business is an exporter, an importer, or both.
3. **Do the arithmetic twice:** once holding the foreign price and showing the revenue effect, once holding the home-currency revenue and showing the price effect.
4. **Scale it up** to a total annual figure using the volume given.
5. **Bring in elasticity** to judge whether the price could actually be raised.

**Structuring the assessment:**

1. **Identify the net exposure.** Does the business import, export, or both?
2. **Quantify the effect** on contribution or profit.
3. **Set out the options** and the cost of each.
4. **Compare with competitors' exposure**, which determines whether a price rise is possible.
5. **Recommend**, with the condition: it depends on elasticity, on competitors' currencies, and on whether the movement is permanent or temporary.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **A business that imports and exports is partly hedged already**, so calculate the net position.
- **What competitors' currencies do decides whether a price rise is possible.**
- **Volatility can hurt more than the level**, because it prevents planning.
- **Hedging removes the downside and the upside**, and costs money.
- **Local production is the most robust hedge**, because it removes the exposure.
- **Skill shortages can constrain expansion even when demand and finance are available**, which is the sharpest point on that half of the topic.
- **It depends on whether the movement is permanent or temporary:** hedge a temporary one, relocate for a permanent one.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Getting SPICED backwards.** A stronger currency makes exports dearer, not cheaper.
- **Treating the business as only an exporter** when it also imports inputs.
- **Calculating the per-unit effect and not scaling it up** to an annual figure.
- **Ignoring elasticity** when saying the business can simply raise its price.
- **Forgetting competitors.** If they share the currency, the whole industry faces the same pressure.
- **Describing hedging as removing all risk.** It fixes the rate and gives up any favourable movement.
- **Treating skill shortages as only a cost problem.** The output and expansion constraint is more serious.
- **Recommending training with no reference to the time it takes** or the risk of losing the trained staff.

</Mistakes>
