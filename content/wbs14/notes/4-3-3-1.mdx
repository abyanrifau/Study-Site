## Global strategy and glocalisation

**A global marketing strategy** treats the world as one market: the same product, brand, positioning and broadly the same promotion everywhere.

- **For:** enormous economies of scale in production, packaging and advertising; one consistent global brand that travels with the customer; simpler management; faster rollout.
- **Against:** it ignores real differences in taste, income, culture, climate, regulation and infrastructure, and a message that works at home can be meaningless or offensive elsewhere.

**Glocalisation** keeps the global brand and adapts what has to be adapted. The phrase worth remembering is **think global, act local**.

**What usually stays global:** the brand name, the logo, the core positioning, the quality standards.
**What usually adapts:** flavours and formulation, sizes and packaging, price points, distribution channels, advertising execution, and the language and imagery of the message.

**The key judgement:** every adaptation costs money and loses some scale economy, so adapt only where the local difference is large enough to matter. The test is whether the adaptation buys more extra revenue than it costs, and stating that test explicitly is worth a mark.

## The three approaches

| | Ethnocentric (domestic) | Polycentric (international) | Geocentric (mixed) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Basis** | the home market's offer, exported | each country treated separately | global strategy with local adaptation |
| **Product** | unchanged | designed for each market | core product adapted at the margin |
| **Cost** | lowest | highest | moderate |
| **Local fit** | poor | best | good |
| **Brand consistency** | high | low | high |
| **Suits** | products with universal appeal; early, cautious entry | culturally sensitive products such as food | most large multinationals |

**Ethnocentric** is cheap and treats foreign markets as an afterthought, which is why it so often fails. **Polycentric** fits each market well and forfeits all the scale economies and any consistent global brand. **Geocentric** is what most successful multinationals actually do, and it is the honest answer to most questions here: standardise where you can, adapt where you must.

## Adapting the marketing mix

<Chunk title="The 4Ps abroad" open>

**Product.** Formulation and flavour for local taste; size and packaging for local incomes, household sizes and retail formats; voltage, plugs, driving side, climate durability; and compliance with local safety, labelling and ingredient rules. Regulation often forces adaptation whether the business wants it or not.

**Price.** Set by local incomes, competitors and what the market will bear, not by converting the home price. **Two techniques worth naming:** smaller pack sizes to reach a lower price point while protecting the price per unit, and a deliberately premium position where being foreign is itself a selling point. Watch the effect of tariffs and distribution costs on the final price, and note that a price far above the local norm restricts the business to a small urban segment.

**Place.** Distribution differs more between countries than any other element. Modern supermarkets in some markets, small independent retailers and open markets in others; direct sale in some, an entrenched distributor network in others; high e-commerce penetration in some, cash and low card use in others. Reaching the customer is frequently the binding constraint, not persuading them.

**Promotion.** The medium as much as the message: television, radio, print, mobile and social media penetration all vary. Language must be translated with care, since brand names and slogans can carry unintended meanings. Imagery, humour, the use of family, gender roles and religious references are all culturally specific, and advertising content is regulated differently everywhere.

</Chunk>

## Ansoff and Porter applied globally

<Figure caption="Ansoff's matrix: selling an existing product in a new country is market development">
<AnsoffDiagram />
</Figure>

**Ansoff.** Selling an existing product in a new country is **market development**: existing product, new market. It is the second-riskiest of the four options, riskier than market penetration because the customers, competitors and conditions are unknown, and less risky than diversification because the product is proven and the development cost is already sunk.

**Adapting the product for the new market moves the strategy towards diversification**, which raises both the cost and the risk. That is a neat way of expressing the cost of glocalisation in Ansoff's terms, and it is the kind of link that earns analysis marks.

<Figure caption="Porter's strategies: decide whether you compete abroad on cost or on differentiation">
<PorterStrategyDiagram />
</Figure>

**Porter.** Decide the basis of competition in the new market:

- **Cost leadership** requires scale and low costs, which global volume can deliver, and it invites competition from local producers with lower costs still.
- **Differentiation** relies on brand, quality or design, and being foreign can itself be a point of difference in some markets.
- **Focus** targets a narrow segment, which is often the sensible entry route: serve the urban premium segment first and broaden later.

**The trap Porter warns about** is being stuck in the middle: neither cheapest nor distinctive. A foreign entrant is particularly exposed to it, because it usually cannot beat local producers on cost and has not yet built enough brand recognition to charge a premium. Naming that risk is a strong evaluative point.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Fast food menus.** The brand, the restaurant format and the service standards are global; a significant part of the menu is local, reflecting religious dietary rules, regional tastes and locally available ingredients. It is the clearest widely known example of glocalisation: standardise the system, adapt the product.

**Pack sizes and price points.** Consumer goods companies selling in lower-income markets have made small single-use sachets a standard format, because it brings the product within reach of a daily rather than a monthly budget. The product is unchanged and the packaging and price point are completely different, which is adaptation in the place and price elements rather than the product itself.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define glocalisation or one of the three approaches (2 marks); Explain (4) on one adaptation; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business should standardise or adapt its marketing, which is one of the most frequent essay questions in this unit.

**Structuring "standardise or adapt?":**

1. **Identify the product and what it depends on.** Culturally neutral technology standardises well; food, clothing and media rarely do.
2. **Argue for standardisation:** economies of scale in production and advertising, a consistent global brand, speed, simplicity. Quantify if the source allows.
3. **Argue for adaptation:** taste, income, culture, regulation, infrastructure, each with a specific example from the source.
4. **Go through the 4Ps and give a different answer for each.** This is the move that lifts the answer: the product may standardise while the promotion and distribution must adapt.
5. **Judge**, and land on glocalisation with a reason: standardise the brand and the core product, adapt the price, place and promotion.
6. **State the condition:** it depends on how culturally sensitive the product is and how large the scale economies are.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The answer differs by P**, so a single verdict for the whole mix is a weak answer.
- **Every adaptation costs scale economies**, so the test is whether the extra revenue exceeds the cost.
- **Regulation can force adaptation** whether or not the business wants it.
- **Being foreign can be an asset**, which is an argument for less adaptation, not more.
- **Adaptation moves the strategy towards diversification** in Ansoff's terms, raising the risk.
- **A foreign entrant is exposed to being stuck in the middle.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing the three approaches.** Ethnocentric exports the home offer, polycentric treats each country separately, geocentric combines the two.
- **Defining glocalisation as simply translating the advertising.** It is adaptation of the offer, not just the language.
- **Giving one verdict for the whole marketing mix.**
- **Placing global expansion as diversification in Ansoff.** An existing product in a new country is market development.
- **Listing the 4Ps without applying them** to the specific country and product.
- **Assuming adaptation is always better.** It costs scale and can dilute the brand.
- **Forgetting distribution**, which is often the hardest element to get right abroad.

</Mistakes>
