## The nine drivers

<Chunk title="Trade liberalisation and the WTO" open>

Average tariffs have fallen substantially over decades, through multilateral agreements and a growing number of regional and bilateral deals.

**The WTO's three roles:** administering the agreed rules of trade, providing a forum for negotiating further liberalisation, and settling disputes between members, with the authority to permit retaliation where a member breaks the rules.

**Its limits:** multilateral negotiation has largely stalled, so liberalisation now happens mainly through regional deals; enforcement depends on the willingness of large members to comply; and critics argue the rules have favoured developed economies. For a business, the practical point is that **the rules can change**, and a business built entirely on current tariff levels carries a policy risk.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Political change">

Economies that were closed have opened: central planning was abandoned in many countries, import substitution gave way to export-led strategies, and capital controls were relaxed. Hundreds of millions of consumers and workers entered the world economy as a result.

**The reverse is also possible**, and recent years have seen tariffs reimposed, investment screened on security grounds and supply chains deliberately shortened. Globalisation is a trend, not a law.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Reduced cost of transport and communication">

**Transport.** Containerisation standardised handling, so loading a ship became a matter of hours rather than days, and larger vessels cut the cost per unit shipped. Air freight made high-value and perishable goods tradable.

**Communication.** Fibre optics, mobile networks and the internet reduced the cost of coordinating activity across continents to almost nothing. That is what makes it possible to design in one country, manufacture in a second, and provide customer service from a third.

**Why this matters more than the tariff reductions:** falling coordination costs are what made **global supply chains** possible, not just global selling. Without cheap communication, a business could export but could not manage production it did not stand next to.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Multinationals, investment and people">

**Increased significance of MNCs.** Large firms now organise production across borders, and a substantial share of world trade is between different parts of the same company.

**Increased investment flows.** FDI grew faster than trade for long periods, building productive capacity inside foreign markets.

**Migration.** Movement of workers within and between economies fills skill gaps, holds down wage growth in receiving countries, and generates remittances that are a major source of income for some sending countries.

**Growth of the global labour force.** The opening of large economies roughly doubled the labour available to internationally mobile capital, which held down wages for lower-skilled work in developed economies and raised them in the countries receiving the work.

**Structural change.** Manufacturing employment fell as a share of developed economies and rose in emerging ones, while services grew everywhere. The consequences are uneven: aggregate gains from trade coexist with concentrated losses in particular industries and regions, and that is the origin of much of the political pressure for protectionism in **4.3.1.4**.

</Chunk>

## Impact on businesses

**Opportunities:**

- **A much larger market**, so higher volumes and greater economies of scale.
- **Lower input costs** from global sourcing.
- **Access to skills and labour** wherever they are cheapest or best.
- **Extended product life cycles**, by selling a mature product into a growing market.
- **Risk spreading** across countries and currencies.
- **Access to finance** from international capital markets.

**Threats:**

- **More competition**, including from low-cost producers the business never previously faced.
- **Exchange rate exposure** on both sales and inputs.
- **Longer, more fragile supply chains.**
- **Greater complexity:** multiple regulators, tax systems, languages and cultures.
- **Reputational exposure.** Conditions anywhere in the supply chain can become a story everywhere, which is why **4.3.4.2** matters commercially and not just ethically.
- **Policy risk**, because tariffs and investment rules can be reimposed.

**The judgement most often required:** globalisation is not uniformly good or bad for a business. It benefits firms that can reach new markets or source more cheaply, and it threatens firms whose advantage was protection from foreign competition. Saying which type the business in the source is, and why, is the answer.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Container shipping.** The standard container did more to integrate world production than most trade agreements, because it collapsed the cost and unpredictability of handling cargo. It is the clearest single example of a technology, rather than a policy, driving globalisation.

**Services delivered remotely.** Software development, accounting, design and customer support are now routinely provided from countries far from the customer, which was impossible before cheap reliable communication. It also means that jobs once thought safe from international competition are not, which is a sharper version of structural change than the manufacturing story.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define globalisation or the WTO (2 marks); Explain (4) on one driver; Analyse (6) on how a driver has affected a business; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on the impact of globalisation on a business or an economy.

**Structuring the essay:**

1. **Define globalisation** and identify which drivers matter most in this case.
2. **Two developed benefits**, chained to effects: larger market to higher volume to lower unit cost to a stronger competitive position.
3. **Two developed threats**, chained the same way.
4. **Weigh them for this business**, using the source. Does it export, import, or only sell at home?
5. **Judge**, and give the condition: it depends on whether the business can reach new markets or is simply exposed to new competitors.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Falling communication costs mattered more than falling tariffs**, because they made supply chains possible rather than just selling.
- **The gains are aggregate and the losses concentrated**, which is why the politics turns against it.
- **Globalisation is reversible**, and policy risk is real.
- **It benefits some firms and threatens others**, so the answer depends on the business.
- **Larger markets bring stronger competitors** into the home market at the same time.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Defining globalisation as simply more trade.** It includes investment, migration and information flows.
- **Listing drivers without explaining their effect** on a business.
- **Treating the WTO as a body that sets tariffs.** It administers rules, negotiates and settles disputes; members set their own tariffs within those rules.
- **Presenting globalisation as wholly beneficial or wholly harmful.**
- **Ignoring the reversal.** Tariffs and investment restrictions have returned in several sectors.
- **Forgetting structural change**, which is where the political pressure comes from.
- **Writing generally when the question names a business.** Apply every point to it.

</Mistakes>
