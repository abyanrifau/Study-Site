## The five factors

<Chunk title="Levels and growth of disposable income" open>

**The level** tells you whether the product is affordable. **The growth rate** tells you whether the market will be larger next year, and it usually matters more, because a business entering a market is buying its future rather than its present.

**What the better answers add:**

- **Distribution.** A modest average income can conceal a large and rapidly growing middle class in the cities. For most consumer products the relevant market is that group, not the national average.
- **Income elasticity of demand**, from WBS11 **1.1.3.2**. A luxury good with high income elasticity gains disproportionately from rising incomes; a necessity gains little.
- **Population size.** Income per head multiplied by population is what determines total market value, and a high income in a tiny population is a small market.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Ease of doing business">

Company registration, permits and licences, property registration, contract enforcement, the tax system, and the ability to repatriate profits.

**Why it matters commercially:** it determines the time and cost of entry and the legal risk of operating. Weak contract enforcement means a distributor who does not pay may be beyond practical recourse. Restrictions on repatriating profit mean the returns cannot be brought home. Corruption raises costs, creates delay, and exposes the business to the ethical and legal problems in **4.3.4.2**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Infrastructure">

Roads, ports, airports, rail, power, water, telecommunications, internet and payment systems.

**For a market**, infrastructure determines whether you can physically get the product to the customer, how much distribution costs, and whether the customer can pay. Unreliable power, poor roads or low card and mobile-payment penetration can make an otherwise attractive market unserviceable. Weak cold-chain infrastructure rules out anything perishable.

**The geographic dimension** is easy to miss: a country may have good infrastructure in two cities and none elsewhere, so the addressable market is those cities.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Political stability">

Predictability of government, law and policy over the life of the investment. The risks are expropriation or forced local ownership, sudden tax or tariff changes, currency controls, civil unrest and conflict, and simple policy reversal.

**The crucial link to the type of entry:** exporting exposes the business to a modest amount of working capital and can be stopped. FDI commits assets that cannot be moved. **So political stability matters far more for a production location or a direct investment than for an export sale**, and making that distinction is a strong evaluative point.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Exchange rates">

Two separate issues.

**The level** determines price competitiveness. A strong host currency makes imported products cheap for local customers and makes locally produced exports expensive.

**The volatility** is what businesses complain about most, because it makes planning impossible. A contract priced today and paid in six months can lose its whole margin to a currency movement. **Management options:** price in your own currency and pass the risk to the customer, use forward contracts to fix the rate, match revenues and costs in the same currency by producing locally, or simply accept the risk and price a margin for it. This is developed in **4.3.2.5**.

</Chunk>

## Applying Porter's five forces to a potential market

The five factors above tell you the **size** of the opportunity. Porter tells you how much **profit** is likely to be available once you are in it, which is a different question and one students routinely skip.

| Force | The question to ask about the target market |
| --- | --- |
| **Competitive rivalry** | How many competitors, how strong, how aggressive on price? Is there a dominant local incumbent? |
| **Threat of new entrants** | How easy is entry? If it is easy for you, it is easy for the next entrant too, and margins will not last. |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | Are customers concentrated, such as a few large retailers who can dictate terms? |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | Are local inputs and distribution controlled by a few firms? |
| **Threat of substitutes** | What do customers use instead, including local products a foreign entrant may not have considered? |

**The conclusion Porter supports:** a large, fast-growing market with hostile forces may be less profitable than a smaller, slower market with weak forces. A growing market attracts entrants, which erodes exactly the margins that attracted them. Saying that is the single most sophisticated point available in this topic.

**A practical warning about entrant status.** A foreign entrant usually faces weaker bargaining power with suppliers and distributors than the local incumbent, no established brand, and no local knowledge. The five forces are not the same for every firm in the market, and they are usually worse for the newcomer.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Assessed against the five factors, the picture is mixed and instructive.

**Income** is relatively high for the region but the resident population is very small, so total market value for a consumer product is low whatever the income figure says. **Infrastructure** is dominated by geography: goods move between islands by boat or seaplane, which makes distribution costs high and lead times long, and rules out anything highly perishable at a mass-market price. **Ease of doing business** and **political stability** must be assessed on current evidence rather than assumed. **Exchange rates** matter unusually much, because almost all inputs are imported while much of the revenue in the tourism sector is earned in foreign currency.

**The lesson that generalises:** for a small island economy the market assessment usually fails on population and distribution cost rather than on income, and a business is far more likely to be interested in it as a **destination for tourism services** than as a market for physical goods.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is one of the most heavily examined topics in the unit: Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business should enter a particular market, almost always with data in the source.

**Method that earns high marks:**

1. **Use the source data.** Quote income per head, growth rates, population and any ease-of-doing-business or infrastructure evidence given. An answer with no figures cannot reach the top band.
2. **Take the factors in order of importance for this product**, not in the order they are listed. For a perishable food product, infrastructure comes first. For a luxury good, income distribution comes first.
3. **Link every factor to the product.** "GDP per head is $6,400 and growing at 6%" is data; "so the target group can now afford a $200 product, and the number who can is growing quickly" is analysis.
4. **Apply Porter explicitly**, naming two or three forces that matter most here.
5. **Identify the biggest single risk** and say how it could be managed.
6. **Recommend**, and give the condition it depends on, plus what further information you would want.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Growth matters more than the level**, because the business is buying the future.
- **Averages hide distribution**, and the real market may be an urban middle class.
- **Market size and market profitability are different questions**, and Porter answers the second.
- **A growing market attracts entrants**, which erodes the margins that attracted them.
- **The five forces are worse for a new entrant** than for the incumbent.
- **Political stability matters far more for FDI than for exporting.**
- **It depends on the entry method:** exporting can be reversed, FDI largely cannot.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing the five factors with no reference to the product or the data.**
- **Using total GDP instead of GDP per capita** to judge affordability.
- **Ignoring population size** when income per head looks attractive.
- **Assessing market size and never market profitability.**
- **Applying Porter as five definitions** rather than five questions about this market.
- **Treating political stability as equally important for exporting and for FDI.**
- **Mentioning exchange rates without saying whether the concern is the level or the volatility.**
- **Reaching a recommendation with no condition attached**, which caps the evaluation marks.

</Mistakes>
