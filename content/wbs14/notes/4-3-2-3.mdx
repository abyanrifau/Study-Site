## The nine factors

<Chunk title="Costs of production" open>

Wages, materials, energy, land and buildings, transport, and taxation.

**The distinction that separates a good answer from a weak one:** the wage rate is not the cost. What matters is **unit labour cost**, which combines the wage with productivity.

<Worked title="Worked example: why the cheaper wage can be the dearer option">

| | Country A | Country B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wage per hour | $4.00 | $18.00 |
| Units produced per hour | 2 | 12 |
| **Unit labour cost** | **$2.00** | **$1.50** |

Country A pays less than a quarter of Country B's wage and its **labour cost per unit is a third higher**, because productivity in B is six times greater.

Now add the rest. Shipping from A to the main market costs **$0.60** per unit and from B **$0.05**. A faces a **12%** tariff on a product with other costs of $6.00, so about **$0.96** per unit; B is inside the bloc and faces none.

`Country A: 2.00 + 0.60 + 0.96 = $3.56 per unit`

`Country B: 1.50 + 0.05 + 0 = $1.55 per unit`

The low-wage location is more than twice as expensive delivered. **Always build the delivered cost per unit**, not the wage comparison, and note that this is exactly why high-wage economies retain capital-intensive production.

</Worked>

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Skills and availability of labour force">

Not just cost but whether the right skills exist in sufficient numbers, and whether they can be recruited and retained. Consider the education level, technical and vocational training, language skills for service operations, the size of the available pool, and labour turnover.

**Skill shortages are the constraint that most often defeats a location decision**, and training takes years. Where the business needs a skill nobody local has, the choice is to train, to import expatriate staff at high cost, or to go elsewhere. This connects directly to **4.3.2.5**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Infrastructure">

For a **production** location the requirements are more demanding than for a market: reliable power above all, water, ports and airports with adequate capacity, roads to move inputs and output, and telecommunications to coordinate with head office.

**Power reliability is decisive for manufacturing.** A plant that stops when the supply fails either needs its own generation, which adds capital and running cost, or loses output. This is the factor that rules out many otherwise low-cost locations.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Location in a trade bloc">

Producing inside a bloc gives tariff-free access to every member, which can transform the economics, as the worked example shows. It also means complying with the bloc's regulations, which may be stricter and more expensive than national rules.

**In a free trade area, check the rules of origin.** Assembling inside the bloc from components made outside may not qualify for tariff-free treatment unless enough of the value originates within it.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Government incentives">

Grants, tax holidays, subsidised land, training subsidies, relaxed regulation, and special economic zones.

**Treat them with caution, which is the evaluative move.** They are usually time-limited, so the advantage expires while the plant remains. They can be withdrawn or changed by a new government. They may come with conditions on employment or local content. And a location that has to pay businesses to come is telling you something about its other disadvantages. **Incentives should tip a decision that is already marginal; they should not make the decision.**

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Ease of doing business and political stability">

As in **4.3.2.2**, and both matter **more** for a production location than for a market, because the investment is large and cannot be moved. Add the specific production concerns: employment law and the ease of adjusting the workforce, the enforceability of supplier contracts, customs efficiency for inputs and exports, and the security of property rights against expropriation or forced local ownership.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Natural resources">

Availability, quality, cost and security of supply. Decisive for extractive and processing industries, and important for any business where a raw material is a large share of cost or is bulky and expensive to move. Processing close to the resource saves transport on waste material.

**The related question is sustainability**, both because a depleting resource is a finite basis for an investment and because of the environmental considerations in **4.3.4.2**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Likely return on investment">

The factor that brings the others together. Estimate the revenue and costs the location would produce, and appraise it as an investment.

<Formula name="Return on investment" note="Compare with the return available from the alternative locations AND with the business's cost of capital. A positive return that is below the cost of capital destroys value.">
Return on investment = (Annual profit ÷ Investment) × 100
</Formula>

**Risk belongs in this judgement.** A higher expected return in an unstable country may be worth less than a lower return in a stable one, because the range of possible outcomes is wider. A business can express that by requiring a higher return from the riskier location, which is the practical meaning of a risk premium.

</Chunk>

## Weighing them up

The factors are not equally important, and which dominates depends on the business:

- **Labour-intensive manufacturing:** unit labour cost and skills first.
- **Capital-intensive manufacturing:** power reliability, infrastructure and skills, with wages far less important.
- **Bulky or heavy products:** transport cost and proximity to the market or the resource.
- **Perishable products:** transport time and cold-chain infrastructure.
- **Service operations:** language, education, telecommunications and time zone.
- **Extractive industries:** the resource itself, then the infrastructure to move it.

**So the correct opening move in any answer is to identify what kind of production this is**, and then rank the factors accordingly. A generic list in a fixed order cannot reach the top band.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A clear example of a location that fails on production and succeeds on services. **Against:** no significant natural resources for manufacturing, a very small labour pool, high imported input costs, island geography that makes moving materials expensive, and limited industrial infrastructure. **For:** a workforce with genuine hospitality skills, and a natural asset that cannot be reproduced anywhere else.

So a manufacturer would not locate there and a resort operator would, and it is the same set of factors producing opposite answers. That is the point worth taking from it: **location factors are only meaningful relative to what the business actually does.**

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business should locate production in a particular country, usually with cost and wage data in the source.

**Method:**

1. **Say what kind of production it is** and which factors therefore matter most.
2. **Build the delivered cost per unit** for each option: labour, materials, transport, tariff. Use unit labour cost, never the wage rate alone.
3. **Test the non-cost factors** that could defeat the cost advantage: skills, power, political stability, ease of doing business.
4. **Treat incentives as a bonus, not a reason.**
5. **Estimate or comment on the return** and compare it with the alternatives and with the required return.
6. **Recommend**, with the condition, and say what further information you would want.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Low wages do not mean low costs.** Unit labour cost is what matters.
- **Wages rise as economies grow**, so a cost advantage is temporary.
- **Incentives expire; the plant does not.**
- **Political stability matters more for FDI than for exporting**, because the assets cannot be moved.
- **Location inside a bloc can outweigh a higher production cost**, as the tariff calculation shows.
- **Skill shortages defeat more location decisions than wage rates do.**
- **It depends on the type of production**, which should be the first sentence of the answer.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Comparing wage rates instead of unit labour costs.**
- **Ignoring transport cost and tariffs**, so the cheapest factory gate looks like the cheapest delivered cost.
- **Listing all nine factors equally** instead of ranking them for this business.
- **Treating government incentives as a permanent advantage.**
- **Confusing the market assessment with the location assessment.** They use different factors and can point in opposite directions.
- **Forgetting rules of origin** when locating inside a free trade area.
- **Omitting the return on investment**, which is the factor that brings the others together.
- **Assuming a low-cost location stays low cost.**

</Mistakes>
