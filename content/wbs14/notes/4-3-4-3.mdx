## Why control is difficult

**MNCs are global; laws are national.** A company can locate profit, production and legal ownership in whichever country suits it, while no single government has authority over the whole group. Three consequences follow, and they shape every answer in this topic:

- **Regulatory arbitrage.** Put the activity where the rules are weakest.
- **Asymmetric bargaining.** A large multinational may have revenues exceeding a small country's national income, and the country needs the investment more than the company needs that country.
- **Competition between governments.** Countries bidding for investment with tax and regulatory concessions produce a **race to the bottom**, in which each is individually rational and all end up worse off.

## The seven factors

<Chunk title="Power of the MNC" open>

Size, market share, the number of jobs it provides, its share of a country's exports or tax base, and its control of technology or brands.

**The mechanism that matters:** the power is the **threat to relocate**. A company that can credibly move production elsewhere does not need to argue with a government; the possibility is the argument. Where the investment is genuinely mobile, such as assembly or service centres, that power is real. Where it is tied to a resource, a market or an immovable asset such as a mine or a resort, the power is far weaker, because the company cannot take the asset with it.

**So the first question in any answer is how mobile the operation actually is.** That single distinction explains most of the variation in how much leverage governments have.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Political influence">

Lobbying, industry associations, party funding where it is lawful, providing expertise to governments drafting the very regulation that will apply to them, employing former officials, and the simple weight of being a major employer.

**The effect** is not usually that laws are broken but that they are shaped, delayed or diluted before they are passed. Where institutions are weak, influence extends to corruption, which imposes costs on the business too and exposes it to prosecution under home-country anti-bribery law.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Legal control">

The main instruments: company law and licensing, employment law and minimum wages, health and safety, environmental standards, competition law, consumer protection and labelling, tax law and transfer pricing rules, and restrictions on foreign ownership or profit repatriation.

**Strengths.** Legally binding, enforceable through the courts, with penalties that alter behaviour, and it applies to every firm equally so it does not disadvantage the ethical one.

**Weaknesses.** It reaches only within national borders, so a group can restructure around it. Enforcement requires capacity that many countries lack: inspectors, courts and technical expertise. Penalties may be trivial relative to profit. Regulation takes years while business models change in months. And a government dependent on the investment may be unwilling to enforce what it has passed.

**The most effective responses are therefore cooperative:** international agreements on tax transparency and minimum corporate tax rates, coordinated action through blocs, and home-country legislation with extraterritorial reach, such as anti-bribery and modern slavery laws that hold a company responsible at home for conduct abroad. **That last mechanism is the most important development in this area** and is well worth naming.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Consumer pressure">

Boycotts, switching to competitors, online reviews and complaints, and ethical purchasing based on certification schemes.

**Strengths.** It goes straight to revenue, which is the language a business responds to fastest, and it needs no legislation. Reputation is a large part of the value of a consumer brand, so the threat is credible.

**Weaknesses.** The gap between what customers say and what they do is wide: stated willingness to pay more for ethical products consistently exceeds actual behaviour. Attention is short. It is ineffective against businesses with no consumer brand, so a component manufacturer or a mining company is largely immune. And it can harm exactly the workers it intends to help, because a boycott that cuts orders cuts their income.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Pressure groups">

Investigation and publicity, targeting brands rather than governments because brands respond faster, shareholder resolutions at annual meetings, lobbying for legislation, and running certification schemes.

**Strengths.** Expertise and persistence; the ability to investigate what regulators cannot reach; and the power of publicity, since a single documented case can achieve more than years of negotiation. They also operate internationally, which national regulators cannot.

**Weaknesses.** No legal authority, so they depend on persuasion. They may be accused of selecting evidence or pursuing an agenda. Attention concentrates on visible consumer brands, leaving the rest of an industry untouched. And they can only address the issues they choose to campaign on.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Social media">

**Why it changed the balance.** Evidence can be published by anyone from anywhere, instantly and at no cost. A photograph of a supplier's factory reaches customers before the company knows it exists. Campaigns organise in hours rather than months, and the record is permanent and searchable.

**Strengths.** Speed, reach, low cost, and it makes conduct visible in places regulators do not go. It has made it impossible to keep a local problem local, which is a genuine constraint on behaviour.

**Weaknesses.** Accuracy is not guaranteed, and businesses are damaged by claims that turn out to be false. Attention is extremely short, so pressure fades before change is delivered. Outrage is easily manufactured and easily faked. And a company can respond with public relations rather than substance, which social media is poorly equipped to detect.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Self-regulation">

Codes of conduct, supplier codes, industry standards, voluntary certification, and published sustainability reporting.

**Strengths.** Faster and more flexible than legislation; it can apply across the whole group in every country at once, which no national law can; it uses the company's own expertise about what is practicable; it is cheaper than compliance with badly designed regulation; and it can pre-empt regulation the industry would like even less.

**Weaknesses.** It is **voluntary and unenforceable**. Standards are set by those they apply to, so they tend to be undemanding. Monitoring is by the company or by auditors it pays. It is easily abandoned when it becomes expensive. And it is frequently used as a substitute for regulation rather than a complement to it: a published code with no verification is a public relations document.

**The fair judgement:** self-regulation is necessary, because it reaches where the law cannot, and insufficient, because nothing compels compliance. It works best where an industry's reputation is collectively at stake and where the codes are independently verified.

</Chunk>

## Reaching a judgement

No single mechanism is adequate. The realistic answer is that they work **together**: legal control sets a floor, international agreement closes the gaps between national laws, social media and pressure groups supply the information that enforcement lacks, consumer pressure makes reputation a financial matter, and self-regulation reaches the parts of a global group that no regulator can.

**The condition that decides how much control is achievable:** the mobility of the investment and the strength of the host country's institutions. A strong state regulating an immobile asset has real power. A weak state competing for mobile investment has very little, and in that situation control comes from **outside**: home-country legislation, consumer pressure in the company's main markets, and international agreement.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Extraterritorial legislation.** Anti-bribery and modern slavery laws in several developed economies require companies to answer at home for conduct abroad, and to report on their supply chains. This shifts enforcement from the weakest jurisdiction to the strongest, which is the only structural answer to regulatory arbitrage.

**International tax agreement.** Coordinated work on a minimum effective corporate tax rate and on country-by-country reporting attacks transfer pricing directly, by removing the benefit of shifting reported profit. It is slow, contested and the clearest illustration that controlling a global company requires governments to act together rather than compete.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one factor; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on how effectively MNCs can be controlled, or on which method is most effective.

**Structuring "how effectively can MNCs be controlled?":**

1. **State the core problem:** MNCs are global, laws are national.
2. **Take three or four mechanisms**, not all seven, and give the strength and the weakness of each.
3. **Use the source.** How large is this company relative to the country, how mobile is the investment, does it have a consumer brand?
4. **Make the mobility distinction:** a resort or a mine cannot relocate, a service centre can.
5. **Make the brand distinction:** consumer pressure works on branded businesses and barely touches others.
6. **Judge:** the mechanisms work together, and the achievable level of control depends on institutional strength and the mobility of the asset.
7. **Say what would improve it:** international agreement and home-country legislation with extraterritorial reach.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **MNCs are global, laws are national**, which is the structural problem.
- **The power is the threat to relocate**, so immobile investment is far easier to regulate.
- **Consumer pressure only works against consumer brands.**
- **Boycotts can harm the workers they aim to help.**
- **Self-regulation reaches where law cannot and compels nothing.**
- **Social media is fast and unreliable**, and its attention fades before change arrives.
- **Home-country extraterritorial law shifts enforcement to the strongest jurisdiction**, which is the most sophisticated point available.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing all seven factors superficially** instead of developing three or four.
- **Treating legal control as sufficient**, which ignores that a group can restructure around national law.
- **Assuming self-regulation is worthless.** It reaches across a whole group in a way no national law can.
- **Overstating consumer pressure**, particularly against businesses with no consumer brand.
- **Ignoring the mobility of the investment**, which is the factor that decides how much leverage a government has.
- **Forgetting international cooperation and extraterritorial legislation.**
- **Concluding that control is impossible.** The honest answer is that it is partial and depends on identifiable conditions.

</Mistakes>
