## Push factors

**Saturated markets.** Once most potential customers own the product, sales fall back to replacement demand. Growth then has to come from taking share from competitors, which is expensive, or from a new market. **The examinable indicator** is a high market penetration figure with low market growth: from WBS11 **1.1.1**, a mature or declining market.

**Competition.** A crowded home market means price pressure, heavy marketing spend and thin margins. A less competitive foreign market may offer better margins for the same product. Porter's five forces from WBS13 **3.3.1.2** is the tool for showing this formally.

**Other push factors worth naming:** tight domestic regulation, high domestic costs, a shrinking or ageing population, and unfavourable domestic economic conditions.

## Pull factors

**Increased sales and profitability.** The direct attraction: a larger or faster-growing customer base, and often higher margins where competition is weaker.

**Risk spreading.** Operating in several countries means a recession, a regulatory change or a bad season in one does not threaten the whole business. **The limit** is that countries are increasingly correlated, so a global downturn hits everywhere at once, and a business with a hundred markets still has one global exposure.

**Economies of scale.** Higher total volume spreads fixed costs, particularly research, development and brand-building, and improves purchasing power. This is the strongest pull factor for a business with high fixed costs, because unit cost falls with every unit of extra volume.

**Other pull factors:** access to resources, skills or technology, government incentives, and being close to major customers.

## Cost competitiveness through off-shoring and outsourcing

The distinction matters and is frequently confused.

| | Off-shoring | Outsourcing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What moves** | the activity, to another country | the activity, to another business |
| **Who performs it** | the business itself | a supplier |
| **Control** | retained | reduced |
| **Main gain** | lower cost, same standards | specialist expertise, lower fixed costs, flexibility |
| **Main risk** | management distance, cultural and time-zone issues | quality, reliability, loss of expertise, supplier power |

**They can combine:** contracting manufacture to a firm in another country is off-shored outsourcing, which is the most common arrangement and carries both sets of risks.

**Arguments for:** lower labour and production costs, access to specialist capability, lower fixed costs and greater flexibility, and the ability to concentrate management attention on core activities.

**Arguments against:** loss of control over quality and timing; longer, more fragile supply chains; the reputational exposure in **4.3.4.2**, since customers hold the brand responsible for its suppliers' conduct; loss of in-house expertise that is expensive to rebuild; rising wages eroding the saving; transport costs and lead times; and the redundancies and damage to morale at home.

**The evaluative point to make:** the saving is measurable and immediate, and the costs are largely non-financial and delayed. That asymmetry is why off-shoring decisions are often regretted, and naming it is worth a mark.

## Extending the product life cycle

A product that is mature or declining at home may be at the growth stage elsewhere, so the same product, with the development already paid for, generates growth again.

**Why it works:** development costs are sunk, production is already at scale, and the marketing does not have to educate the market about the product category. **Where it fails:** the product may not suit local tastes, infrastructure or income levels, and a product visibly obsolete at home can damage the brand's positioning if customers notice.

## Bringing it together

Push and pull almost always operate together, and the better answers say **which dominates**, because it changes the recommendation. A business **pushed** out of a saturated home market is under pressure and may accept a weaker opportunity than it should. A business **pulled** by a genuinely attractive market is choosing from strength and can walk away. Identifying which one applies is a genuine analytical move rather than a list.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

**Pull factors for a resort operator.** A hotel group based in a saturated European market finds a high-margin luxury market with very limited competition at the top end and a natural asset that cannot be replicated elsewhere. The pull is strong, and the push from home-market saturation reinforces it.

**Push factors for a Maldivian business.** The reverse case is more instructive. A domestic business faces a tiny home market: a small population dispersed across many islands, which caps volume whatever its market share. Expansion abroad is therefore driven almost entirely by push, and the option of growing at home simply is not available. That is the situation of businesses in every small economy, and it makes the push-pull distinction concrete.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define a push or pull factor, off-shoring or outsourcing (2 marks); Explain (4) on one factor; Analyse (6); Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on why a business should expand abroad, or on whether it should off-shore production.

**Structuring "should this business expand abroad?":**

1. **Identify the push factors in the source**, with evidence: market growth rates, penetration, competitor numbers, margins.
2. **Identify the pull factors**, with evidence: income growth, market size, competitive intensity.
3. **Say which dominates**, and what that implies about the strength of the business's position.
4. **Set out the main risk** and how it could be reduced.
5. **Recommend**, with the condition.

**Structuring "should it off-shore?":**

1. **Quantify the saving.** Wage differential multiplied by hours, or cost per unit multiplied by volume.
2. **Set out the non-financial costs:** control, quality, lead time, reputation, redundancies.
3. **Note the asymmetry:** the saving is immediate and measurable; the costs are delayed and hard to quantify.
4. **Consider alternatives:** automation at home, outsourcing without off-shoring, or partial relocation.
5. **Recommend**, with the condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Push and pull differ in what they imply about bargaining position.**
- **Risk spreading has limits**, because economies are correlated.
- **Off-shoring savings are measurable; the costs largely are not.**
- **Wage advantages erode** as the host economy grows.
- **Extending the life cycle works only if the product suits the new market.**
- **Economies of scale are the strongest pull factor where fixed costs are high.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing off-shoring with outsourcing.** One is about location, the other about ownership of the activity.
- **Listing push and pull factors without saying which dominates.**
- **Treating risk spreading as complete protection.**
- **Assuming a saturated home market makes expansion abroad automatically correct.**
- **Ignoring the reputational risk** of an off-shored or outsourced supply chain.
- **Forgetting that off-shoring creates redundancies at home**, with morale and reputational consequences.
- **Not quantifying** when the source gives wage rates or volumes.

</Mistakes>
