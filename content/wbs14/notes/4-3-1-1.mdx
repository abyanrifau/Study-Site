## The three categories

| | Developed | Emerging | Developing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Income per head | high | rising fast | low |
| Dominant sector | services | manufacturing growing | primary, agriculture |
| Infrastructure | mature | improving, often patchy | limited |
| Growth rate | low, 1–3% | high, 4–8% | variable, often volatile |
| Business opportunity | replacement demand, premium products | **fastest-growing consumer markets** | low-cost production, basic goods |
| Main risks | saturation, high costs | volatility, regulation, exchange rates | political instability, weak infrastructure |

**Use the categories as a starting point, not a conclusion.** Averages hide enormous variation within a country, and a single national figure tells you little about the urban market a business would actually sell into.

## The shift in economic power

Growth has been concentrated in Asia, and increasingly in parts of Africa, with several economies expanding at several times the rate of the developed world for sustained periods. The consequences for business are specific:

- **New markets.** A rising middle class creates demand for goods that were previously unaffordable.
- **New competitors.** Firms from emerging economies now compete internationally, often on cost and increasingly on quality.
- **New suppliers.** Supply chains have been rebuilt around low-cost manufacturing locations.
- **New sources of investment.** Capital now flows out of emerging economies as well as into them.

## Implications for individuals and businesses

**Trade opportunities.** Rising incomes shift spending from necessities to discretionary goods: processed food, consumer electronics, cars, financial services, travel. A business selling a product with high income elasticity of demand benefits most, which is the link to WBS11 **1.1.3.2**.

**Employment patterns.** Growth moves labour out of agriculture into manufacturing and then into services. Urbanisation accelerates. Skills demand rises, and skill shortages appear in exactly the roles growth creates. Wages rise, which erodes the low-cost advantage that attracted production in the first place, so **the location that is cheap today may not be cheap in ten years**.

## Measuring growth and development

<Formula name="GDP per capita" note="Compare countries per head, not by total GDP. A large total with a large population may mean a poor market for consumer goods.">
GDP per capita = GDP ÷ Population

Growth rate = ((GDP this year − GDP last year) ÷ GDP last year) × 100
</Formula>

**GDP and GDP per capita.** Output measures, and the usual first indicator of market size. **Limitations:** they say nothing about distribution, so a high average can coexist with widespread poverty; they exclude the informal economy, which is large in many developing countries; and comparisons need adjustment for differences in the cost of living.

**HDI.** Combines income per head with **life expectancy** and **years of schooling**, giving a figure between 0 and 1. Because it includes health and education, it captures whether growth has actually improved lives. **Limitations:** it excludes inequality, environmental quality and political freedom, and three indicators cannot capture everything that matters.

**Which one a business should use** depends on the decision. Assessing **market size** points to GDP per capita and its growth. Assessing a **production location** points to HDI, because education and health predict the skills and reliability of the workforce.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A useful case for the limits of the categories. GDP per capita is high for the region, driven by tourism, yet the economy is narrow: one sector dominates output, employment and foreign exchange earnings. HDI is relatively high, reflecting genuine investment in health and education.

For a business, the national figures mislead in both directions. As a **market**, the resident population is small and dispersed across many islands, so distribution costs are high and volumes low, whatever the income figure suggests. As a **location**, the skills are concentrated in hospitality and the geography makes manufacturing impractical. The general lesson applies far beyond one country: **a national average is a starting question, not an answer.**

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define GDP, GDP per capita or HDI (2 marks); calculate a growth rate or GDP per capita (2–4); Explain (4) on one implication of growth; Assess (8–12) on whether a country represents an attractive market.

**Structuring an assessment:**

1. **Classify the economy** from the evidence in the source.
2. **Use the data.** Quote the growth rate, the income per head, the HDI.
3. **Link each figure to the business.** Rising income per head means more demand for the specific product in the question.
4. **Note what the figures hide:** distribution, the informal economy, the cost of living, regional variation.
5. **Judge**, and say what further information you would want.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Averages hide distribution.** The market may be a small urban middle class rather than the whole population.
- **GDP measures output; HDI measures development**, and the right one depends on the decision.
- **High growth is volatile growth**, and a fast-growing economy can contract sharply.
- **Wages rise as economies grow**, so a low-cost location is a temporary advantage.
- **Population size matters as much as income per head** for market potential.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing developing with emerging.** Emerging economies are growing rapidly from a developing base.
- **Using total GDP to judge income levels.** Use GDP per capita.
- **Treating a high GDP per capita as proof of a good market** without considering population size or distribution.
- **Defining HDI as an income measure.** It combines income, health and education.
- **Quoting data without linking it to the business** in the question.
- **Assuming growth benefits all businesses equally.** It depends on income elasticity of demand.
- **Forgetting that growth raises wages**, which erodes a cost advantage over time.

</Mistakes>
