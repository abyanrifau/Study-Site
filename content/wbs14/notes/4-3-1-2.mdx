## Exports and imports

**Exports bring money in; imports take money out.** For a business, exporting means access to a market many times larger than the domestic one, and it means dealing with different regulation, currencies, distribution and tastes.

**Why a business exports:** a saturated home market, spare capacity, spreading risk across countries, extending a product's life cycle where the home market has matured, and economies of scale from higher volume.

**Why importing matters to a business too:** cheaper inputs, materials unavailable at home, and access to specialist components. A business with imported inputs and domestic sales is exposed to exchange rate movements in the opposite direction from an exporter, which is a point worth making in **4.3.2.5**.

## Specialisation

The logic is that a country, or a business, should concentrate on what it produces **relatively** best and trade for the rest. Total output is then higher than if each tried to produce everything.

**Implications for countries:**

- **Higher output and income**, and lower prices for consumers.
- **Economies of scale** in the specialised industries.
- **Dependence.** A country specialising in one or two products is exposed to a price fall, a crop failure, or a collapse in demand for that one thing.
- **Structural vulnerability.** When demand shifts away from the specialism, the skills and capital are in the wrong industries, and adjustment takes years.

**Implications for businesses:**

- **Focus on core competences** raises quality and lowers unit cost.
- **Longer, thinner supply chains.** Specialisation at each stage means more suppliers in more countries, which is efficient and fragile. A disruption anywhere stops production everywhere, which is the contingency planning point from WBS13 **3.3.6.2**.
- **Dependence on a few suppliers or customers** raises their bargaining power, which is Porter again.

**The balanced judgement:** specialisation raises efficiency and reduces resilience. How far to take it depends on how volatile the market is and how easily a disrupted supply could be replaced.

## Foreign direct investment

FDI means putting productive assets into another country, rather than simply selling into it. **Greenfield** means building new; **brownfield** means buying or expanding what exists.

**Why a business chooses FDI over exporting:**

- **Tariffs and other barriers** make exporting expensive, so producing inside the market avoids them.
- **Transport costs** on bulky or perishable products.
- **Being close to the customer** for service, responsiveness and local adaptation.
- **Lower production costs** in the host country.
- **Access to resources, skills or technology** that exist there.
- **Local knowledge and credibility**, particularly where customers or governments prefer a local presence.

**Greenfield against brownfield.** Greenfield gives complete control over design, culture and standards, and it is slow and starts with no customers or local knowledge. Brownfield is fast and brings customers, staff and local relationships, and it carries the integration and culture-clash risks from WBS13 **3.3.2.3**.

**What FDI does for the host country:** capital, jobs, technology and skills transfer, tax revenue, an improved balance of payments, and pressure on local firms to raise standards. **Against that:** profits are repatriated, local competitors may be driven out, the investment may be footloose and leave when incentives end, and the host government's bargaining position against a large multinational is weak. That is the substance of **4.3.4.1**.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Vietnam as a production location.** Manufacturers relocated assembly there as wages rose elsewhere, attracted by labour costs, improving infrastructure and trade agreements giving access to larger markets. Inward FDI brought jobs and technology transfer, and rising wages have already begun to erode the original cost advantage, which is the recurring pattern.

**Automotive assembly inside trade blocs.** Producers build inside a bloc rather than export into it, because a car assembled inside the bloc avoids the external tariff. The FDI decision is driven by trade policy rather than by production cost, which is a useful example of protectionism changing business behaviour rather than simply raising prices.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define exports, FDI or specialisation (2 marks); Explain (4) on one reason for FDI; Analyse (6) on the implications of specialisation; Assess (12) or an essay on whether a business should export or invest directly.

**Structuring "export or invest directly?":**

1. **State what the business currently does** and why the decision has arisen.
2. **Make the case for exporting:** lower risk, less capital, reversible, keeps production together for economies of scale.
3. **Make the case for FDI:** avoids tariffs and transport costs, closer to the customer, lower production costs, local credibility.
4. **Quantify where the source allows it:** the tariff rate, the wage differential, the transport cost.
5. **Consider the risk:** FDI is a large, largely irreversible commitment in a country the business may not know well.
6. **Recommend**, and give the condition: it depends on the size of the barriers, the expected volume, and how much risk the business can carry.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Exporting is reversible; FDI largely is not.** That asymmetry is the heart of the decision.
- **Specialisation raises efficiency and reduces resilience.**
- **Tariffs can make FDI the cheaper option** even when production costs are higher.
- **Cost advantages erode as host-country wages rise.**
- **Greenfield gives control; brownfield gives speed and local knowledge.**
- **It depends on the product:** bulky or perishable goods favour local production; high-value, low-weight goods favour exporting.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing FDI with portfolio investment.** FDI means productive assets and a controlling interest, not buying shares for a return.
- **Treating exporting and FDI as the same growth route.** They differ in cost, risk and reversibility.
- **Presenting specialisation as purely beneficial.** Dependence is the cost.
- **Forgetting the importing side.** Many businesses are exposed to exchange rates through inputs, not sales.
- **Assuming FDI always benefits the host country.** Profit repatriation and footloose investment are real objections.
- **Describing FDI without applying it** to the business and country in the question.

</Mistakes>
