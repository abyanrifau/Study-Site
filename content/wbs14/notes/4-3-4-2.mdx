## Stakeholder conflicts

Operating internationally multiplies the stakeholders and sharpens the conflicts.

| Stakeholder | What they want | Conflicts with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** | returns, low costs | wages, environmental spending, tax paid |
| **Home-country employees** | job security | off-shoring to lower-cost locations |
| **Host-country employees** | pay, conditions, security | the cost advantage that brought the investment |
| **Host government** | tax, jobs, technology, compliance | transfer pricing, tax holidays, imported inputs |
| **Home government** | jobs and tax at home | production and profit moving abroad |
| **Local community** | employment, environment, land | emissions, water use, displacement |
| **Customers** | low prices | the wages and conditions that make low prices possible |
| **Pressure groups and media** | higher standards | the cost of meeting them |

**The conflict that defines the topic** is between **customers wanting low prices** and **workers in the supply chain wanting decent pay**. It cannot be resolved by good intentions, because the low price depends on the low wage. Stating that clearly is what separates an analytical answer from a moralising one.

## Environmental considerations

**Emissions and waste disposal.** Air and water pollution, greenhouse gases, hazardous waste, and the disposal of products at end of life. Standards and enforcement vary enormously, so a company can lawfully do abroad what would be a criminal offence at home. That legal freedom is exactly where the ethical question begins.

**Sustainability.** Deforestation, overfishing, water depletion, soil degradation, and single-use materials. **The commercial case for sustainability is often strong**, because depleting the resource destroys the business, and because using less material and energy costs less. The specific temptation is that the damage falls in the host country and the saving accrues to the company.

**The response:** a global environmental standard applied everywhere regardless of local law; measuring and reporting impact; investing in efficiency, which frequently pays for itself; and designing products to use less material. **The risk in the response** is **greenwashing**: claiming more than the business does, which is worse than claiming nothing because the gap becomes the story.

## Supply chain considerations

**Pay and working conditions.** Wages at or below subsistence, excessive hours, compulsory overtime, unsafe buildings and machinery, no sick pay or job security, and suppression of union representation.

**Exploitation of labour and child labour.** Children working instead of attending school; forced or bonded labour; workers whose documents are held; unsafe work given to those with no alternative.

**Why a business cannot disclaim responsibility.** Customers, media and pressure groups hold **the brand** responsible for its suppliers' conduct, whatever the contract says. The business has the commercial power in the relationship, so it has the practical ability to set conditions. And it benefits from the low cost, so it cannot claim no connection to how that cost is achieved.

**Why it is genuinely difficult, which a fair answer acknowledges:**

- **Supply chains are long and opaque.** A first-tier supplier subcontracts, and the subcontractor subcontracts again. Auditing tier one does not reveal what happens at tier three.
- **Audits can be evaded.** Notice is given, records are prepared, and underage workers are absent on the day.
- **Withdrawing can harm the workers.** Cancelling the order removes the income and does nothing about the conditions, so the ethical response is usually to require improvement and verify it, not to leave.
- **Cost.** Higher standards raise prices in a market where competitors' prices have not risen.
- **Local norms differ**, and imposing home-country standards can be criticised as ignoring local circumstances. The counter-argument is that some standards, such as safety and the prohibition of child labour, are not matters of local preference.

**What responsible practice looks like:** a published supplier code, mapping the chain beyond tier one, unannounced audits, long-term relationships rather than lowest-price switching, paying enough to make compliance possible, remediation rather than immediate termination, and transparency about what has been found.

## Marketing considerations

**Misleading product labelling.** Overstated health claims, hidden sugar, salt or fat content, false claims of origin, unsubstantiated environmental claims, and packaging designed to imply more content than there is. Where labelling regulation is weaker, the temptation to claim more is greater.

**Inappropriate marketing activities.** Marketing to children, promoting products in ways that discourage a better alternative, aggressive selling of credit to people who cannot afford it, exploiting lower advertising standards to make claims that would be unlawful at home, and promoting products known to be harmful in markets with weaker regulation.

**The pattern worth naming across all four areas:** the ethical failure is almost always **doing abroad what would not be permitted at home**, justified by the fact that the local law allows it. That is the double standard, and it is the single most useful framing for an essay on international business ethics.

## Judging it

**The case for high global standards:** reputation is global and a scandal anywhere is a scandal everywhere; staff and customers increasingly expect it; regulation follows public anger, so getting ahead is cheaper than being forced; efficiency and sustainability often coincide; and consistent standards are simpler to manage than a different standard in each country.

**The case against, honestly stated:** it costs more, competitors who do not do it have lower costs, customers frequently say they will pay more for ethical products and then do not, and enforcement in a long supply chain is expensive and imperfect.

**The balanced conclusion:** in the short run ethics costs money; over a long enough horizon reputation, retention and avoided scandal usually outweigh it. Where the resource being protected **is** the business, as with a reef, a fishery or a forest, the conflict largely disappears, because damaging the asset destroys the business.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

**Environment.** The strongest available example of ethics and long-run profit coinciding: reef damage from construction, waste and sewage destroys the asset tourists pay to see. An environmental standard here is a commercial policy, and the only pull in the other direction is the short-run construction saving.

**Labour.** Staff on remote island properties may work long seasons away from their families, living in staff accommodation. Legal minimum standards exist; the ethical questions concern rest days, accommodation quality, family contact and pay relative to the rates guests pay. The short-run cost is real and the long-run return comes through retention, which matters enormously where a departing specialist cannot be replaced mid-season.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one ethical consideration; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business should apply the same ethical standards worldwide, or on how it should respond to a supply chain problem in the source.

**Structuring "should it apply home standards everywhere?":**

1. **Identify the specific issue** in the source: wages, conditions, child labour, emissions, labelling.
2. **State the short-run cost** with figures if given.
3. **Argue the commercial case for high standards:** reputation, retention, regulation, market access, efficiency.
4. **Argue the case against:** cost, competitor advantage, the gap between what customers say and do, and the difficulty of enforcement in a long chain.
5. **Name the double standard explicitly** and say why legality is not the test.
6. **Recommend a specific course of action**, not a sentiment: publish a code, map the chain, audit unannounced, require remediation, pay enough to make compliance possible.
7. **State the condition:** it depends on the visibility of the issue, the customers, and the time horizon.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The double standard is the core issue:** legal abroad does not make it right.
- **Customers say they will pay more and often do not**, which undermines the commercial case.
- **Withdrawing from a supplier can harm the workers**, so remediation usually beats termination.
- **Audits can be evaded**, and tier-one compliance says nothing about tier three.
- **Where the environment is the product**, ethics and profit align.
- **Greenwashing is worse than silence.**
- **Short run against long run** is the distinction that organises the whole judgement.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Moralising instead of analysing.** Set out the commercial consequences on both sides.
- **Confusing legality with ethics.** The whole topic lives in the gap between them.
- **Claiming a business has no responsibility for its suppliers.**
- **Recommending immediate withdrawal from a supplier** without considering the effect on the workers.
- **Assuming customers will pay a premium** without evidence.
- **Treating an audit as proof of compliance.**
- **Ignoring the genuine difficulties**, which makes the answer one-sided and weaker.
- **Forgetting the marketing considerations**, which students omit far more often than the supply chain ones.

</Mistakes>
