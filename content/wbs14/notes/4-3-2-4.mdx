## The ten reasons

The specification lists ten. They group usefully into four themes.

<Chunk title="Scale and risk" open>

**Spreading risk and economies of scale.** Operating across more countries reduces dependence on any one, and higher combined volume spreads fixed costs, particularly research, development and brand-building.

**Sharing costs and risks.** A joint venture splits the capital required and the downside. This is why it is common for very large projects: an investment that would be reckless alone is manageable shared.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Access to markets">

**Entering new markets or trade blocs.** An acquisition inside a bloc gives tariff-free access to every member. This is the fastest entry route available: the customers, distribution and regulatory approvals already exist.

**Accessing supply chains and distribution networks.** In many markets distribution is the binding constraint, controlled by established players. Buying or partnering with one of them solves in a day what would take years to build, and building it may not be possible at all.

**Making use of local knowledge.** Customer preferences, regulation, business practices, government relationships and how things actually get done. It is the factor most often underestimated by confident foreign entrants, and the one a local partner supplies most cheaply.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Assets and resources">

**Acquiring national or international brand names and patents.** A brand takes decades to build and can be bought in months. The same applies to patents, which may be unobtainable any other way.

**Securing resources and supplies.** Vertical integration to guarantee access to a material, or to secure it before a competitor does. It also protects the business against supplier bargaining power.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Competition and compulsion">

**Maintaining or increasing global competitiveness.** Scale is itself a competitive weapon in industries where it determines cost. Consolidation in one part of an industry forces consolidation in the rest.

**Reducing competition.** Buying a competitor removes it. Effective, and the reason competition authorities exist, and large deals may be blocked or permitted only with conditions.

**Government or legal requirement.** Some countries require foreign businesses to operate through a joint venture with a local partner, or cap foreign ownership, particularly in strategic sectors. Here the joint venture is not a choice but the price of entry.

</Chunk>

## Comparing the three routes

| | Merger or takeover | Joint venture | Organic growth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | fast | fast | slow |
| Cost | high, often with a premium | shared | spread over time |
| Control | full | shared | full |
| Risk | carried alone | shared | lower per stage |
| Local knowledge | acquired with the business | supplied by the partner | must be learnt |
| Main problem | integration and culture | partner disagreement | too slow for the opportunity |
| Reversibility | very low | moderate | high |

**How to choose.** Where speed matters and the business has the capital and the confidence to manage integration, acquire. Where the risk is large, the market unfamiliar, or a local partner is required, use a joint venture. Where the market can be entered gradually and mistakes must stay small, grow organically.

## Why they fail

- **Overpayment.** A premium is paid for the target, and the expected synergies do not appear. This is the single most common financial cause.
- **Culture clash.** Two workforces with different attitudes to hierarchy, working hours and decision-making do not merge because a contract was signed. From WBS13 **3.3.4.1**, it is the most common single cause of acquisition failure, and it is worse across borders.
- **Overestimated synergies.** Cost savings assume the two operations can actually be combined, which regulation, geography and incompatible systems may prevent.
- **Poor due diligence.** Liabilities, contracts or quality problems discovered after completion.
- **Diseconomies of scale.** Coordination and communication costs rise faster than expected across time zones and languages.
- **Regulatory intervention.** Blocked, delayed, or permitted only with disposals that remove the point of the deal.
- **In a joint venture:** disagreement over strategy, profit sharing or reinvestment; unequal effort; and the risk of transferring technology or know-how to a partner who later becomes a competitor. That last point is the sharpest criticism of joint ventures and is worth remembering.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Joint ventures as the price of entry.** In several large markets, foreign firms in sectors such as automotive, banking and telecommunications have had to operate through local joint ventures. The arrangement gave access and local knowledge, and it also transferred technology and management practice to partners who subsequently competed internationally. The access was real; so was the cost.

**Acquiring a brand rather than building one.** Consumer goods groups routinely enter a market by buying an established local brand instead of launching their own, because local brand loyalty in food and drink is strong and expensive to overcome. The acquirer gains distribution and shelf space immediately, and then faces the decision whether to keep the local name or replace it with its global one, which is the glocalisation question in **4.3.3.1**.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one reason; Analyse (6); Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a business should enter a market by acquisition, joint venture or organic growth.

**Structuring the comparison:**

1. **State the objective.** Speed, access, scale, risk reduction or compliance with a local requirement.
2. **Take each route in turn** and give its main advantage and main drawback **for this business and this market**.
3. **Use the source.** The business's cash position, its previous experience abroad, and any legal requirement in the target country usually decide the answer.
4. **Address integration explicitly** if recommending an acquisition, because that is where these deals fail.
5. **Recommend**, with the condition: it depends on the urgency, the capital available, how unfamiliar the market is, and whether a local partner is required.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Acquisition buys speed and imports someone else's culture.**
- **A joint venture shares the risk and shares the control**, and disagreement is the usual failure.
- **Technology transfer to a joint venture partner can create a future competitor.**
- **Overpayment is the commonest financial cause of failure.**
- **A legal requirement removes the choice**, so check whether the country permits full ownership.
- **Reversibility differs sharply** between the three routes, and that matters most where the market is unfamiliar.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing a merger with a takeover.** A merger is agreed and creates a new combined business; a takeover is an acquisition of control.
- **Confusing a joint venture with a strategic alliance.** A joint venture involves shared ownership of a new enterprise.
- **Listing all ten reasons** instead of selecting the two or three that apply.
- **Ignoring integration and culture** when recommending an acquisition.
- **Forgetting that a joint venture may be legally required.**
- **Assuming synergies will be achieved** because they were forecast.
- **Omitting organic growth** as an option when the question asks how to expand.

</Mistakes>
