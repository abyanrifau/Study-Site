## Why governments protect

- **Protecting jobs** in industries facing cheaper foreign competition, and the political pressure that goes with concentrated job losses.
- **Infant industries.** A new industry cannot reach the scale to compete until it has grown, so it is protected temporarily. **The objection:** temporary protection rarely ends, because the industry has no incentive to become competitive and every incentive to lobby.
- **Strategic industries:** food, energy, defence, and after recent disruptions, semiconductors and medicines. Self-sufficiency is bought at a cost.
- **Preventing dumping.** If foreign producers sell below cost to destroy domestic competitors, an anti-dumping duty is a defensible response, and proving dumping is difficult and the accusation is easily misused.
- **Correcting a trade deficit**, which is generally ineffective because imports are also inputs and retaliation reduces exports.
- **Raising revenue**, which matters most for governments with limited tax systems.
- **Standards.** Genuine health, safety and environmental requirements, which can also serve as disguised protection.

## Tariffs

<Figure caption="The effect of a tariff on price, domestic output and imports">
<TariffDiagram />
</Figure>

A tariff raises the price paid in the domestic market from the world price to the world price plus the tariff. Three things follow:

- **Domestic producers supply more**, because the higher price makes more output profitable.
- **Domestic consumers buy less**, because the price is higher.
- **Imports fall**, squeezed from both ends, which is the whole purpose.

**Who gains and who loses.** Domestic producers gain higher prices and volumes. Government gains the tariff revenue. **Consumers lose**, paying more and consuming less. Businesses that **use** the imported good as an input lose too, and that is the effect students most often miss: a tariff on steel protects steelmakers and raises costs for every manufacturer that buys steel.

<Worked title="Worked example: the cost of a tariff">

A business imports a component at **$40** per unit and uses **50,000** a year. A **15%** tariff is imposed.

`Tariff per unit = 40 × 15% = $6.00`

`Additional annual cost = 50,000 × 6.00 = $300,000`

If the business sells 50,000 finished units and passes the whole increase on, the price rises by $6. If it absorbs it, and contribution per unit was $22, contribution falls to **$16**, a **27% reduction**.

`New break-even output = Fixed costs ÷ 16 rather than ÷ 22`

So fixed costs of $250,000 would push break-even from 11,364 units to **15,625 units**, a rise of nearly 38%. That is the calculation that turns a description of a tariff into analysis, and it also shows why a business might respond by producing inside the market instead of importing.

</Worked>

## Quotas and other barriers

<Figure caption="An import quota caps the quantity allowed in, so the price rises">
<ImportQuotaDiagram />
</Figure>

**Quotas** cap the quantity regardless of price. Compared with a tariff: a quota gives a **certain** limit on the volume of imports, while a tariff gives a certain cost and an uncertain volume. A quota raises the domestic price without generating any government revenue, and the gain goes instead to whoever holds the import licences. For an exporting business a quota is worse than a tariff, because no amount of cost cutting gets more product through.

**Government legislation and standards.** Safety, technical, labelling, environmental and licensing requirements; customs procedures and documentation; local content rules. These are the fastest-growing form of protection precisely because they are hard to challenge as protectionist when they are dressed as consumer protection. For a business the cost is **compliance and delay**: redesigning a product for one market can cost more than the tariff would have.

**Domestic subsidies.** Payments to domestic producers reduce their costs, so imports become relatively more expensive without any barrier at the border. Subsidies are paid by the taxpayer rather than the consumer, which makes them politically easier and no less distorting.

## Impact on businesses

**On an exporter facing barriers:** a smaller accessible market, lower price competitiveness, higher compliance costs, and possibly exclusion altogether under a quota. **Responses:** absorb the tariff and accept lower margins, raise the price and lose volume, redesign to meet local standards, produce inside the market through FDI, license or franchise to a local producer, or shift attention to other markets.

**On an importer of inputs:** higher costs, which either reduce contribution or raise prices, with the break-even consequences shown above. **Responses:** find a domestic supplier, absorb it, pass it on, or relocate production.

**On a domestic producer competing with imports:** short-run relief. **The longer-run danger** is that protection removes the pressure to become efficient, so the industry becomes progressively less competitive and more dependent on the protection continuing.

**Retaliation** is the factor that turns a gain into a loss. A tariff imposed to protect one industry invites a tariff on another country's exports, and the businesses harmed are usually not the ones that were protected.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Producing inside the barrier.** Vehicle manufacturers have repeatedly built assembly plants inside protected markets rather than export into them, because assembling locally avoids the tariff on finished vehicles. The investment decision was driven by trade policy, not by production cost, which is the clearest illustration that protectionism changes where businesses locate and not only what things cost.

**Small island economies.** Import duties are a major source of government revenue where income tax systems are limited, so tariffs there serve a fiscal purpose as much as a protective one. For a business importing almost everything, that duty is a permanent cost of operating, not a temporary policy.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define tariff or quota (2 marks); calculate the cost of a tariff (2–4); Explain (4) on one impact; Assess (12) or a 20-mark essay on the impact of protectionism on a business, or on whether a government should impose it.

**Structuring "what is the impact on this business?":**

1. **Identify the barrier and which side of it the business is on:** exporter, importer of inputs, or domestic producer.
2. **Quantify it.** Calculate the extra cost per unit and per year, and the effect on contribution or break-even.
3. **Set out the options** and the cost of each: absorb, pass on, redesign, relocate, or exit the market.
4. **Recommend**, using the figures.
5. **Note the longer-run factors:** retaliation, the permanence of the policy, and whether protection weakens the protected industry.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **A tariff protects one industry and raises costs for every industry that buys from it.**
- **A quota is worse than a tariff for an exporter**, because cost cutting cannot get more through.
- **Retaliation harms businesses that were never protected.**
- **Temporary protection rarely ends**, because the industry lobbies to keep it.
- **Non-tariff barriers can cost more than tariffs**, through redesign and delay.
- **It depends on elasticity.** Where demand is inelastic, the tariff can be passed on; where it is elastic, the business absorbs it.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing a tariff with a quota.** A tariff is a tax; a quota is a quantity limit.
- **Saying a tariff raises government revenue and stopping there.** The consumer and the input-buying business pay it.
- **Forgetting that many businesses import inputs**, so protection raises their costs.
- **Treating protection as automatically good for domestic firms**, which ignores retaliation and lost efficiency.
- **Omitting non-tariff barriers**, which are now the most common form.
- **Not quantifying** when the source gives you a rate and a volume.
- **Ignoring the FDI response.** Producing inside the barrier is often the answer the source is pointing at.

</Mistakes>
