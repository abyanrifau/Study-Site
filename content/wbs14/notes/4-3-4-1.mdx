## Impact on the local economy

<Chunk title="Local labour, wages, working conditions and job creation" open>

**Benefits.** Direct employment, often at wages above the local average, since multinationals typically pay a premium to attract and retain staff. Training and transferable skills. Formal contracts and better health and safety standards than local employers in some sectors. An indirect employment multiplier as local suppliers and services grow around the operation.

**Costs.** Jobs may be low-skilled and insecure, with the higher-value work kept in the home country. Conditions may be poor by international standards even where they exceed local norms. Local wage structures can be distorted, as the multinational recruits the best staff away from local employers and drives up wages in the roles it needs. And the jobs may be temporary: if the operation closes, an area that reorganised itself around it is left with nothing.

**The examinable nuance:** paying above the local average and below the home-country rate are both true at once. Which comparison the answer uses determines the conclusion, and a good answer says so explicitly rather than choosing one silently.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Local businesses">

**Benefits.** Demand for local suppliers, and pressure on them to raise quality and reliability, which makes them more competitive generally. Skills spill over as trained staff leave and start or join local firms. Infrastructure built for the multinational is used by everyone.

**Costs.** Local competitors may be unable to match the multinational's prices, marketing or purchasing power, and close, which is **crowding out**. Suppliers become dependent on one dominant customer with enormous bargaining power, which is Porter's supplier-power force in reverse. Local firms lose their best staff. And inputs may be imported rather than bought locally, in which case very little of the promised local benefit materialises.

**The question that decides the answer:** how much of the multinational's spending stays in the local economy? An operation importing all its inputs and repatriating its profit creates jobs and little else.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="The local community and environment">

**Benefits.** Higher local incomes and the multiplier effect. Infrastructure: roads, power, water, sometimes schools and clinics. Community investment programmes. Reduced migration away from the area where jobs are created.

**Costs.** Emissions, waste, water use and noise. Pressure on housing, transport and public services from an influx of workers. Displacement of existing land use, including agriculture and fishing. Loss of traditional livelihoods, and cultural change that the community did not choose. Where regulation is weak, environmental standards may be far below those the same company applies at home, and that double standard is the substance of **4.3.4.2**.

</Chunk>

## Impact on the national economy

<Chunk title="The seven named effects" open>

**Economic growth.** The investment itself, plus the output and the multiplier effect, raise GDP. **Against:** growth concentrated in one sector or region raises inequality, and the reported figures may overstate the benefit to residents where profits accrue to foreign owners.

**FDI flows.** Inward investment supplies capital a developing economy cannot raise domestically, and it is recorded in the balance of payments financial account. **Against:** it can be volatile and footloose, and success attracts further inflows that push up the exchange rate and make other exports less competitive.

**Balance of payments.** Complicated, and worth getting right. The **initial investment** is an inflow. **Exports** produced by the operation are a credit on the current account. **Against that:** imported inputs and capital equipment are debits, and **repatriated profit is a debit on the primary income account** every year thereafter. So the balance of payments effect is positive at first and can turn negative permanently once profits exceed the original investment.

**Technology and skills transfer.** New technology, management methods and quality standards enter the economy, and workers trained by the multinational carry the skills elsewhere. **Against:** the most valuable research and development usually stays at home, technology may be protected by patents and kept inside the company, and transfer happens only if local firms and workers are capable of absorbing it, which depends on the education system.

**Consumers.** Wider choice, lower prices through scale, better quality, and pressure on local firms to improve. **Against:** local products and producers may disappear, reducing choice in the long run, and a dominant multinational can raise prices once competition has gone.

**Business culture.** Professional management, formal contracts, corporate governance, reporting standards and anti-corruption practice can raise standards across the economy. **Against:** cultural change may be unwelcome, and multinationals have also been the source of corruption rather than a remedy for it.

**Tax revenue.** Corporation tax, employment taxes, and the taxes paid by employees and suppliers. **Against, and this is the sharpest criticism available:** tax holidays granted to attract the investment may mean little tax is paid for years, and **transfer pricing** lets a group shift profit to low-tax countries by adjusting the prices at which its own subsidiaries trade with each other. A multinational can therefore report large local sales and little local profit. Governments competing for investment bid each other's tax rates down, which is why the revenue benefit is often far smaller than expected.

</Chunk>

## Reaching a judgement

**The answer always depends on three things:**

1. **The host country's bargaining power.** A large economy with a skilled workforce and a market the multinational wants can negotiate conditions on local employment, local sourcing and technology transfer. A small economy competing with others for the same investment has almost none and ends up offering incentives instead of extracting conditions.
2. **How much stays in the country.** Local sourcing, local employment at all levels, reinvested profit and tax actually paid, against imported inputs, expatriate management and repatriated profit.
3. **Whether the government regulates effectively**, which is **4.3.4.3**.

**A useful structuring distinction:** benefits such as jobs and investment are **immediate and visible**, while costs such as environmental damage, crowding out and profit repatriation are **gradual and cumulative**. That asymmetry explains why governments compete to attract investment and are then disappointed by it, and it makes a strong concluding point.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

International resort operators are the clearest local case, and the balance sheet cuts both ways.

**Benefits.** Employment across a dispersed population, training in hospitality skills that are genuinely transferable internationally, foreign exchange earnings, tax revenue that funds public services, and infrastructure including transport links and power that the resort pays for.

**Costs.** A high import content, since most food, fuel and materials come from abroad, so much of the spending leaves immediately. Profit repatriation by foreign-owned operators. Senior positions often held by expatriate staff, which limits skills transfer at the top. Environmental pressure on reefs from construction, waste and water use. And dependence: an economy built on one sector owned largely from outside has very little bargaining power and no fallback if global travel demand collapses.

**The point that generalises:** for a small economy the question is not whether the multinational brings benefits, which it does, but what share of the value created stays in the country.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is the most likely 20-mark essay in the unit: evaluate the impact of MNCs on a host country, or assess whether a country should encourage a particular investment.

**Structuring the essay:**

1. **Define MNC** and identify the specific one and country in the source.
2. **Separate local from national impacts**, which follows the specification and guarantees structure.
3. **Take three or four impacts and give the benefit AND the cost of each**, chained to a consequence. Jobs to higher incomes to the multiplier to higher local demand; and against it, low-skilled jobs and the risk of closure.
4. **Use the data.** Number of jobs, investment value, tax paid, wage levels, export value.
5. **Bring in the strongest criticisms:** profit repatriation, transfer pricing and tax holidays, footloose investment.
6. **Judge**, using the three conditions: bargaining power, how much stays in the country, and whether the government can regulate.
7. **State what further information you would want**, such as the local sourcing proportion or the tax actually paid.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Above the local average and below the home rate are both true**, and the comparison chosen decides the verdict.
- **Profit repatriation turns a balance of payments gain into a permanent outflow.**
- **Transfer pricing and tax holidays** can make the tax benefit far smaller than promised.
- **Benefits are immediate and visible; costs are gradual and cumulative.**
- **The net effect depends on the host's bargaining power**, which small economies lack.
- **Technology transfer requires the local capacity to absorb it.**
- **Footloose investment can leave** when the incentives end.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing benefits and costs with no judgement**, which caps the marks.
- **Treating job creation as an unqualified benefit** without considering skill level or security.
- **Getting the balance of payments wrong.** The initial investment is an inflow; repatriated profit is an outflow every year afterwards.
- **Assuming technology transfer happens automatically.**
- **Forgetting transfer pricing and tax holidays** when discussing tax revenue.
- **Ignoring the import content** of the multinational's operation.
- **Writing generally about MNCs** when the question names one.
- **Confusing local and national impacts**, which loses the structure the specification provides.

</Mistakes>
