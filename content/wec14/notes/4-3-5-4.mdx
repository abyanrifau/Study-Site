## The five instruments

| Instrument | Tools | Acts | Main strength | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fiscal** | spending, taxation | AD, and AS through capital spending | targeted, powerful, can be aimed at particular groups | lags, borrowing cost, crowding out, political difficulty |
| **Monetary** | interest rates, QE, reserve requirements | AD through borrowing, spending and the exchange rate | fast to change, can be reversed | blunt, works through confidence, ineffective near zero rates |
| **Exchange rate** | intervention, interest rates, QE | net exports and import prices | fast, helps all exporters at once | inflationary, invites retaliation, nothing real improves |
| **Supply-side** | education, infrastructure, competition policy, deregulation, incentives | AS | permanent, raises capacity without inflation | slow, expensive, uncertain |
| **Direct controls** | price caps, capital controls, import restrictions, rationing | the specific behaviour | immediate and certain in effect | shortages, black markets, distortion, avoidance |

**The organising principle for every question in this topic: match the instrument to the objective, then state what it costs in terms of the other objectives.** That sentence is the structure of a good answer.

## Applying them to the four named objectives

<Chunk title="Reducing fiscal deficits and national debts" open>

**Fiscal:** raise taxes, cut spending. Direct and effective, and it reduces AD, so output and employment fall, which reduces revenue and raises benefit spending, partly offsetting the intended saving. The multiplier determines how much.

**Monetary:** lower interest rates reduce debt servicing costs and support growth, which raises revenue. Indirect and it depends on the inflation position.

**Supply-side:** raise growth so that revenue rises and the debt-to-GDP ratio falls without a surplus. The most attractive route and the slowest.

**Evaluation:** distinguish cyclical from structural, as in **4.3.5.3**; consolidate over the cycle rather than within a recession; protect capital spending; and remember that growth faster than the interest rate reduces the ratio on its own.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Controlling the rate of inflation" open>

**Monetary, the standard instrument:** raise interest rates, which raises the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving, so consumption and investment fall, AD falls and inflationary pressure eases. It also appreciates the currency, which lowers import prices directly.

**Fiscal:** higher taxes or lower spending reduce AD. Effective and politically much harder, and slower.

**Exchange rate:** an appreciation lowers import prices and reduces imported inflation. It also damages export competitiveness.

**Supply-side:** raising productivity lowers unit costs, so AS shifts right and the price level falls without any loss of output. The only instrument that reduces inflation without reducing growth.

**Direct controls:** price and wage controls suppress measured inflation and create shortages and black markets, because the underlying excess demand remains.

**The essential evaluative distinction:** identify whether the inflation is **demand-pull** or **cost-push**. Raising interest rates to combat cost-push inflation caused by an external oil price shock reduces output and employment without addressing the cause, since the central bank cannot influence world oil prices. That diagnosis should come before any recommendation.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Responding to external shocks" open>

**Fiscal:** a stimulus to support demand after a negative demand shock, or targeted support for the sector affected.

**Monetary:** cut rates and use QE to support demand and liquidity.

**Exchange rate:** allow a depreciation to absorb the shock, which is the strongest argument for a floating rate: the currency adjusts automatically rather than the domestic economy having to.

**Supply-side:** diversify the economy so that a shock to one sector matters less. Prevention rather than cure, and the only durable protection.

**Direct controls:** capital controls to stop capital flight, or export restrictions during a food crisis. Effective in an emergency and damaging to confidence and future investment.

**Evaluation:** distinguish a **demand** shock from a **supply** shock. A negative supply shock, such as an oil price rise, raises the price level and reduces output simultaneously, so any demand-side response must worsen one objective to improve the other. There is no policy that fixes both, and saying so is the correct answer rather than a failure to answer.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Reducing poverty and inequality" open>

**Fiscal:** progressive taxation and transfers, the most direct instrument, with the incentive and financing costs from **4.3.5.2**.

**Supply-side:** education, training and health spending raise the earning capacity of the poorest, which addresses the cause rather than the symptom, over a generation.

**Direct controls:** a minimum wage raises the incomes of the lowest paid directly. **Against:** it may reduce employment if set above the market clearing wage, and it does not help those who are not in work at all, who are usually the poorest.

**Monetary:** lower rates support employment, which helps the poorest most, since unemployment is concentrated among them. **Against:** low rates raise asset prices, which benefits existing wealth owners and can raise **wealth** inequality even while reducing income inequality. That is a genuinely sophisticated point and it applies directly to the post-2008 period.

**Evaluation:** transfers are fast and treat the symptom; education is slow and treats the cause. The two are complements rather than alternatives.

</Chunk>

## Demand-side policies and the 2008 financial crisis

The specification names this specifically, so the details matter.

**What happened:** a banking crisis triggered a collapse in credit, confidence, investment and consumption. AD fell sharply and the world economy entered the deepest recession since the 1930s.

**The demand-side response:**

- **Fiscal stimulus.** Coordinated tax cuts and spending increases across the major economies, including infrastructure programmes and temporary measures such as car scrappage schemes and VAT reductions. The justification was the multiplier and the presence of very large spare capacity, so crowding out was minimal.
- **Interest rate cuts** to near zero, faster and further than ever before.
- **Quantitative easing** once rates could go no lower, raising the money supply, lowering long-term rates and supporting asset prices.
- **Bank recapitalisation and guarantees** to prevent the financial system collapsing, which was a precondition for any other policy working.

**The assessment:**

- **For:** a far deeper depression was probably avoided, the financial system was stabilised, and unemployment rose by much less than in the 1930s.
- **Against:** fiscal deficits and national debts rose sharply, prompting the subsequent austerity; QE inflated asset prices and therefore **wealth inequality**; near-zero rates persisted for a decade, leaving little room to respond to the next shock; recovery in productivity and real wages was unusually weak; and the underlying banking problems took years to resolve.
- **The lasting lesson:** demand-side policy can stop a collapse in demand and cannot repair a damaged supply side, which is why the recovery in output was slow even after demand had been restored.

## Measures to control TNCs

**The problem:** TNCs are global while tax systems are national, so profit can be reported wherever the tax rate is lowest. **Transfer pricing** is the main mechanism: the prices at which subsidiaries trade with each other are set internally, so costs can be booked in high-tax countries and profits in low-tax ones. Related techniques include routing royalty payments for intellectual property through low-tax jurisdictions and loading debt onto high-tax subsidiaries so that interest deductions reduce taxable profit there.

**Measures to reduce tax avoidance:**

- **Regulation of transfer pricing**, requiring intra-group transactions to be priced at **arm's length**, meaning at the price independent parties would have agreed.
- **Country-by-country reporting**, so that profits, employees and tax paid must be disclosed for each country. Transparency alone changes behaviour, because the discrepancy between where activity occurs and where profit is reported becomes visible.
- **An international minimum effective corporate tax rate**, which removes the benefit of shifting profit to very low-tax jurisdictions and is the collective answer to tax competition.
- **Diverted profits or digital services taxes**, unilateral national measures aimed at profit clearly generated locally.
- **Limits on interest deductibility** and on royalty payments to related parties.
- **General anti-avoidance rules**, allowing tax authorities to disregard arrangements with no commercial purpose other than avoiding tax.

**Limits to government ability to control TNCs:**

- **The arm's length principle is very hard to apply** where no comparable market price exists, which is precisely the case for intellectual property, brands and specialised components.
- **Enforcement capacity.** Large TNCs employ more and better-paid tax specialists than the authorities auditing them, and the asymmetry is greatest in developing countries.
- **Tax competition.** Any country tightening alone risks losing the investment, so unilateral action is constrained. This is the collective action problem that makes international agreement necessary and difficult.
- **Mobility.** Where activity is genuinely mobile, the threat of relocation limits what can be imposed.
- **Political influence and lobbying.**
- **Complexity and speed.** New structures are devised faster than legislation can close them.
- **Sovereignty.** No country can legislate for another's tax system, and some jurisdictions have an interest in remaining low-tax.

**The judgement:** unilateral national action has limited effect, because the problem arises from the interaction of national systems. **Coordinated international action, particularly transparency and a minimum rate, is the only approach that addresses the cause**, and it is slow, contested and incomplete.

## Impact of policy changes at three levels

The specification asks for local, national and global effects.

**Local.** Spending decisions fall on particular places: a new hospital or transport link raises local employment and land values; closing a facility devastates a town. Regional effects are frequently the opposite of the national ones, which matters politically as well as economically.

**National.** The standard objectives: growth, employment, inflation, the current account, the fiscal position and the distribution of income.

**Global.** Large economies' policies transmit abroad. QE and low interest rates pushed capital into emerging markets in search of returns, raising their currencies and asset prices, and it flowed out again when rates rose. A fiscal stimulus in a large economy raises demand for other countries' exports. A devaluation exports unemployment to trading partners. **The general point:** for a large economy, policy is a global act, and for a small economy other countries' policy is an external shock it cannot influence.

## Problems facing policymakers

<Chunk title="The three named problems" open>

**Inaccurate information.**

- Data is published with a lag, and revised, sometimes substantially.
- Key concepts cannot be observed at all: the output gap, trend growth, the natural rate of unemployment, the revenue-maximising tax rate.
- Some activity is unrecorded, in the informal economy.
- Forecasts of the effect of a policy rest on estimated multipliers and elasticities that vary with circumstances.

**So policy is set using estimates of unobservable quantities derived from data that will later be revised.** That is the single best sentence available for an evaluation of any policy recommendation.

**Risks and uncertainties.**

- Behavioural responses are uncertain: how much will consumers spend from a tax cut?
- Expectations matter and are hard to predict. A policy believed to be credible works better than an identical policy that is not.
- The size of multipliers varies with the state of the economy.
- Time lags mean the policy takes effect in an economy different from the one it was designed for.
- Unintended consequences: QE supported demand and raised wealth inequality; a minimum wage raises incomes and may reduce employment.

**Inability to control external shocks.**

- Commodity prices, global recessions, financial crises, pandemics, wars and other countries' policy decisions.
- A small open economy is particularly exposed, since its policy instruments cannot offset a shock to its main export market.
- Shocks arrive during, and interact with, existing policy, so their effects cannot be isolated.

**The implication for policy design:** build in **flexibility and resilience** rather than attempting precision. Automatic stabilisers, foreign exchange reserves, a diversified economy, fiscal space in good times and a credible policy framework all reduce vulnerability without requiring policymakers to forecast correctly. That is the constructive conclusion, rather than simply listing the difficulties.

</Chunk>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is the single most likely 20-mark essay area in the paper: "Evaluate the policies a government could use to reduce its fiscal deficit / reduce inflation / respond to an external shock / reduce poverty", or "Assess the extent to which governments can control TNCs."

**The template that works for every policy essay in this unit:**

1. **Identify the problem precisely from the source**, with data, and **diagnose its cause**. Demand-pull or cost-push; cyclical or structural; demand shock or supply shock. This step is what separates the top band, and most candidates skip it.
2. **Policy one**, chained through at least three steps to the objective, **with a diagram**, and the diagram referred to in the text.
3. **Policy two**, chained the same way, drawn from a different instrument.
4. **Policy three** briefly, usually supply-side, for the long run.
5. **Evaluate each policy:** time lag, cost, side effects on other objectives, the information required, and the conditions under which it works.
6. **Compare** the policies explicitly and prioritise.
7. **Conclude** with a judgement, a condition, and if appropriate a sequence: this in the short run, that in the long run.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Diagnose before prescribing.** The right policy depends entirely on the cause.
- **Every policy has a conflict with another objective**, and naming it is the most reliable evaluation point.
- **Time lags mean the policy acts on a different economy** from the one it was designed for.
- **Policy rests on unobservable estimates and revised data.**
- **Only supply-side policy raises capacity**, and it is the slowest.
- **There is no demand-side answer to a supply shock** that improves both output and prices.
- **Unilateral action on TNCs is limited**, because the problem is the interaction of national systems.
- **Credibility and expectations** determine how well a policy works.
- **Resilience beats precision**, because shocks cannot be forecast.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Recommending a policy without diagnosing the cause of the problem.**
- **Listing policies without chaining the analysis** to the objective.
- **Omitting diagrams**, or drawing them and never referring to them.
- **Ignoring policy conflicts**, which is where most of the evaluation marks are.
- **Treating monetary policy as effective against cost-push inflation** from an external shock.
- **Claiming governments can simply legislate transfer pricing away.**
- **Forgetting the 2008 detail**, which the specification names explicitly.
- **Concluding that nothing can be done.** The correct conclusion is which policy, in what order, under what conditions.

</Mistakes>
