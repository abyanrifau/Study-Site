## The three types of expenditure

| | What it is | Examples | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Capital** | spending on assets lasting beyond the period | roads, rail, schools, hospitals, broadband | raises both AD now and **AS** later |
| **Current** | day-to-day running costs | public sector wages, medicines, school books, maintenance | raises AD; sustains the services that support AS |
| **Transfer** | payments with nothing received in return | pensions, unemployment benefit, child benefit | redistributes income; raises AD through consumption |

**The distinction that carries marks:** **capital expenditure shifts aggregate supply to the right** as well as aggregate demand, because it creates productive capacity. Current expenditure and transfers principally affect demand. So a given sum spent on infrastructure has a very different long-run effect from the same sum spent on transfers, and an essay that recognises that is doing genuine economics.

**A qualification on transfers:** they are not costless to the economy in the way the phrase "just a transfer" suggests, because the taxation that funds them affects incentives, and they raise consumption because recipients have a high marginal propensity to consume. They are also the fastest-acting instrument for reducing poverty.

**A qualification on current spending:** teachers' salaries are current expenditure and they produce human capital, so the boundary between current spending and investment in capacity is not as sharp as the classification implies. Making that point shows real understanding.

## Reasons for the changing size and pattern

<Chunk title="The three named reasons" open>

**Changing incomes.** As incomes rise, demand for health care, education, transport and environmental quality rises more than proportionately, because these are income elastic. Citizens of richer countries demand more and better public services, so public spending rises as a share of GDP as economies develop. This is sometimes called Wagner's law.

**Changing age distributions.** An ageing population raises the dependency ratio and with it spending on pensions, health care and social care, which are the three largest and fastest-growing items in most developed economies. **In the opposite direction**, a young and growing population raises spending on education and on job creation. So the same demographic argument produces different spending patterns at different stages of development, and the answer depends on which the country in the source has.

**Changing expectations.** Voters expect provision that would have been unthinkable a generation earlier: universal health care, higher education, environmental protection, digital infrastructure. Expectations ratchet upwards and are politically very difficult to reverse, which is a large part of why public spending is hard to cut.

</Chunk>

**Other reasons worth citing:** the cost of public services rises faster than average prices, because they are labour-intensive and productivity growth in them is slow, which is the Baumol effect; the economic cycle, since recession automatically raises benefit spending and reduces revenue; debt interest, which rises with accumulated borrowing; war and defence; and deliberate policy choices about the role of the state.

## Significance of the level of public expenditure

<Chunk title="Productivity and growth" open>

**The case that higher spending raises productivity and growth:**

- **Infrastructure** reduces costs for every firm in the economy: transport, energy, water and telecommunications.
- **Education and training** raise human capital and therefore productivity.
- **Health care** raises the size and productivity of the workforce.
- **Research and development** produces knowledge that private firms would under-provide because they cannot capture all the benefit.
- **Public goods** such as law, defence and property rights would not be provided at all by the market, because of the free rider problem, and no productive economy functions without them.
- **Correcting market failure** in merit goods and externalities raises welfare above the market outcome.

**The case that higher spending reduces productivity and growth:**

- **Higher taxation** to fund it reduces incentives to work, save, invest and take risk.
- **Public sector productivity growth is generally slower**, so a larger public sector lowers average productivity growth in the economy.
- **Government failure:** imperfect information, political rather than economic priorities, bureaucratic inefficiency and poor project selection mean the money may not achieve what was intended.
- **Crowding out**, below.
- **Dependency effects** where transfers reduce work incentives.

**The honest conclusion:** the level of spending matters far less than its **composition and its efficiency**. Capital spending on infrastructure and education raises long-run capacity; a similar sum spent on poorly targeted subsidies does not. Countries with high public spending and high productivity exist, and so do countries with high spending and low productivity, which is strong evidence that composition dominates.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Crowding out">

**Financial crowding out.** Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds, which raises interest rates, which reduces private investment and consumption. So public spending partly displaces private activity rather than adding to total demand.

**Resource crowding out.** The public sector employs workers, materials and land that the private sector would otherwise have used, bidding up their prices.

**The conditions under which crowding out matters, which is where the evaluation is:**

- **It requires the economy to be near full capacity.** In a deep recession with spare capacity and unemployed resources, government spending uses resources that would otherwise be idle, so crowding out is minimal. This is the central Keynesian point and the justification for the fiscal expansions of 2008 to 2009.
- **It depends on how the borrowing is financed.** Borrowing from abroad, or in a country with an independent central bank purchasing government debt, has much weaker interest rate effects.
- **It depends on the interest sensitivity of private investment.** If investment responds little to interest rates, crowding out is small.
- **Crowding in works in the opposite direction.** Public investment in infrastructure raises the return on private investment, and higher demand raises business confidence, so private investment can rise rather than fall. Naming crowding in is a strong evaluative move.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Levels of taxation">

Higher spending must eventually be funded by higher taxation, higher borrowing or, in the long run, a smaller state elsewhere. **The consequences of higher taxation:** reduced work and investment incentives, possible tax avoidance and capital flight, and lower international competitiveness if business taxes are high relative to competitors. **Against that**, the spending it funds may raise productivity by more than the taxation reduces incentives, and the empirical size of incentive effects at moderate tax rates is contested.

**The intergenerational point:** funding current spending through borrowing shifts the burden to future taxpayers. That is more defensible for **capital** spending, since the asset created will still be there for them to use, than for **current** spending or transfers, from which they receive nothing. That distinction between borrowing to invest and borrowing to consume is the most useful thing to say about the financing question, and it links directly to **4.3.5.3**.

</Chunk>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Definitions distinguishing the three types (2 marks); Explain (4) on one reason for the change in spending; Analyse (6) with a diagram; and a 20-mark essay on whether a government should increase public expenditure, or on the significance of the size of the state.

**Structuring "should public spending be increased?":**

1. **Establish the current position** from the data, as a share of GDP, and compare it with other countries if the source allows.
2. **Say what the money would be spent on**, since that determines the answer.
3. **The case for**, chained: capital spending on infrastructure raises AD now and shifts AS right later, so output rises and inflationary pressure is limited. **Use an AD/AS diagram showing both shifts.**
4. **The case against**, chained: the borrowing raises interest rates, which reduces private investment, so the net effect on demand is smaller than the spending suggests.
5. **The financing question:** taxation, borrowing, and the intergenerational argument.
6. **Evaluate:** the state of the economy, since crowding out depends on spare capacity; composition against level; government failure; and crowding in.
7. **Conclude** with a judgement and a condition: capital spending on productive assets during a period of spare capacity is far more defensible than current spending at full capacity.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Composition matters more than level**, which is the central point in this topic.
- **Capital spending shifts AS as well as AD.**
- **Crowding out requires the economy to be near capacity**, so it is weak in a recession.
- **Crowding in is the counter-argument**, where public investment raises the return on private investment.
- **Borrowing to invest is more defensible than borrowing to consume.**
- **Government failure** may mean the spending does not achieve its purpose.
- **Public service costs rise faster than average prices**, so maintaining the same service requires rising spending.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Classifying public sector wages as capital expenditure.** They are current expenditure.
- **Classifying a pension as current expenditure on goods and services.** It is a transfer payment.
- **Treating all public spending as identical in its effects.**
- **Asserting crowding out without stating the capacity condition.**
- **Forgetting crowding in.**
- **Omitting the AS effect of capital spending**, which is the strongest argument for it.
- **Discussing the level of spending with no reference to what it buys.**
- **Ignoring how the spending is financed.**

</Mistakes>
