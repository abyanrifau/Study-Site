## The role of the WTO

**Three functions:**

1. **Administering the agreed rules** of world trade, including the **most favoured nation** principle that a member must offer the same terms to all members.
2. **Providing a forum for negotiation** to reduce barriers further.
3. **Settling disputes**, with the authority to permit retaliation where a member is found to have broken the rules.

**Its achievements:** average tariffs on manufactures have fallen substantially; rules-based dispute settlement replaced unilateral retaliation for many years; and smaller economies gained a forum in which they have formal equality with large ones, which they would not have in bilateral negotiation.

**Its limitations:**

- **Multilateral negotiation has largely stalled**, so liberalisation now happens through regional and bilateral deals instead.
- **Enforcement depends on compliance.** The WTO can authorise retaliation; it cannot compel a large member.
- **Dispute settlement has been weakened** where appointments to its appellate body have been blocked.
- **Agriculture and services remained far less liberalised** than manufactures, which disadvantages developing countries whose comparative advantage is often agricultural.
- **Critics argue the rules favour developed economies**, particularly on intellectual property.
- **Non-tariff barriers grew as tariffs fell**, and they are harder to identify and challenge.

## The four types of bloc

| Type | Internal tariffs | Common external tariff | Free factor movement | Single currency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Free trade area** | removed | no | no | no |
| **Customs union** | removed | **yes** | no | no |
| **Common market** | removed | yes | **yes** | no |
| **Economic and monetary union** | removed | yes | yes | **yes** |

**Free trade area.** Each member keeps its own external tariffs, so **rules of origin** are needed to stop goods entering through the member with the lowest external tariff. That paperwork is a real cost.

**Customs union.** The common external tariff removes the rules-of-origin problem and removes each member's freedom to set its own trade policy, since trade negotiation moves to the bloc.

**Common market.** Free movement of labour and capital, plus harmonised standards so a product approved in one member is accepted in all. **The economic significance** is that factors of production move to where their return is highest, which raises allocative efficiency across the whole bloc.

**Economic and monetary union.** A single currency removes exchange rate risk and currency transaction costs between members entirely. **The cost** is that members give up an independent monetary policy and the ability to let the exchange rate adjust, so an economy in recession cannot cut its own interest rate or depreciate. Whether that is acceptable depends on how similar members' economic cycles are, which is the optimal currency area question.

## Costs and benefits of membership

<Chunk title="The six named effects" open>

**Trade creation.** Removing barriers means trade shifts from higher-cost domestic production to lower-cost production within the bloc. This is a **genuine efficiency gain**: resources are used where they are most productive.

**Trade diversion.** The common external tariff makes an efficient non-member supplier more expensive than a less efficient member, so trade shifts to the higher-cost producer. This is an **efficiency loss**, and the imported good costs more in real terms than it need.

**The central judgement:** a bloc raises welfare if trade creation exceeds trade diversion. That is more likely when members are already substantial trading partners, when the external tariff is low, and when members' economies are complementary rather than identical.

**Costs and prices.** Prices fall through the removal of tariffs and greater competition, raising consumer surplus and real incomes. **Against this**, trade diversion raises the price of some goods relative to buying from the cheapest world supplier.

**Economies of scale.** A much larger market allows firms to produce at a scale that lowers long-run average cost, which lowers prices and improves international competitiveness against firms outside the bloc.

**Transaction costs.** Fewer customs procedures, one set of standards, and in a monetary union no currency conversion or hedging cost. These savings are unglamorous and substantial, particularly for small firms for whom the fixed cost of compliance is proportionally largest.

**Movement of factors of production.** Labour moves to where wages and employment prospects are best, and capital to where returns are highest. This raises efficiency and **can** create problems: emigration from poorer members can amount to a brain drain, and immigration into richer ones puts pressure on services and on lower-skilled wages.

</Chunk>

**Other costs to bring into an evaluation:**

- **Loss of policy independence**, on trade policy in a customs union and on monetary policy in a monetary union.
- **Exposure of uncompetitive domestic industries** to member competition, with structural unemployment during the adjustment.
- **Regulatory compliance** with bloc-wide rules that may be stricter than national ones.
- **Budget contributions** in deeper unions.
- **Uneven gains.** Members with stronger industries capture more of the benefit, which can widen differences within the bloc.

## Conflicts between blocs and the WTO

The tension is fundamental and the specification asks for it directly.

**Blocs discriminate.** They offer members better terms than non-members, which is exactly what the **most favoured nation** principle prohibits. The WTO permits regional agreements as an exception, on the condition that they cover substantially all trade between members and do not raise barriers against non-members. **But:**

- **Trade diversion harms non-members**, particularly developing countries excluded from the major blocs.
- **The proliferation of overlapping agreements** produces a tangle of different rules and rules-of-origin requirements, which raises costs and reduces transparency, the opposite of what a rules-based system is for.
- **Bloc formation has substituted for multilateral negotiation.** Countries able to secure preferential access regionally have less incentive to pursue global liberalisation, which is part of why multilateral talks stalled.
- **Blocs concentrate bargaining power**, so negotiations become contests between large blocs rather than a multilateral process in which small economies have a voice.

**The counter-argument, which a balanced answer needs:** blocs liberalise faster and deeper than the WTO has managed, they can act as a stepping stone towards wider liberalisation, and deeper integration achieves harmonisation that multilateral negotiation cannot. Whether blocs are **building blocks or stumbling blocks** for global free trade is the standard way of framing this, and it is an excellent line to use in an essay.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Definitions of bloc types (2 marks); Explain (4) on trade creation or diversion; Assess (10–12) or a 20-mark essay on whether a country benefits from bloc membership, or on the conflict between blocs and the WTO.

**Structuring "should this country join, or does it benefit from, a bloc?":**

1. **Identify the type of bloc** and what that specifically involves.
2. **Trade creation**, chained: barriers fall, so trade shifts to lower-cost members, prices fall, consumer surplus and real income rise.
3. **Economies of scale and lower transaction costs**, chained to competitiveness.
4. **Trade diversion**, chained: the external tariff shifts trade to a higher-cost member, so some prices rise and efficiency falls.
5. **Loss of policy independence and structural unemployment** in exposed industries.
6. **Judge on whether creation exceeds diversion**, and give the conditions: existing trade patterns with members, the level of the external tariff, and the complementarity of the economies.
7. **State what further information you would want**, such as the share of the country's trade already with members.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Creation against diversion** is the core welfare test.
- **Creation is more likely to dominate** where members are already major trading partners and the external tariff is low.
- **Deeper integration means less policy independence**, which matters when a member's cycle diverges from the bloc's.
- **Non-members lose**, particularly excluded developing countries.
- **Building blocks or stumbling blocks** is the framing for the WTO conflict.
- **Gains within a bloc are uneven**, so the aggregate effect hides the distribution.
- **Transaction cost savings matter most to small firms.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing a free trade area with a customs union.** The common external tariff is the difference.
- **Confusing a common market with a monetary union.** Free factor movement does not require a single currency.
- **Omitting trade diversion**, which is the main economic criticism.
- **Treating the WTO as a body that sets tariffs.** Members set their own within the agreed rules.
- **Listing benefits without the corresponding costs.**
- **Ignoring non-members**, who bear the cost of diversion.
- **Failing to state the condition** under which creation exceeds diversion.
- **Forgetting the most favoured nation principle**, which is why blocs and the WTO are in tension at all.

</Mistakes>
