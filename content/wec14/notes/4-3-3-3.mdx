## The three measures

<Formula name="Unit labour cost" note="This is the single most useful measure, because it combines the wage rate with productivity. A high-wage country can be highly competitive if productivity is high enough.">
Unit labour cost = Total labour cost ÷ Total output

Equivalently = Wage per worker ÷ Output per worker
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example">

| | Country X | Country Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual wage per worker | $9,000 | $46,000 |
| Output per worker per year (units) | 1,500 | 9,200 |
| **Unit labour cost** | **$6.00** | **$5.00** |

`X: 9,000 ÷ 1,500 = $6.00 per unit`

`Y: 46,000 ÷ 9,200 = $5.00 per unit`

**Country Y pays wages more than five times higher and is the more competitive**, because productivity is over six times greater. That is the result to be able to produce on demand, and it is the reason high-wage economies retain manufacturing.

**Now the exchange rate.** Suppose Y's currency appreciates by **25%**. Its unit labour cost in the currency buyers pay rises to 5.00 × 1.25 = **$6.25**, so **X becomes the cheaper producer with no change in wages or productivity at all**. This shows why competitiveness must be measured in **relative** terms adjusted for the exchange rate, and why a currency movement can destroy a competitive position that took decades to build.

**Suppose instead Y's wages rise 10% while productivity rises 4%.** Unit labour cost becomes (46,000 × 1.10) ÷ (9,200 × 1.04) = 50,600 ÷ 9,568 = **$5.29**, a rise of 5.8%. **Competitiveness is lost whenever wage growth exceeds productivity growth**, which is the single most useful relationship in this topic.

</Worked>

**Relative productivity rates.** Output per worker or per hour compared with competitors. It is the fundamental driver, since it determines how much can be produced from given resources.

**Relative export prices.** Domestic export prices as an index against competitors'. **Its advantage** is that it reflects the final price buyers actually face, including the exchange rate. **Its limitation** is that it captures only price competitiveness and ignores quality, reliability and design, so a country with rising export prices may be gaining share on the strength of its products.

## Factors influencing competitiveness

- **Productivity**, determined by capital investment, technology, skills, management quality and infrastructure.
- **Quality of human capital:** education, training, health, and the match between skills taught and skills needed. It is the slowest to change and the most durable once achieved.
- **The exchange rate**, which changes price competitiveness immediately without changing anything real.
- **Wage costs**, and specifically wage growth relative to productivity growth.
- **Non-wage costs:** employer social contributions, pensions, holiday and sick pay entitlements, and redundancy costs. These can be a large share of total employment cost and are often overlooked.
- **Regulation.** Product, environmental, planning and employment regulation raises costs and compliance burdens. **The two-sided point:** some regulation raises costs and also raises quality, safety and the human capital that competitiveness depends on, so less regulation is not automatically better.
- **Quality of infrastructure:** transport, ports, energy reliability, broadband. Poor infrastructure raises costs for every firm and cannot be fixed by any individual firm.
- **Non-price factors:** quality, design, reliability, delivery times, after-sales service, branding and reputation. **This is the most important addition to any answer**, because it explains how high-cost economies compete successfully: they do not compete on price at all.
- Also relevant: the tax system, access to finance, the exchange rate's stability as well as its level, and the openness of the economy.

## Measures to increase competitiveness

<Chunk title="The five named policies" open>

**Policies to improve education and training.** Raise human capital, therefore productivity, therefore lower unit labour costs and better non-price competitiveness.

- **For:** it addresses the fundamental cause, raises real incomes rather than reducing them, and the effect is durable.
- **Against:** it takes a decade or more to affect the workforce, it is expensive, the outcome is uncertain, and trained workers may emigrate, which is the brain drain.

**Investment incentives.** Tax allowances, grants and enterprise zones to raise capital investment, which raises capital per worker and therefore productivity.

- **For:** it can work faster than education, and new capital embodies new technology.
- **Against:** the cost to the government; deadweight, where investment that would have happened anyway is subsidised; and firms may relocate once the incentive ends.

**Privatisation and deregulation.** Transferring state assets to the private sector and removing rules, on the argument that the profit motive and competition raise efficiency.

- **For:** stronger incentives to cut costs and innovate; competition disciplines prices; lower compliance costs.
- **Against:** a privatised natural monopoly may exploit market power rather than become efficient; deregulation can create market failure, as financial deregulation did before 2008; and environmental and employment protections have value that a cost comparison ignores.

**Measures to reduce the exchange rate.** Lower interest rates, sell the currency, or use quantitative easing, making exports cheaper abroad.

- **For:** fast, and it works for every exporting industry at once.
- **Against:** inflationary through higher import prices, which erodes the gain; it invites retaliation and competitive devaluation; it lowers real incomes; and **nothing real has improved**, so it is a substitute for reform rather than reform. It is the quickest and least durable of the five.

**Trade liberalisation.** Removing the country's own barriers.

- **For:** cheaper imported inputs lower costs; competition forces domestic firms to become efficient; and consumers gain.
- **Against:** structural unemployment where domestic industries cannot compete, concentrated in particular regions, and adjustment takes years.

</Chunk>

## Significance of competitiveness

**Advantages of being competitive:** export-led growth; a current account surplus or a smaller deficit; employment in export industries; inward FDI attracted by a productive location; higher real wages, because productivity rather than low pay is the source of the advantage; and greater resilience to external shocks.

**Problems of being uncompetitive:**

- **A persistent current account deficit**, requiring continuous financing.
- **Structural unemployment** in industries losing to imports.
- **Slower growth**, as the export component of AD weakens.
- **Downward pressure on the exchange rate**, which raises import prices and inflation.
- **Lower inward FDI**, and domestic firms relocating abroad.
- **Reduced tax revenue** and pressure on public services.
- **Pressure for protectionism**, which reduces welfare further and delays adjustment.
- **A downward spiral:** low investment leads to low productivity, which leads to low competitiveness, which leads to low profits and therefore low investment. Breaking that cycle requires intervention, which is the strongest argument for active supply-side policy.

**The overriding judgement:** competitiveness can be bought quickly through a lower exchange rate or lower wages, and both reduce living standards, and neither lasts. **Only productivity growth raises competitiveness and living standards together.** That sentence is the conclusion to almost every essay in this topic.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A unit labour cost calculation (4 marks); Explain (4) on one factor; and a 20-mark essay on how a country can improve its international competitiveness, which is among the most predictable essays in the paper.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Divide labour cost by output**, not the other way round.
2. **Adjust for the exchange rate** if the question gives one, so the comparison is in a common currency.
3. **Compare wage growth with productivity growth** if given both: competitiveness is lost whenever wages grow faster.
4. **Interpret:** state which country is more competitive and why, referring to productivity explicitly.

**Structuring the essay:**

1. **Define competitiveness** and measure the country's current position from the data, using unit labour costs.
2. **Diagnose the cause.** Low productivity, high wage growth, a strong currency, poor infrastructure or weak non-price factors. **The policy must match the cause**, and this step is what separates the top band.
3. **Policy one, education and training**, chained: higher skills raise productivity, which lowers unit labour costs, which raises export competitiveness and real wages together.
4. **Policy two, investment incentives**, chained through capital per worker to productivity.
5. **Policy three, a lower exchange rate**, chained to export prices, then immediately qualified: inflationary, retaliatory, and nothing real has changed.
6. **Evaluate:** short run against long run, cost to the government, opportunity cost, uncertainty of outcome, time lags, and the risk of the brain drain.
7. **Conclude** with a prioritised recommendation and the condition, and use the line that only productivity growth raises competitiveness and living standards together.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **High wages are compatible with high competitiveness** if productivity is high enough.
- **Competitiveness is lost whenever wage growth exceeds productivity growth.**
- **The exchange rate changes competitiveness without changing anything real.**
- **Non-price factors explain how high-cost economies compete**, and they are the most frequently omitted point.
- **Deregulation can create market failure**, so less regulation is not automatically better.
- **Supply-side policies work over a decade**, and a country in immediate difficulty cannot wait.
- **Competitiveness is relative**, so it also depends on what competitors do.
- **Only productivity growth raises competitiveness and living standards together.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Equating low wages with competitiveness.** Use unit labour costs.
- **Inverting the unit labour cost formula.**
- **Ignoring the exchange rate** when comparing costs between countries.
- **Omitting non-price competitiveness entirely.**
- **Recommending a lower exchange rate as a solution** without noting inflation and retaliation.
- **Treating deregulation as costless.**
- **Recommending education and training with no reference to the time lag** or the risk of emigration.
- **Failing to diagnose the cause** before recommending a policy.

</Mistakes>
