## Possible benefits

<Chunk title="The six named benefits" open>

**Increased economic growth.** Trade raises aggregate demand through exports, and FDI raises both AD, through investment, and AS, through productive capacity. Access to larger markets and cheaper inputs raises productivity.

<Figure caption="Globalisation raises both aggregate demand and aggregate supply, so output rises">
<AdAsDiagram />
</Figure>

**The point worth making with the diagram:** if globalisation shifted only AD, output and the price level would both rise. Because it also shifts **AS** to the right, through investment, technology and cheaper imported inputs, output can rise **without** inflationary pressure. That is why the decades of fastest global integration combined growth with low inflation, and it is a strong analytical use of the diagram.

**Increased tax revenue.** Higher output, employment and profit widen the tax base, financing health, education and infrastructure. **Against this** sits the transfer pricing cost below, so the net effect is genuinely uncertain.

**Economies of scale.** Firms serving world rather than national markets spread fixed costs over far greater volume, so long-run average cost falls. This lowers prices and raises competitiveness.

**Lower prices and higher consumer surplus.** Imports from lower-cost producers, plus competition and scale economies, reduce prices. Consumers gain surplus, and the gain is largest proportionally for **low-income households**, who spend a larger share of income on traded goods such as clothing and food. That distributional point is frequently missed and is worth making, because it partly offsets the inequality criticism.

**More choice.** A wider range of goods, services, brands and qualities than any single economy could produce.

**Higher living standards.** The combination of higher real incomes, lower prices and wider choice. Hundreds of millions of people have moved out of absolute poverty during the period of fastest integration, particularly in Asia, which is the strongest single piece of evidence for globalisation.

</Chunk>

## Possible costs

<Chunk title="The six named costs" open>

**Displaced workers.** Industries that cannot compete with imports contract. The resulting unemployment is **structural**: the skills and location of the displaced workers do not match the jobs created elsewhere, so it persists. It is concentrated in particular regions, which is why the political effect is so much larger than the aggregate economic effect.

**Exploitation of workers.** Very low pay, excessive hours, unsafe conditions and child labour in parts of global supply chains. **The counter-argument must be acknowledged:** the alternative available locally is often worse, and wages in export sectors are frequently above the local average. **The response to that counter-argument** is that the comparison with the local alternative is not the only relevant standard, and that some conditions, particularly safety and child labour, are not matters of local preference.

**Environmental impact of increased trade.** Emissions from shipping and air freight; production relocating to countries with weaker environmental regulation, so global emissions rise even as one country's fall; resource depletion; and loss of biodiversity. These are **negative externalities**: the cost falls on third parties and is not in the price, so the market over-produces.

**Loss of tax revenue from transfer pricing.** A TNC sets the prices at which its subsidiaries trade with each other, so it can report costs in high-tax countries and profits in low-tax ones. Governments competing for investment also reduce rates and grant holidays, which is the **race to the bottom**. The revenue lost is the same revenue that appears as a benefit above, which is why that benefit is uncertain.

**Increased income inequality within countries.** Demand rises for skilled and capital-owning workers and falls for lower-skilled workers in developed economies, so the wage gap widens. Capital is mobile and labour is not, which shifts bargaining power towards capital and raises the profit share of national income.

**The influence of TNCs on domestic economic policy.** Lobbying, and the implicit threat to relocate, constrain what a government can tax or regulate. The constraint is greatest where the investment is mobile and the economy small.

</Chunk>

## The distributional judgement

The strongest structure for an essay on globalisation is to separate the **efficiency** question from the **distribution** question.

**On efficiency there is little dispute:** specialisation, scale and competition raise total world output. **The disputes are about distribution**, and the evidence points in two directions at once:

- **Between countries, inequality has fallen.** Fast growth in Asia and elsewhere narrowed the gap with developed economies and moved hundreds of millions out of absolute poverty.
- **Within many countries, inequality has risen.** Lower-skilled workers in developed economies lost, while skilled workers and capital owners gained.

**So globalisation produced aggregate gains with concentrated losses**, and the political reaction against it is explained by that distribution rather than by the total. The policy conclusion follows directly: the answer is not to reverse the integration but to **compensate and retrain** the losers, through education and training, regional policy and the tax and benefit system. That is a genuine evaluative conclusion rather than a verdict.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Asia's fall in absolute poverty.** The period of fastest global integration coincided with the largest reduction in absolute poverty in history, concentrated in export-oriented Asian economies. Growth was not caused by trade alone, since investment in education, infrastructure and institutions mattered too, and it is very hard to tell that story without trade at its centre.

**Deindustrialised regions in developed economies.** Manufacturing regions that lost production to lower-cost countries experienced persistent unemployment, falling relative incomes and population decline, decades after the initial shock. The aggregate economy grew; those regions did not. It is the clearest evidence for the concentrated-losses argument.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is one of the most likely 20-mark essays in the paper: "Evaluate the effects of globalisation on a developing economy" or "Assess whether globalisation has benefited consumers."

**A skeleton that works for this essay:**

1. **Define globalisation** in one sentence, covering trade, investment and migration.
2. **Establish the context from the source.** Is this a developed, emerging or developing economy, and how open is it?
3. **Benefit one, developed and chained.** Growth: trade and FDI raise AD and AS, output rises, employment and tax revenue rise. **Use the AD/AS diagram** and refer to it in the text.
4. **Benefit two, developed and chained.** Lower prices raise real incomes and consumer surplus, with the largest proportional gain for low-income households.
5. **Cost one, developed and chained.** Structural unemployment among displaced workers, persistent because of the skills and location mismatch.
6. **Cost two, developed and chained.** Transfer pricing reduces tax revenue, which constrains public spending on the very retraining the displaced workers need. Linking two points like that is worth a mark on its own.
7. **Evaluation paragraph**, using the distribution structure: efficiency gains are clear, distribution is the issue, inequality between countries has fallen and within countries has risen.
8. **Conclusion** with a judgement and a condition: it depends on the country's stage of development, the flexibility of its labour market and whether government redistributes the gains.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Separate efficiency from distribution.** It is the cleanest structure available for this topic.
- **The gains are spread thinly; the losses are concentrated.**
- **Inequality between countries fell while inequality within many countries rose.**
- **Lower prices help low-income households most in proportional terms**, which partly offsets the inequality effect.
- **The tax benefit and the transfer pricing cost cancel to an uncertain degree.**
- **AS shifts as well as AD**, so growth need not be inflationary.
- **The policy answer is compensation and retraining, not reversal.**
- **It depends on the stage of development:** the effects on a developing, an emerging and a developed economy differ sharply.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing benefits and costs with no judgement**, which caps the marks at the lower bands.
- **Failing to chain the analysis.** Each point needs at least two steps of consequence.
- **Omitting the diagram.** An AD/AS diagram is expected in a growth essay and must be referred to in the text.
- **Claiming globalisation has raised inequality everywhere**, which ignores the fall between countries.
- **Treating exploitation as self-evident** without acknowledging the local-alternative counter-argument.
- **Asserting higher tax revenue and lost tax revenue** without noticing they conflict.
- **Ignoring the source data** in a Section B or C answer.
- **Concluding that globalisation should be reversed** without considering compensation and retraining.

</Mistakes>
