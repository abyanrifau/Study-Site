## Market-orientated strategies

<Chunk title="The six named strategies" open>

**Trade liberalisation.** Removing the country's own barriers, on the argument that competition raises efficiency, imported inputs and capital goods become cheaper, and specialisation according to comparative advantage raises output.

- **For:** cheaper inputs and consumer goods, competitive pressure on domestic firms, export growth, and access to technology.
- **Against:** structural unemployment where domestic industries cannot compete; the infant industry argument, that liberalising too early prevents any industry from establishing itself; loss of tariff revenue, which matters greatly where income tax capacity is limited; and exposure to volatile world prices. **The strongest version of the objection** is that today's developed economies industrialised behind protection and liberalised afterwards.

**Promotion of FDI.** Tax incentives, special economic zones, relaxed regulation and investment guarantees.

- **For:** it fills the savings gap and the foreign currency gap simultaneously; brings technology, management and access to export markets; creates employment and tax revenue.
- **Against:** profit repatriation is a permanent outflow; tax holidays reduce the revenue benefit; investment may be footloose; local firms may be crowded out; the country's bargaining power is weak; and the technology transfer depends on local capacity to absorb it.

**Removal of government subsidies.** Fuel, food and agricultural subsidies are expensive and distort prices.

- **For:** they consume a large share of the budget in many countries, money that could fund health, education and infrastructure; they distort consumption and encourage waste; and universal subsidies are **regressive in absolute terms**, since the wealthy consume more fuel than the poor.
- **Against:** removal raises the price of necessities immediately, which hits the poorest hardest in proportional terms, and it has repeatedly triggered unrest. **The standard resolution:** replace a universal subsidy with a targeted cash transfer, which protects the poorest at far lower cost, provided the administrative capacity exists to deliver it.

**Privatisation.** Transferring state enterprises to private ownership.

- **For:** the profit motive raises efficiency; it raises revenue for the government; it removes loss-making enterprises from the budget; and it can attract investment and expertise.
- **Against:** a privatised natural monopoly exploits market power unless it is well regulated, and regulatory capacity is often exactly what is lacking; assets are frequently sold below value; job losses follow; and services to unprofitable rural areas may be withdrawn.

**Floating exchange rate systems.** Letting the market set the rate.

- **For:** no reserves are needed; a current account deficit corrects automatically; monetary policy is freed; and an overvalued rate cannot persist.
- **Against:** volatility deters trade and investment; for an import-dependent economy a depreciation raises the price of essentials directly and the Marshall-Lerner condition may not hold; and the currency is exposed to speculation and capital flight.

**Microfinance.** Very small loans, savings accounts and insurance for people without access to banks.

- **For:** it breaks the credit constraint that stops small enterprise; it reaches women in particular, with strong evidence of effects on children's health and schooling; repayment rates are high; and mobile banking has made it far cheaper to deliver.
- **Against:** interest rates are high, reflecting administrative costs on tiny loans; it can create over-indebtedness; most loans fund consumption smoothing or petty trade rather than transformative investment; and **the evidence that it raises incomes at scale is weaker than early enthusiasm suggested**. It relieves hardship reliably and transforms economies rarely.

## Interventionist strategies

<Chunk title="The six named strategies" open>

**Development of human capital.** Education, training and health provision.

- **For:** it raises productivity; it loosens more other constraints than any other single measure; it enables technology transfer and movement up the value chain; and the returns to basic education in particular are very high.
- **Against:** it takes a generation; it is expensive; trained workers may emigrate, which is the brain drain; and it delivers little if there are no jobs for the newly skilled.

**Protectionism.** Tariffs and quotas to shelter infant industries.

- **For:** the infant industry argument, that an industry needs time to reach scale and accumulate experience; it protects employment during structural change; and it retains foreign exchange.
- **Against:** it removes the pressure to become efficient; protection rarely ends because the industry lobbies to keep it; consumers pay throughout; it invites retaliation; and it is constrained by trade agreements. **The defensible version** requires a firm timetable for removal and a clear reason to expect the industry to become competitive.

**Managed exchange rates.** Holding the rate within a range, often slightly undervalued.

- **For:** stability for traders and investors; an undervalued rate supports export-led growth, which is how several East Asian economies grew; and it prevents the destabilising volatility a float can produce.
- **Against:** it requires large reserves; it constrains monetary policy; an undervalued rate makes imports of essential capital goods dearer and reduces living standards; it invites accusations of currency manipulation; and a misaligned peg attracts speculative attack.

**Infrastructure development.** Transport, power, water, sanitation and telecommunications.

- **For:** it lowers costs for every firm; it is a public good that the private sector will not adequately provide; it connects farmers to markets and workers to jobs; and it raises both AD now and AS later.
- **Against:** the capital cost is very large and the payback long, which is precisely the savings gap problem; it is vulnerable to corruption and poor project selection; and maintenance is routinely underfunded, so assets deteriorate.

**Promoting joint ventures with TNCs.** Requiring or encouraging foreign investors to partner with local firms.

- **For:** it captures the benefits of FDI while forcing **technology and skills transfer** to a local partner, retains more of the profit domestically, and builds local managerial capacity. It is the standard answer to the criticism that FDI transfers little.
- **Against:** it deters some investors; the local partner may lack capability; and it can become a vehicle for political patronage.

**Buffer stock schemes.** Stabilising commodity prices by buying and storing when prices are low and releasing when they are high.

- **For:** it stabilises producers' incomes and government revenue, which allows planning and investment; and in principle it is self-financing, since stock is bought low and sold high.
- **Against:** storage is expensive and commodities perish; if the band is set above the long-run average, the agency buys continuously until its funds are exhausted, which is how most historical schemes failed; it requires a great deal of capital up front; it can be undermined by producers outside the scheme; and it distorts the price signal that would otherwise move resources out of an oversupplied crop. **The honest verdict is that the theory is attractive and the record is poor**, and stabilisation funds that save revenue in good years have worked better than schemes that intervene in the market itself.

</Chunk>

## Other strategies

<Chunk title="Industrialisation and the Lewis model" open>

**The Lewis structural dual-sector model.** The economy has a large, low-productivity agricultural sector with **surplus labour**, whose marginal product is close to zero, and a small, higher-productivity industrial sector. Moving labour from the first to the second raises total output **without reducing agricultural output**, because the workers who leave were adding almost nothing. Industrial profits are reinvested, industry expands, more labour transfers, and the process continues until the surplus is exhausted and agricultural wages begin to rise.

**Why it is attractive:** it explains how growth can begin in a poor economy without any external resources, and it accounts reasonably well for the East Asian experience.

**Its limitations:** it assumes industrial profits are reinvested domestically rather than repatriated or exported as capital flight; it assumes industry is labour-intensive, whereas modern industry often is not, so it creates far fewer jobs than the model requires; agricultural labour is not always surplus, particularly at harvest; the migration produces urban slums where job creation is slower than migration; and it ignores the possibility of raising agricultural productivity instead, which may be a better route for some economies.

**Industrialisation more broadly:** it offers higher value added, economies of scale, technology and export earnings, and it requires capital, skills, infrastructure and energy, all of which are the constraints from **4.3.6.2**.

</Chunk>

**Development of tourism.**

- **For:** it earns foreign exchange directly, addressing the foreign currency gap; it is labour-intensive, so it creates employment quickly and for those with limited formal qualifications; it uses natural and cultural assets a country already has; and it generates demand for local agriculture, construction and transport.
- **Against:** it is highly volatile, depending on incomes abroad, exchange rates, security and global events; much of the revenue leaks out through imported food and foreign-owned hotels repatriating profit; work is seasonal and often low paid; it can damage the environment on which it depends; and it can displace agriculture and local communities. **The most useful evaluation point is the leakage question:** the development benefit depends on how much of each tourist dollar stays in the country.

**Development of primary industries.**

- **For:** it uses existing comparative advantage; it can generate substantial revenue quickly; and extraction and processing can fund the education and infrastructure needed to diversify later.
- **Against:** volatile prices and the Prebisch-Singer tendency; capital-intensive extraction creates few jobs; **Dutch disease**, where commodity earnings appreciate the currency and make all other exports uncompetitive; and the resource-curse pattern, where resource wealth is associated with corruption and weak institutions rather than development. **The decisive question is what is done with the revenue:** saved in a sovereign fund and invested in human capital, or consumed.

**Debt relief.**

- **For:** it frees foreign exchange and government revenue immediately for health, education and investment; it removes a deterrent to investment; and where the debt cannot realistically be repaid, relief recognises reality rather than granting a favour.
- **Against:** **moral hazard**, in that relief may encourage further imprudent borrowing by debtors and lending by creditors; it may reward the governments that borrowed badly; the freed funds may be misused where governance is weak; and it may reduce future access to credit. **Conditionality on transparent use of the freed revenue** is the standard resolution.

**Aid.**

- **Types:** bilateral from one government to another; multilateral through institutions; tied, requiring purchases from the donor; emergency or humanitarian; and project or programme aid.
- **For:** it fills the savings gap and the foreign currency gap; it funds health, education and infrastructure with high social returns that private finance will not provide; it is essential in emergencies; and its results in disease control and primary education have been substantial.
- **Against:** dependency; corruption and misallocation; **tied aid** is worth substantially less than its face value; volatility and unpredictability make planning hard; food aid can destroy local farmers' markets; it can prop up poor governments; and the administrative burden of many uncoordinated donors is itself a cost. **The evidence supports the conclusion that aid works where governance is reasonable and achieves little where it is not**, which is why aid effectiveness rather than aid volume is the live question.

## The role of international institutions

**The World Bank.** Long-term lending and grants for development projects and programmes: infrastructure, health, education and institutional reform, plus research and technical advice.

- **For:** it lends at below-market rates for very long terms, which no commercial lender would do; it provides expertise as well as finance; and it can fund projects with high social returns and low financial ones.
- **Against:** conditionality has been criticised as imposing a standard market-orientated model regardless of circumstances; large infrastructure projects have had environmental and displacement costs; voting power reflects contributions, so borrowing countries have limited influence; and it adds to debt.

**The IMF.** Short-term lending to countries in balance of payments crisis, surveillance of member economies, and technical assistance.

- **For:** it is the lender of last resort for a country that cannot finance its imports or debt service, and without it a crisis would force far more brutal adjustment.
- **Against:** its **conditionality** has been the most criticised feature: fiscal austerity, devaluation, liberalisation and privatisation required at the point of crisis, which deepened recessions and fell hardest on the poorest. The criticism that the conditions treated the symptom while worsening the immediate hardship is widely accepted, including in later IMF assessments of its own programmes.

**NGOs.** Deliver projects directly: health, education, water, agriculture, emergency relief, and advocacy.

- **For:** they reach communities that governments do not; they are close to local needs; they can bypass a corrupt or incapable state; and they are often more cost-effective on small-scale projects.
- **Against:** they are small in scale relative to the need; they are not democratically accountable; many uncoordinated NGOs duplicate effort and impose administrative burdens; they can substitute for rather than build state capacity; and they depend on donor priorities, which may not match local ones.

## Choosing between the approaches

**The market-orientated case:** governments in developing countries frequently lack the information and capacity to direct an economy, and intervention creates rent-seeking and corruption. Markets, competition and the profit motive allocate resources better than officials do.

**The interventionist case:** development involves exactly the areas where markets fail: public goods, infrastructure, education, health, externalities and the coordination of investment. Every economy that has industrialised rapidly did so with substantial state involvement, and pure market approaches have often produced volatility without structural change.

**The synthesis, which is the right conclusion for an essay:** the successful cases combined both. They were market-orientated in **prices, competition and openness to trade and investment**, and interventionist in **education, infrastructure and the coordination of industrial development**. So the question is not market against state but **which functions belong to each**, and the answer depends on the specific constraint and on the state's capacity to act.

**The condition that determines everything:** governance. Interventionist strategies require a competent, reasonably honest state; where that does not exist, market-orientated strategies are safer because they demand less of government. That is the most important conditional judgement available in this topic.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is the most likely 20-mark essay in the whole paper: "Evaluate the strategies a developing country could use to promote growth and development", or an essay on one named strategy such as aid, tourism, FDI or debt relief.

**Structuring the general essay:**

1. **Identify the country's binding constraints from the source**, with data. Do not skip this: the right strategy depends entirely on the constraint.
2. **Strategy one, market-orientated**, chained: promoting FDI fills the savings and foreign currency gaps, which raises investment, which raises the capital stock and productivity, which raises output and employment.
3. **Strategy two, interventionist**, chained: investment in education raises human capital, which raises productivity and enables movement up the value chain, so export earnings rise and dependence on primary products falls.
4. **Strategy three, briefly**, chosen to suit the source: tourism, debt relief, infrastructure or microfinance.
5. **Evaluate each:** time to take effect, cost and how it would be financed, the risk of failure, the distributional effect, and the governance capacity required.
6. **Compare and prioritise**, and if appropriate sequence: this first, that afterwards.
7. **Conclude** with a judgement and the governance condition.

**Structuring a single-strategy essay, for example on aid:**

1. Define it and identify the type in the source.
2. Two developed benefits, chained.
3. Two developed costs, chained.
4. The conditions under which it works: governance, targeting, predictability, untied.
5. Compare with an alternative use of the same resources, such as trade access or debt relief.
6. Judge, with the condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The right strategy depends on the binding constraint**, so diagnose first.
- **Governance determines whether interventionist strategies are viable at all.**
- **Successful cases combined market and state**, so the question is which functions belong to each.
- **Tied aid is worth less than its face value.**
- **Aid works where governance is reasonable and achieves little where it is not.**
- **The development benefit of tourism depends on leakage.**
- **For a resource exporter, what matters is what is done with the revenue.**
- **Buffer stock schemes are attractive in theory and have a poor record.**
- **Microfinance relieves hardship reliably and transforms economies rarely.**
- **Replace universal subsidies with targeted transfers**, provided the capacity exists to deliver them.
- **Short run against long run:** transfers and debt relief act immediately; education and infrastructure act over decades.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing strategies without chaining them** to growth or development.
- **Failing to diagnose the constraint** before recommending a strategy.
- **Treating aid as unambiguously good or unambiguously useless.**
- **Presenting the Lewis model without its limitations**, particularly that modern industry is capital-intensive.
- **Forgetting leakage** when recommending tourism.
- **Omitting Dutch disease** when recommending primary industry development.
- **Recommending subsidy removal with no protection for the poorest.**
- **Confusing the World Bank with the IMF.** The Bank lends long-term for projects; the Fund lends short-term for balance of payments crises.
- **Concluding "a mixture of both"** with no reasoning about which functions belong to the market and which to the state.
- **Ignoring governance**, which is the condition most of the strategies depend on.

</Mistakes>
