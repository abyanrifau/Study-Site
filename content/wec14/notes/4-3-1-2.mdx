## The five causes

<Chunk title="Trade liberalisation" open>

Average tariffs have fallen substantially over the last fifty years, through multilateral negotiation and a growing number of regional and bilateral agreements, overseen by the WTO and its predecessor. Lower barriers mean more trade, greater specialisation and larger markets for producers.

**The qualification:** liberalisation has been uneven. Agriculture and services remained more protected than manufacturing for long periods, and non-tariff barriers grew as tariffs fell. So liberalisation in aggregate coexists with substantial remaining protection in particular sectors.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Increased number and size of trading blocs">

Regional agreements multiplied, and existing blocs deepened and enlarged. Within a bloc, barriers fall to zero and factors of production may move freely.

**The two-sided effect, which is the analytical point:** blocs create trade between members and **divert** it away from more efficient non-members behind a common external tariff. So blocs contribute to globalisation between members and can work against it globally. Naming that tension is worth a mark.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Political change">

The specification names two events: **the breakdown of the Soviet system** and **the opening up of China**.

Together they brought hundreds of millions of workers and consumers into the world market economy within a generation. The consequences were large and specific: a vast increase in the global labour supply available to internationally mobile capital; the emergence of the world's largest manufacturing exporter; enormous new consumer markets as incomes rose; and the spread of market institutions such as private property and company law.

**Other political changes** reinforced it: the abandonment of import substitution in favour of export-led strategies in many developing countries, and the relaxation of capital controls, which allowed investment to flow across borders.

**And political change can reverse**, as recent tariff increases, investment screening and deliberate supply chain shortening show. Globalisation is a trend driven by policy, not a one-way process.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Reduced cost of transport and communications">

**Transport.** Containerisation standardised handling and cut both cost and unpredictability; larger vessels lowered cost per unit shipped; air freight made high-value and perishable goods tradable.

**Communications.** Fibre optics, mobile networks and the internet reduced the cost of coordinating activity across continents to close to zero.

**Why this matters more than the tariff reductions.** Falling communication costs made **global supply chains** possible, not just global selling. Without cheap coordination, a firm could export but could not manage production it did not stand beside. It also made services tradable, so software, design, accounting and customer support could be delivered from anywhere. That is a genuinely strong analytical point and few candidates make it.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Increased significance of TNCs">

TNCs both cause and result from globalisation. They organise production across borders, so a large share of world trade is intra-firm; they transfer technology and management practice; they lobby for further liberalisation; and their investment decisions shape which countries industrialise.

</Chunk>

## FDI by TNCs

**Reasons for FDI:**

- **Market access.** Selling to a large or fast-growing market, particularly where exporting is expensive.
- **Avoiding trade barriers.** Producing inside a tariff wall or a trading bloc.
- **Lower production costs:** wages, land, energy, taxation, and lighter regulation.
- **Access to resources**, raw materials or skills.
- **Transport costs**, for bulky or perishable goods.
- **Acquiring brands, patents or local knowledge**, usually through acquisition.
- **Government incentives:** grants, tax holidays, special economic zones.
- **Risk spreading** across countries and currencies.
- **Exchange rate advantage**, since a weak host currency makes assets and costs there cheap.

**The impact of FDI on recipient countries:**

| Benefit | Cost or qualification |
| --- | --- |
| **Capital inflow**, filling a savings gap | It is **footloose** and may leave when incentives end |
| **Employment** and often above-average wages | Jobs may be low-skilled and insecure |
| **Technology and skills transfer** | The best research usually stays at home, and absorption depends on local education |
| **Higher growth** through investment and the multiplier | Growth may be concentrated in one sector or region, raising inequality |
| **Tax revenue** | Reduced by tax holidays and **transfer pricing** |
| **Improved balance of payments** initially | **Profit repatriation** is a permanent primary income outflow, and inputs are often imported |
| **Competition** raises local firms' standards | Local competitors may be **crowded out** |
| **Infrastructure** built for the investor | Environmental damage, and displacement of existing land use |

**The key evaluative structure:** FDI's benefits are **immediate and visible**, while several of its costs, particularly profit repatriation and environmental damage, are **gradual and cumulative**. And the net effect depends on the recipient's **bargaining power** and on how much of the value created stays in the country: local sourcing, local employment at all levels, reinvested profit and tax actually paid.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one cause; Analyse (6) on the effect of FDI; and it is a frequent 20-mark essay: "Evaluate the impact of FDI on a developing economy" or "Assess the causes of increased globalisation."

**Structuring the FDI essay:**

1. **Define FDI** and identify the country and the type of investment in the source.
2. **Two developed benefits**, each chained: FDI raises investment, which raises aggregate demand and productive capacity, which raises growth and employment, which raises tax revenue.
3. **Two developed costs**, chained the same way: profit repatriation is a primary income debit, which worsens the current account permanently once cumulative outflows exceed the original inflow.
4. **Use an AD/AS diagram** to show the growth effect, since FDI shifts both AD, through investment, and AS, through capacity.
5. **Use the data.** Investment value, jobs created, share of GDP, tax paid.
6. **Judge**, with the conditions: bargaining power, how much value stays, and whether the government can regulate transfer pricing.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Profit repatriation turns an initial balance of payments gain into a permanent outflow.**
- **Technology transfer requires the local capacity to absorb it.**
- **FDI is footloose**, so incentives buy investment that may not stay.
- **Transfer pricing and tax holidays** can make the tax benefit far smaller than promised.
- **Benefits are immediate; several costs are cumulative.**
- **Blocs create trade between members and divert it globally**, so they are not unambiguously a cause of globalisation.
- **Political change can reverse**, so globalisation is not a one-way process.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing causes with no explanation of the mechanism.**
- **Omitting the political change named in the specification**, which is the breakdown of the Soviet system and the opening up of China.
- **Treating FDI as unambiguously good for the recipient.**
- **Forgetting that profit repatriation is an annual outflow**, not a one-off.
- **Assuming technology transfer is automatic.**
- **Confusing trade liberalisation with the formation of blocs.** They are separate causes and partly in tension.
- **Ignoring the role of falling communication costs**, which is the strongest single cause and the least frequently cited.

</Mistakes>
