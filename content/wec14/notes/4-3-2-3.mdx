## Calculating the terms of trade

<Formula name="Terms of trade" note="Export prices divided by import prices. A rise in the index is an IMPROVEMENT, a fall is a DETERIORATION. The index is usually set at 100 in a base year.">
Terms of trade = (Index of export prices ÷ Index of import prices) × 100
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example">

In the base year both indices are 100, so the terms of trade are 100.

**Year 2:** export price index **114**, import price index **105**.

`Terms of trade = (114 ÷ 105) × 100 = 108.6`

An **improvement of 8.6%**. Export prices rose by 14% while import prices rose by 5%, so each unit exported now buys about 8.6% more imports than in the base year.

**Year 3:** export price index **106**, import price index **128**.

`Terms of trade = (106 ÷ 128) × 100 = 82.8`

A **deterioration**. Compared with year 2:

`Percentage change = ((82.8 − 108.6) ÷ 108.6) × 100 = −23.8%`

**What this means in real terms.** If the country exports coffee and imports oil, a 23.8% deterioration means it must export roughly a quarter more coffee to buy the same quantity of oil. Real national income falls even though nothing has changed in the volume produced, and that is the sentence that turns the calculation into economics.

</Worked>

## Factors influencing the terms of trade

The specification names five.

- **Relative inflation rates.** If domestic inflation exceeds that of trading partners, export prices rise faster than import prices, so the terms of trade **improve**. Note the trap: higher inflation improves the terms of trade and damages competitiveness. The two point in opposite directions.
- **Relative productivity rates.** Faster productivity growth lowers unit costs and therefore export prices, so the terms of trade **deteriorate** while competitiveness improves. Again the two move in opposite directions.
- **Relative labour costs.** Rising wages relative to competitors raise export prices, improving the terms of trade and weakening competitiveness.
- **The exchange rate.** An appreciation raises export prices in foreign currency and lowers import prices in domestic currency, so the terms of trade **improve** immediately. A depreciation does the reverse.
- **The prices of imports and exports** directly, most importantly commodity prices. A commodity exporter's terms of trade are largely determined by world prices it cannot control.

**The pattern worth memorising:** almost everything that **improves** the terms of trade **worsens** competitiveness, and vice versa. Making that explicit is one of the most reliable evaluation points in the whole unit.

## Impact of a change

<Chunk title="Export revenues" open>

**This depends entirely on price elasticity of demand for exports**, and the question is really a revenue question.

An improvement in the terms of trade means export prices have risen relative to import prices.

- **If demand for exports is price elastic (PED > 1)**, the proportional fall in quantity exceeds the proportional rise in price, so **export revenue falls**.
- **If demand is price inelastic (PED < 1)**, quantity falls proportionally less than the price rises, so **export revenue rises**.

**Which case applies depends on what the country exports.** Manufactured goods facing many close substitutes tend to be elastic. Primary commodities with few substitutes tend to be inelastic in the short run, though a single country's exports of a commodity can be highly elastic if buyers can switch supplier easily. Say which case you think applies and why.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Living standards">

An improvement means a given volume of exports buys more imports, so **real national income rises**: the same work buys more goods. Imported inputs are cheaper, which lowers costs and inflationary pressure.

**Against that**, if the improvement came from an appreciation or from wage growth, export industries lose competitiveness, output and employment fall in those industries, and the workers affected are worse off. So living standards rise for consumers and fall for workers in the export sector, which is a distributional effect worth naming.

A **deterioration** reduces real national income directly, and it is particularly damaging for a developing economy that must export more merely to buy the same quantity of essential imports such as fuel, food and machinery.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Balance of trade">

**The answer is again about elasticity.** An improvement raises the price of exports and lowers the price of imports.

- **If demand for both is inelastic**, values move favourably and the balance of trade **improves**.
- **If demand for both is elastic**, export volumes fall sharply and import volumes rise sharply, so the balance of trade **worsens**.

**The Marshall-Lerner condition** from **4.3.3.2** is the formal statement: the sum of the two elasticities must exceed 1 for a price change to move the trade balance in the expected direction. Referring to it here shows synthesis between topics and is worth a mark.

</Chunk>

## The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis

The proposition that the terms of trade of primary product exporters **decline over the long run** relative to manufactured goods.

**The reasoning:** demand for primary products is income inelastic, so as world incomes rise, demand for food and raw materials grows more slowly than demand for manufactures and services. Meanwhile productivity gains in primary production are passed to buyers as lower prices, because producers are numerous and competitive, whereas gains in manufacturing are captured as higher wages and profits by firms with market power. Synthetic substitutes and improved material efficiency reduce demand further.

**The consequence** is that a developing country dependent on primary exports faces a structural, long-run tendency for its purchasing power to fall, on top of the short-run volatility of commodity prices. **That is the central economic argument for diversification**, and it appears again in **4.3.6.2**.

**The qualification:** the evidence is contested, the decline is not uniform across commodities, and commodity booms driven by emerging economy demand have interrupted it for long periods. Say so rather than presenting it as settled fact.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

An unusually clean illustration, because the export and import baskets are so distinct.

**Exports** are tourism services and fish; **imports** are fuel, food, construction materials and consumer goods. A rise in world oil prices raises the import price index directly and sharply, while tourism prices adjust slowly because they are set in advance in brochures and contracts. The terms of trade therefore **deteriorate** whenever oil rises, and real national income falls without any change in the volume of tourism or fishing.

**The policy implication** follows: the exposure is not to a trade deficit as such but to an import price shock the country cannot influence, which is an argument for energy diversification and for foreign exchange reserves rather than for trade restriction.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A calculation with interpretation (4–6 marks in Section B) is very likely; an essay on the effects of a change in the terms of trade, or on primary product dependency, appears in Section C.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Export prices on top.** Write the formula down before substituting.
2. **State whether it is an improvement or a deterioration.**
3. **Give the percentage change** if two periods are given.
4. **Interpret in real terms:** how much more or less can a unit of exports buy?

**Structuring the analysis of an improvement:**

1. State what caused it: appreciation, relative inflation, commodity prices.
2. **Real income effect:** exports buy more imports, so real income rises and imported input costs fall.
3. **Volume effect:** exports are dearer abroad, so volumes fall and export industries contract.
4. **Net effect on revenue and on the trade balance:** state the elasticity assumption explicitly and say why it applies here.
5. **Distributional effect:** consumers gain, export sector workers lose.
6. **Judge**, with the condition on elasticity and on the cause of the change.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **An improvement in the terms of trade is not automatically good**, because it usually means lost competitiveness.
- **Almost everything that improves the terms of trade worsens competitiveness.**
- **The revenue effect depends entirely on PED**, so state your assumption and justify it.
- **The cause matters:** a commodity price rise is different from an appreciation, even if the index moves identically.
- **Short run against long run:** elasticities are lower in the short run, so effects grow over time.
- **Prebisch-Singer gives a long-run structural argument for diversification**, and the evidence is contested.
- **Volatility may matter more than the level** for a commodity exporter, because it makes planning and budgeting impossible.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Inverting the formula.** Export prices divided by import prices.
- **Describing an improvement as unambiguously beneficial.**
- **Discussing export revenue without mentioning elasticity.**
- **Confusing the terms of trade with the balance of trade.** One is a price ratio, the other a value difference.
- **Assuming a rise in the index means export volumes have risen.** It is a price index.
- **Presenting Prebisch-Singer as established fact.**
- **Forgetting the distributional effect** between consumers and export sector workers.
- **Omitting the interpretation** after a correct calculation.

</Mistakes>
