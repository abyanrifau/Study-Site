## Growth against development

**Growth** is more output. **Development** is a broader improvement in welfare: health, education, security, freedom and the environment as well as income.

**The two usually move together and need not.** Growth concentrated in a capital-intensive extractive sector owned from abroad can raise GDP substantially with almost no improvement in health, education or employment. Conversely, development can improve without much growth if existing income is redirected towards health and education. **Growth is a means; development is the end**, and that distinction should open any answer in this topic.

## The three components of HDI

<Chunk title="What each is and how it is measured" open>

**Education**, measured by two indicators combined:

- **Mean years of schooling** of adults aged 25 and over, which captures the education the current workforce actually received.
- **Expected years of schooling** for a child entering school now, which captures current provision and therefore the future.

Using both is deliberate: a country that has recently expanded schooling will have low mean years and high expected years, which tells you the direction of travel.

**Health**, measured by **life expectancy at birth**. It is a good summary indicator because it reflects nutrition, sanitation, clean water, health care access and safety all at once, and it is comparatively reliable to measure.

**Income**, measured by **GNI per capita at purchasing power parity**, in constant dollars.

- **GNI rather than GDP**, because GNI counts income accruing to residents wherever it is earned, which matters where a large share of domestic output is produced by foreign-owned firms whose profits leave the country.
- **At PPP**, so that differences in the cost of living are allowed for.
- **The income component is logged** before being scaled, on the reasoning that an extra $1,000 matters far more at low income levels than at high ones, which is diminishing marginal utility.

**Combining them:** each dimension is scaled to a value between 0 and 1, and the three are combined as a **geometric mean**. That matters: with a geometric mean, a very poor score on one dimension drags the whole index down and cannot be offset by excellence in another. A country cannot buy a high HDI with income alone.

**Interpretation:** closer to 1 is more developed. The categories used are low, medium, high and very high human development.

</Chunk>

## Advantages and limitations of HDI

**Advantages:**

- **It is broader than GDP**, capturing health and education, which are ends in themselves and also inputs to future growth.
- **It is comparable** between countries and over time, and published annually for almost every country.
- **It is simple enough to be used politically**, which has genuinely shifted policy attention towards health and education.
- **Its components are reasonably reliably measured**, more so than most alternatives.
- **The geometric mean prevents** a high income from concealing very poor health or education.

**Limitations:**

- **It ignores inequality entirely.** An average can conceal a wealthy minority and a deprived majority. The inequality-adjusted HDI exists partly for this reason.
- **It ignores the environment.** Growth achieved by depleting resources or polluting raises HDI, and the cost appears nowhere.
- **It ignores political freedom, civil rights, corruption and security**, which most people regard as central to development.
- **Three dimensions cannot capture everything**, and the choice of these three, and the equal weight given to them, is a value judgement.
- **It says nothing about the quality of education or health care**, only quantity: years of schooling, not what was learned.
- **Data quality is weak** in the poorest countries, which are precisely those the index most needs to measure.
- **PPP conversions are imprecise** and revised.
- **It is an average**, so it hides regional and gender differences within a country.
- **It changes slowly**, since life expectancy and mean years of schooling are stocks that move over decades, so it is insensitive to recent policy.

## Other measures of development

The specification names six, and each addresses a gap HDI leaves.

| Measure | What it captures | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Percentage of adult male labour in agriculture** | structural change; a high proportion indicates a subsistence, low-productivity economy | it excludes women's work, which is a serious omission in agricultural economies; some agriculture is highly productive |
| **Access to clean water** | health infrastructure and the most basic determinant of child mortality | access does not mean reliability or quality; distance to the source matters |
| **Energy consumption per capita** | industrialisation and living standards | higher is not better environmentally, and it rewards inefficiency |
| **Access to the internet per thousand people** | information, education, markets and financial inclusion | it says nothing about speed, cost or affordability |
| **Access to mobile phones per thousand people** | communications, and increasingly banking through mobile money | counts devices rather than people, and many own several |
| **Access to doctors per thousand people** | health care capacity | ignores distribution between urban and rural areas, and the quality of training |

**The general lesson**, and the right way to use this list in an answer: **no single indicator is sufficient**, so development should be assessed using several together, chosen to suit the question. A country with high income per head, low access to clean water and few doctors per thousand is telling you something specific about where the income goes.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A useful case for the gap between growth measures and development measures.

**Income per head** is high by regional standards, driven by tourism, and a straightforward income measure would place the economy well above most of its neighbours. **HDI** is also relatively high, reflecting genuine and substantial investment in health and education over decades: life expectancy and schooling have both improved markedly.

**What the averages conceal** is what makes it instructive. Provision across a population dispersed over many small islands is very uneven: access to doctors, secondary schooling and higher education is concentrated in the capital, so the national figures overstate what is available to a resident of an outer island. **Access to clean water** is a real constraint, since fresh groundwater is limited and desalination is expensive. And **environmental sustainability**, which HDI ignores entirely, is arguably the central long-run development question for a low-lying island state.

**The general point:** a national average is the beginning of an assessment, and the distribution and the sustainability of the achievement are what the extra measures are for.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A 2-mark definition or a 4-mark explanation of the components; Analyse (6) of HDI data in the source; and a 20-mark essay on whether HDI is a good measure of development, or comparing the development of two countries from the data.

**Method for a data question:**

1. **Describe the data accurately**, quoting figures and the period.
2. **Compare HDI with the income measure.** Where they diverge, say what that tells you: a high income with a low HDI means the income is not being converted into health and education.
3. **Use the other measures given** to explain the divergence.
4. **State the limitation** of every measure you use.

**Structuring "is HDI a good measure of development?":**

1. **State what HDI measures** and how, with the three components.
2. **Two developed advantages**, with the reason each matters: broader than GDP, and health and education are both ends and means.
3. **Two developed limitations**, chosen for their importance: it ignores inequality, and it ignores the environment.
4. **Suggest what should be added**, using the six named measures, and say what gap each fills.
5. **Judge:** better than GDP alone, insufficient by itself, and best used alongside other indicators.
6. **State the condition:** it depends what the comparison is for. For comparing basic living standards across many countries it is well suited; for assessing sustainability or freedom it is not.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Growth is a means; development is the end.**
- **HDI uses a geometric mean**, so a very low score on one dimension cannot be bought off.
- **GNI rather than GDP** matters where profits leave the country.
- **It ignores inequality, the environment and freedom**, which are the three most important omissions.
- **Years of schooling measure quantity, not quality.**
- **Averages hide regional and gender differences.**
- **No single indicator is sufficient**, so use several chosen for the question.
- **Data quality is weakest where the index matters most.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Naming the wrong components.** Education, health and income, not employment or inequality.
- **Saying HDI uses GDP per capita.** It uses **GNI** per capita at PPP.
- **Forgetting the PPP adjustment.**
- **Claiming HDI includes inequality or the environment.**
- **Treating growth and development as the same thing.**
- **Listing the six other measures without saying what gap each fills.**
- **Quoting an HDI figure with no interpretation or comparison.**
- **Concluding that HDI is useless.** It is better than income alone and insufficient by itself.

</Mistakes>
