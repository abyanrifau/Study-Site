## Absolute against comparative advantage

**Absolute advantage** is about output: who can produce more with the same resources. **Comparative advantage** is about opportunity cost: who gives up less of the other good.

**The central insight:** trade is mutually beneficial whenever **opportunity costs differ**, even if one country is worse at producing everything. A country with an absolute disadvantage in both goods still has a comparative advantage in one of them, because it cannot be relatively worse at both. That is the result students most often fail to state, and it is the whole point of the theory.

## Working it out

<Formula name="Opportunity cost ratios" note="Always convert to the cost of ONE unit. Comparing output levels directly gives absolute advantage, which is not what the question asks.">
Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = Units of Y forgone ÷ Units of X gained

Lower opportunity cost = comparative advantage
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: the full method">

Output per worker per week:

| | Wheat (tonnes) | Cloth (metres) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Country A** | 40 | 20 |
| **Country B** | 15 | 30 |

**Step 1 — absolute advantage.** A produces more wheat (40 against 15). B produces more cloth (30 against 20). Each has an absolute advantage in one good.

**Step 2 — opportunity costs, which is what actually matters.**

`In A: 1 tonne of wheat costs 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 metres of cloth`

`In A: 1 metre of cloth costs 40 ÷ 20 = 2 tonnes of wheat`

`In B: 1 tonne of wheat costs 30 ÷ 15 = 2 metres of cloth`

`In B: 1 metre of cloth costs 15 ÷ 30 = 0.5 tonnes of wheat`

**Step 3 — compare.**

Wheat: A gives up 0.5 cloth, B gives up 2 cloth. **A has the comparative advantage in wheat.**

Cloth: A gives up 2 wheat, B gives up 0.5 wheat. **B has the comparative advantage in cloth.**

**Step 4 — show the gain, which is where the marks are.** Suppose each country has 100 workers, and before trade each splits its workforce equally.

| | Wheat | Cloth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: 50 workers each | 2,000 | 1,000 |
| B: 50 workers each | 750 | 1,500 |
| **World total** | **2,750** | **2,500** |

Now each specialises completely:

| | Wheat | Cloth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: 100 workers on wheat | 4,000 | 0 |
| B: 100 workers on cloth | 0 | 3,000 |
| **World total** | **4,000** | **3,000** |

**World output rises by 1,250 tonnes of wheat and 500 metres of cloth from the same resources.** That extra output is the gain from trade, and it is available to be shared between the two countries.

**Step 5 — the terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs.** A will not accept less than 0.5 cloth per tonne of wheat, since it can get that at home. B will not pay more than 2 cloth per tonne. So any rate **between 0.5 and 2** benefits both, and where it settles within that range determines **how the gain is divided**. Stating that range is a sophisticated point and it links directly to terms of trade in **4.3.2.3**.

</Worked>

## Assumptions of the theory

The specification requires these, and they are the source of the evaluation.

1. **No transport costs.**
2. **Perfect mobility of factors of production** within each country, so workers can move from cloth to wheat instantly and without cost.
3. **Constant returns to scale**, so the opportunity cost ratios do not change as a country specialises.
4. **Free trade**, with no tariffs or other barriers.
5. **Only two countries and two goods**, in the simple model.
6. **Perfect knowledge**, so producers know where the advantage lies.
7. **Externalities are ignored.**
8. **Factors are immobile between countries.**

## Limitations

- **Transport costs** can exceed the cost advantage, particularly for bulky or low-value goods, which removes the gain entirely.
- **Factors are not perfectly mobile.** A textile worker does not become a wheat farmer. The result is **structural unemployment** during the adjustment, and adjustment takes years while the model treats it as instantaneous. This is the single most important limitation.
- **Returns are not constant.** Economies of scale reinforce an existing advantage, so the first country into an industry can keep it whatever the underlying opportunity costs. Diseconomies eventually limit specialisation.
- **Protectionism exists**, so the pattern of trade reflects policy as well as comparative advantage.
- **Comparative advantage changes**, through technology, education, wage growth and exchange rates. A country specialising in today's advantage may find it gone in twenty years.
- **Over-specialisation creates dependence**, which is the primary product dependency problem in **4.3.6.2**: a country specialising in one or two commodities is exposed to volatile prices and to a long-run decline in its terms of trade.
- **Externalities are ignored.** Trade based on low environmental standards is not a genuine comparative advantage; it is an unpriced cost.
- **The distribution of the gain** is not determined by the theory. It depends on the terms of trade, and a country can specialise correctly and still capture very little of the gain.
- **Non-price factors matter**, including quality, reliability and branding, none of which appear in the model.

**The honest overall judgement:** comparative advantage is the strongest explanation of **why** trade raises total output, and it is a poor guide to whether any particular country benefits, because it says nothing about adjustment costs, the division of the gain, or dynamic changes in advantage.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A clear real comparative advantage, and a clear illustration of the limitations.

**The advantage** is in tourism and fishing, resting on natural resources that cannot be reproduced elsewhere. Specialising in them is unambiguously correct in the static theory: producing manufactured goods domestically would be far more costly than importing them.

**The limitation is dependence.** A near-total specialisation in tourism means global travel demand determines national income, and there is nothing to fall back on when it collapses. The static gain from trade is real; the resulting volatility is a cost the theory does not capture. That is why diversification appears in development strategy even where comparative advantage is clear, and it makes an excellent evaluation paragraph in an essay on specialisation.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A calculation of opportunity cost and identification of comparative advantage (4–6 marks in Section B); a 20-mark essay on the benefits and costs of specialisation and trade, or on the limitations of the theory.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Set out the data as a table** if it is not already.
2. **State the absolute advantages** in one line.
3. **Calculate the opportunity cost of one unit of each good in each country.** Four calculations, all shown.
4. **State each comparative advantage** with the figures that prove it.
5. **Demonstrate the gain** with a before-and-after output table if the marks justify it.
6. **State the range of mutually beneficial terms of trade**, which is the part that distinguishes a top answer.

**Structuring the essay on specialisation and trade:**

1. Define comparative advantage and state the mutual-gain result.
2. **Benefit one:** higher world output and lower prices, with the PPF or the arithmetic to support it.
3. **Benefit two:** economies of scale and greater choice, and for a developing economy access to foreign exchange and technology.
4. **Cost one:** structural unemployment during adjustment, because factors are not mobile. This is the strongest single cost.
5. **Cost two:** over-specialisation and dependence, with primary product dependency as the example.
6. **Evaluation:** the assumptions do not hold; the theory explains the size of the gain and not its distribution; comparative advantage changes over time.
7. Conclude with a condition: it depends on the flexibility of the labour market, the diversity of the export base, and the terms of trade the country faces.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **A country worse at everything still has a comparative advantage in something.**
- **The theory determines the size of the gain, not its distribution**, which depends on the terms of trade.
- **Factor immobility is the most damaging assumption**, because it converts a theoretical gain into real unemployment.
- **Economies of scale can lock in an advantage** regardless of underlying opportunity costs.
- **Comparative advantage is dynamic**, so today's specialism may be tomorrow's problem.
- **Over-specialisation creates volatility**, which the static theory ignores.
- **Unpriced externalities are not a genuine advantage.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Identifying comparative advantage from output levels.** That gives absolute advantage.
- **Calculating the opportunity cost the wrong way up.** For 1 unit of X, divide the Y output by the X output.
- **Concluding that a country with an absolute disadvantage in both goods cannot gain from trade.**
- **Failing to show the gain**, which is what converts the calculation into analysis.
- **Omitting the range of mutually beneficial terms of trade.**
- **Listing the assumptions without explaining why each failure matters.**
- **Treating adjustment as costless**, which is the error the theory itself makes.
- **Forgetting that comparative advantage changes over time.**

</Mistakes>
