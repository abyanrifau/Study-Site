## Economic constraints

<Chunk title="Volatility of commodity prices" open>

Commodity prices are highly volatile, because **both supply and demand are price inelastic in the short run**. Supply depends on harvests, weather and long investment lead times; demand is a derived demand for industrial inputs or a necessity in the case of food. So a small shift in either curve produces a large change in price.

**Why volatility is a problem even when the average price is adequate:**

- **Export earnings swing unpredictably**, so the current account and the exchange rate do too.
- **Government revenue swings**, since commodity exporters depend heavily on royalties and export taxes, which makes budgeting and long-term capital planning almost impossible.
- **Investment is deterred**, because returns are unpredictable.
- **Producers' incomes are unstable**, which for smallholder farmers means periods of severe hardship.

**So volatility, not just the level, is the constraint**, which is why buffer stock schemes and stabilisation funds appear among the strategies in **4.3.6.3**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Primary product dependency and Prebisch-Singer" open>

**Primary product dependency** means relying on a narrow range of commodities for export earnings. It combines the volatility above with a long-run structural problem.

**The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis:** the terms of trade of primary exporters decline over the long run relative to manufactures. **The reasoning:**

- **Demand for primary products is income inelastic.** As world incomes rise, demand for food and raw materials grows more slowly than demand for manufactures and services.
- **Productivity gains are passed on differently.** Primary producers are numerous and competitive, so lower costs become lower prices. Manufacturers have more market power, so gains are retained as profit and wages.
- **Synthetic substitutes and improved material efficiency** reduce demand for natural inputs over time.

**The consequence:** a country dependent on primary exports must export ever more merely to buy the same quantity of imports, so its real purchasing power falls over time even with stable output.

**The qualifications:** the evidence is contested, the pattern differs across commodities, and long commodity booms driven by emerging economy demand have interrupted it. Present it as a hypothesis with supporting reasoning, not as a law.

**The other side of dependency:** low value added. Exporting raw cocoa rather than chocolate means the processing profit, and the skilled employment, accrue elsewhere. That is the argument for moving up the value chain rather than simply diversifying.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="The savings gap and Harrod-Domar" open>

<Formula name="Harrod-Domar" note="Growth requires investment, investment requires saving, and saving requires income above subsistence. That circularity is the poverty trap.">
Growth rate = Savings ratio ÷ Capital-output ratio
</Formula>

**The mechanism:** low income means most of it must be consumed, so the savings ratio is low; low savings means low investment; low investment means a small capital stock and low productivity; low productivity means low income. The circle closes.

<Worked title="Worked example">

A country has a savings ratio of **6%** and a capital-output ratio of **4**.

`Growth rate = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5% a year`

With population growing at **2.2%**, **income per head is falling** despite positive growth.

**Now suppose aid or FDI raises the savings and investment ratio to 12%:**

`Growth rate = 12 ÷ 4 = 3.0% a year`

Income per head now rises by about 0.8% a year. **And if efficiency improves so that the capital-output ratio falls to 3:**

`Growth rate = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.0% a year`

**The lesson the model gives:** growth can be raised either by increasing investment or by using capital more efficiently, and **the second is cheaper**. A country that raises investment while wasting it on poorly chosen projects gains far less than the arithmetic suggests. That is the strongest evaluative use of the model.

**Its limitations:** it assumes capital is the only constraint, when skills, institutions and infrastructure may bind harder; it assumes investment is productive, which depends on governance; it ignores technology; and it says nothing about where the savings come from or what the distribution of the resulting income is.

</Worked>

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="The foreign currency gap">

Even with domestic savings, the capital equipment, fuel and technology needed for development must be **imported**, and that requires foreign currency. A country whose export earnings are insufficient faces a **foreign currency gap**.

**Why it is made worse by other constraints:** debt service must be paid in foreign currency, and it competes directly with imports of capital equipment; a fall in commodity prices reduces earnings exactly when they are most needed; and capital flight removes reserves.

**How it is closed:** export growth, FDI, aid, remittances, borrowing, or import restriction, which slows development by restricting the very capital goods needed.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Capital flight">

Wealth held by residents moves abroad, driven by political instability, fear of expropriation, exchange rate risk, better returns elsewhere, or the proceeds of corruption.

**Why it is especially damaging:** it removes the savings that could have funded domestic investment, depletes foreign exchange reserves, depreciates the currency, and it is **self-reinforcing**, because the depreciation itself prompts further outflows. It also frequently coincides with inward aid, so the country is losing private capital while receiving public transfers.

**Restraining it** requires either capital controls, which deter future inward investment, or the confidence that comes from stable policy and secure property rights, which is slow to build and easy to destroy.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Demographic factors">

**A high birth rate and a young population** produce a high dependency ratio: a large non-working population supported by a small workforce. Savings are lower, spending per child on health and education is lower, and output must grow faster than population merely to hold income per head constant, as the Harrod-Domar example shows.

**Rapid population growth also** puts pressure on land, water, housing and infrastructure, and raises urban unemployment as migration to cities outpaces job creation.

**The other side:** a young population becomes a **demographic dividend** once the birth rate falls, because a large working-age cohort supports relatively few dependants. Realising it requires the education and the jobs to be there, otherwise the dividend becomes youth unemployment.

**Migration** relieves unemployment and generates remittances, and it removes skilled workers, which is the **brain drain**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Debt">

**Overseas debt.** Servicing consumes foreign exchange and government revenue, so it competes with health, education and investment. Debt in a **foreign currency** is far more dangerous, because a depreciation raises its real burden just when export earnings have fallen. High debt also deters investment, because investors anticipate future taxation or default.

**Household debt.** Where borrowing is at very high interest rates from informal lenders, it traps households in repayment and prevents the accumulation of any assets. This is the problem microfinance is intended to address.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Access to credit and banking">

Where most households and small firms have no bank account, they cannot save securely, borrow to invest, insure against risk, or receive payments reliably.

**The consequences:** savings are held as cash or livestock rather than being channelled into investment, so the savings gap is worse than income alone would suggest; small businesses cannot buy equipment or hold inventory; and there is no ability to smooth consumption through a bad harvest. **Mobile banking has changed this materially** in several countries, which is why access to mobile phones appears as a development indicator in **4.3.6.1**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Infrastructure">

Poor transport raises the cost of every good and cuts farmers off from markets. Unreliable power forces firms to run their own generators, raising costs, or to stop producing. Inadequate water and sanitation damages health and therefore productivity. Weak telecommunications restricts information and market access.

**Why it is a particularly stubborn constraint:** infrastructure requires very large capital sums with long payback periods, which is exactly what a country facing a savings gap cannot fund. It is also a **public good** in large part, so the private sector will not provide it adequately, meaning it requires a state with the revenue and the competence to build it.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Education and skills">

Low literacy, low school enrolment and completion, poor quality teaching, a mismatch between skills taught and skills needed, and the opportunity cost to poor families of a child in school rather than working.

**Why it constrains everything else:** without skills, technology transfer does not happen even when FDI arrives; the country cannot move up the value chain from primary production; and infrastructure and capital cannot be used productively. **Education is the constraint whose removal loosens the most other constraints**, which is why it appears in almost every development strategy and why it is worth prioritising in an essay.

</Chunk>

## Non-economic constraints

<Chunk title="The five named factors" open>

**Corruption.** Public resources diverted; investment misallocated to projects that generate bribes rather than returns; a tax on business that deters investment, particularly by foreign firms subject to anti-bribery law at home; and the undermining of trust in institutions. It also raises the capital-output ratio directly, since money spent on a project that is never completed produces no output at all.

**Poor governance.** Weak rule of law, insecure property rights, unenforceable contracts, unpredictable policy, incapable administration and no accountability. **This is arguably the binding constraint**, because it determines whether any of the economic remedies work: aid is stolen, investment does not arrive, taxes cannot be collected and infrastructure is not maintained. Countries with similar resources and very different institutions have diverged enormously, which is the strongest evidence available on this point.

**Civil wars.** The most destructive of all: infrastructure and human capital destroyed, populations displaced, investment halted, spending diverted to the military, food production disrupted, and institutions destroyed. The effects persist for decades after the fighting ends. **No development strategy functions during conflict**, so where the source mentions it, it should be addressed first.

**Migration.** Emigration of skilled workers is the brain drain: the country pays to train doctors, nurses, engineers and teachers who then work abroad, so the public investment benefits another economy. **Against that**, remittances are large and reliable, often exceeding aid; returning migrants bring skills and capital; and the prospect of emigration can itself raise enrolment in training.

**Terrorism.** Deters tourism and FDI, raises security costs and insurance premiums, disrupts trade, and diverts government spending. For an economy dependent on tourism the effect is immediate and severe, and reputational damage outlasts the incident by years.

</Chunk>

## How the constraints interact

The single most valuable insight for an essay: **these constraints reinforce each other**, which is why they persist.

- **Low income** means low saving, which means low investment, which means low productivity, which means low income. That is the **poverty trap**.
- **Primary product dependency** means volatile revenue, which means the government cannot plan the infrastructure and education that would allow diversification away from primary products.
- **Poor governance** deters FDI and causes capital flight, which widens the savings gap, which prevents the infrastructure investment that would attract FDI.
- **Low education** prevents technology transfer, which keeps the country in low-value primary production, which does not generate the revenue to fund education.
- **Debt service** consumes the foreign currency needed to import the capital goods that would raise export earnings to service the debt.

**The policy implication that follows** is that isolated interventions achieve little, and that the binding constraint has to be identified and removed first. For most countries that is **governance and education** rather than capital, which is why simply providing more finance has so often disappointed.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one constraint; Analyse (6) on data showing a constraint; and a 20-mark essay on the constraints facing a particular economy, which is very likely given the source material in Section B.

**Structuring the essay:**

1. **Identify the country's situation from the source**, with data: income per head, export composition, savings ratio, debt, HDI.
2. **Constraint one, developed and chained.** Choose the one the data supports most strongly. For a commodity exporter, primary product dependency: volatile prices mean volatile export earnings and government revenue, which prevents long-term planning, which prevents the infrastructure investment needed to diversify.
3. **Constraint two, developed and chained.** For example the savings gap, using Harrod-Domar with the arithmetic.
4. **One non-economic constraint**, developed. Governance or conflict, if the source supports it.
5. **The interaction paragraph**, showing how two or three constraints reinforce each other. This is the strongest paragraph available and few candidates write it.
6. **Judge which is the binding constraint** and why, with a condition.
7. **Say what you would want to know** to be more confident.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The constraints reinforce each other**, which is why poverty traps persist.
- **Volatility is a constraint independent of the level of prices.**
- **Governance may be the binding constraint**, since it determines whether any other remedy works.
- **Education loosens the most other constraints.**
- **Harrod-Domar shows efficiency matters as much as the volume of investment**, because the capital-output ratio is a denominator.
- **Prebisch-Singer is a hypothesis with contested evidence**, not a law.
- **Migration has offsetting effects:** brain drain against remittances.
- **Nothing works during conflict.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing constraints without chaining them** to their effect on growth or development.
- **Inverting the Harrod-Domar formula.** Savings ratio divided by the capital-output ratio.
- **Presenting Prebisch-Singer as established fact.**
- **Treating primary product dependency as only a price-level problem**, ignoring volatility and low value added.
- **Forgetting the non-economic constraints**, which are often the most important.
- **Treating migration as purely a loss.**
- **Failing to identify which constraint binds**, and so offering no judgement.
- **Ignoring the data** in the source.

</Mistakes>
