## Absolute and relative poverty

| | Absolute | Relative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | income below a fixed subsistence level | income far below the average in that society |
| **Benchmark** | fixed, the same in real terms everywhere | moves with average income |
| **Typical measure** | the international poverty line in PPP dollars per day | a proportion of median income, commonly 50% or 60% |
| **Can it be eliminated?** | **yes** | **no**, by construction |
| **Effect of growth** | falls if the gains reach the poorest | may rise, fall or stay the same |

**The distinction that earns marks:** absolute poverty can in principle be eliminated, because the threshold is fixed. **Relative poverty cannot**, because it is defined against the average: if every income doubled, absolute poverty would fall sharply and relative poverty would not change at all.

**A second point that follows:** relative poverty is really a measure of **inequality at the bottom** of the distribution rather than of deprivation. That is why the two measures can move in opposite directions, and noticing that is exactly what an evaluation question rewards.

## Measures

**Absolute poverty:**

- **The international poverty line**, a fixed sum per person per day at purchasing power parity, adjusted periodically for prices. **Strengths:** comparable between countries and over time, and simple. **Limitations:** the threshold is arbitrary; it says nothing about how far below the line people are; PPP conversions are imprecise; subsistence goods differ between countries; and it ignores access to public services, which may matter more than cash income.
- **Multidimensional measures**, combining nutrition, schooling, water, sanitation and electricity. **Strength:** poverty is not only about income. **Limitation:** the weights given to each dimension are a judgement.

**Relative poverty:**

- **A percentage of median income**, commonly 60%. **Strengths:** it captures exclusion from normal life in that society, and it is comparable within a country over time. **Limitations:** it is a measure of the distribution rather than of hardship; it is not comparable between countries at different income levels, since a relatively poor person in a rich country may be far better off than an average person elsewhere; and it can fall during a recession simply because median income has fallen.

**That last point is worth remembering:** in a recession, median income falls, so the relative poverty line falls, so measured relative poverty can improve while everyone is worse off. A measure that improves when the economy collapses needs to be read carefully.

## Causes of changes in poverty

<Chunk title="The seven named causes" open>

**Economic growth.** The most powerful single force reducing **absolute** poverty: higher output raises employment, wages and tax revenue for public services. **The qualification is essential:** growth reduces poverty only if its benefits reach the poorest. Growth concentrated in a capital-intensive extractive sector may raise GDP substantially and leave absolute poverty almost unchanged, and it will raise **relative** poverty by widening the distribution. So the answer depends on the **pattern** of growth, not only its rate.

**Education and training.** Raises human capital, therefore productivity, therefore wages, and it breaks the intergenerational transmission of poverty. **Qualifications:** it takes a generation; it requires the family to be able to forgo a child's earnings; and it delivers little if there are no jobs for the newly skilled, in which case the outcome is emigration rather than poverty reduction.

**Welfare benefits.** Direct income transfers raise the incomes of the poorest immediately and are the fastest-acting instrument. **Qualifications:** they must be financed by taxation or borrowing; they may reduce work incentives where withdrawal rates are high, which is the poverty trap; administrative capacity is limited in poorer countries; and take-up of means-tested benefits is often incomplete. **Means-tested** benefits are better targeted and create worse incentives and lower take-up; **universal** benefits reach everyone and cost more and are less redistributive per dollar spent.

**Changes in tax structure.** A more **progressive** system redistributes towards the poorest, and raising the tax-free threshold or cutting indirect taxes on necessities helps directly. **Qualifications:** very high marginal rates may reduce incentives and encourage avoidance, which is Laffer curve analysis from **4.3.5.2**; indirect taxes on essentials are **regressive**, so their structure matters as much as the total; and developing countries often rely on indirect taxes because income tax is hard to administer.

**Structural changes in the economy.** The shift out of agriculture into manufacturing and then services raises average productivity and incomes over time. **In the transition** it destroys livelihoods faster than it creates replacements, and those displaced frequently lack the skills to follow the work. So structural change reduces poverty in the long run and can raise it sharply in the short run.

**Aid.** Can fund health, education, infrastructure and emergency relief, and fill the savings and foreign currency gaps. **Qualifications:** it may be lost to corruption; it can create dependency; tied aid requires purchases from the donor and is worth less than its face value; it can displace domestic production, as food aid does to local farmers; and it is volatile. Its effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of governance receiving it, which is the point developed in **4.3.6.3**.

**Civil wars and conflict.** The most destructive single cause of poverty: it destroys infrastructure and human capital, displaces populations, halts investment, diverts spending to the military, disrupts food production and destroys the institutions on which every other policy depends. **Nothing else works while conflict continues**, which is why it should be identified first in any answer where the source mentions it.

</Chunk>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Definitions distinguishing absolute from relative poverty (2 marks); Explain (4) on one cause; Analyse (6) on data showing a change in poverty; and a 20-mark essay on how a country can reduce poverty, or on whether growth is the best way to reduce it.

**Structuring "how should this country reduce poverty?":**

1. **State which poverty the question concerns**, absolute or relative, and measure it from the data.
2. **Identify the cause** from the source: subsistence agriculture, conflict, lack of education, structural change, or a weak tax and benefit system.
3. **Policy one, growth-promoting**, chained: investment in infrastructure raises productivity, which raises output and employment, which raises incomes and tax revenue.
4. **Policy two, direct transfers**, chained: benefits raise the incomes of the poorest immediately, reducing absolute poverty, funded by progressive taxation.
5. **Policy three, education**, chained through human capital to productivity and wages.
6. **Evaluate:** speed against durability, the cost and its opportunity cost, incentive effects, administrative capacity, and the pattern of growth rather than its rate.
7. **Conclude** with a prioritised combination and a condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Absolute poverty can be eliminated; relative poverty cannot.**
- **The pattern of growth matters more than the rate.**
- **A policy can reduce absolute poverty while raising relative poverty**, and vice versa.
- **Relative poverty can fall in a recession** because the median falls.
- **Transfers are fast and treat the symptom; education is slow and treats the cause.**
- **Incentive effects and the poverty trap** limit how far benefits can go.
- **Nothing works during conflict**, so it should be addressed first where it is present.
- **Aid depends on governance**, which is why the same aid produces very different results in different countries.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing absolute with relative poverty**, or defining absolute poverty as a proportion of median income.
- **Claiming relative poverty can be eliminated.**
- **Assuming growth automatically reduces poverty** without considering distribution.
- **Treating aid as unambiguously beneficial.**
- **Recommending higher benefits with no reference to financing** or to incentive effects.
- **Ignoring administrative capacity** when recommending means-tested transfers in a developing economy.
- **Overlooking conflict** when the source mentions it.
- **Failing to use the data** in a Section B answer.

</Mistakes>
