## Income and wealth

**Income is a flow**, received over a period: wages, profit, interest, rent, dividends and benefits. **Wealth is a stock**, held at a point in time: property, shares, pensions, savings and businesses.

**The relationship between them is the point to make.** Wealth generates income, through rent, interest, dividends and capital gains, and income allows saving, which accumulates wealth. So the two reinforce each other, and inequality in each compounds the other over time and across generations.

**Wealth is always far more unequally distributed than income**, in every country. Two reasons: wealth accumulates over a lifetime and is inherited, so it compounds across generations, while income is earned within a period; and many households have little or no wealth at all, whereas almost everyone has some income.

## Measuring inequality

</Figure>

**The Lorenz curve** plots the cumulative percentage of income received against the cumulative percentage of the population, ranked from poorest to richest.

- **The diagonal** is the line of perfect equality: 20% of the population receives 20% of income, and so on.
- **The actual curve lies below it**, because the poorest fifth receives less than a fifth of income.
- **The further the curve bows away from the diagonal, the greater the inequality.**

**How to read it:** find a point on the horizontal axis, read up to the curve, and read across. If the curve shows that at 40% of the population the cumulative income share is 12%, then the poorest 40% receive 12% of total income.

<Formula name="Gini coefficient" note="A number between 0 and 1, sometimes expressed as a percentage. Typical values run from around 0.25 in the most equal economies to over 0.60 in the most unequal.">
Gini coefficient = Area between the Lorenz curve and the line of equality ÷ Whole area beneath the line of equality
</Formula>

**Interpretation:** 0 is perfect equality, 1 is one person receiving everything. A rise in the coefficient means inequality has increased.

**Limitations of the Gini coefficient:**

- **It is a single number**, so two very different distributions can share the same coefficient. One country may be unequal because of extreme wealth at the top, another because of extreme poverty at the bottom, and the policy responses are completely different.
- **It says nothing about absolute living standards.** A very poor country can be highly equal.
- **It is usually measured on income rather than wealth**, so it understates overall inequality.
- **It is normally measured before taxes and benefits or after**, and the two figures differ substantially, so always check which.
- **It ignores benefits in kind**, such as free health care and education, which reduce effective inequality.
- **Data quality is weak** where much income is informal or undeclared.
- **It takes no account of age.** A population with many students and pensioners looks more unequal at a point in time than over a lifetime.

## Causes of inequality

**Within countries:**

- **Wage differentials**, reflecting skills, education, occupation and bargaining power. Technology has raised demand for skilled labour and reduced it for routine work, widening the gap.
- **Ownership of capital.** Returns to capital accrue to those who already have wealth, so the profit share of national income raises inequality directly.
- **Unequal access to education**, which transmits inequality between generations.
- **Unemployment**, the single sharpest cause of falling into the bottom of the distribution.
- **The tax and benefit system.** Progressive tax and transfers reduce inequality; regressive indirect taxes raise it.
- **Inheritance**, which transmits wealth inequality across generations.
- **Discrimination** by gender, ethnicity, region or caste.
- **Trade union power**, whose decline in several economies reduced the bargaining power of lower-paid workers.
- **Globalisation**, which raised returns to skills and to mobile capital while exposing lower-skilled workers to competition.
- **Regional differences** in productivity and investment.

**Between countries:** differences in productivity and capital stock, historical factors including colonialism, resource endowments, institutions and governance, access to education and health, population growth, debt burdens and terms of trade movements, and access to world markets.

## Impact of inequality

<Chunk title="The six named impacts" open>

**Enterprise.** **The positive case:** the prospect of large rewards encourages risk-taking and innovation, and existing wealth provides the capital and collateral for new ventures. **The negative case:** where inequality means most of the population has no access to credit or capital, most potential entrepreneurs never start, so enterprise is suppressed rather than encouraged. Which effect dominates depends on whether the financial system reaches ordinary households, which is the microfinance argument in **4.3.6.3**.

**Incentives.** Differentials reward effort, skill acquisition and mobility. **Against that**, very high marginal tax and benefit withdrawal rates at the bottom create a **poverty trap** with the weakest incentives of anyone in the economy, and inequality perceived as unfair reduces effort and cooperation.

**Savings.** Higher-income households save a larger proportion of income, so greater inequality raises the aggregate savings ratio, which finances investment. That is the Harrod-Domar argument for inequality in a developing economy. **Against it**, savings may be held abroad rather than invested domestically, which is **capital flight**, and lower consumption by the poorest reduces aggregate demand. So inequality may raise the funds available and reduce the demand that makes investment worthwhile.

**Education.** Poorer households cannot afford schooling, or need children's labour, so talent is wasted. That is both unjust and **inefficient**, because the economy loses the productivity of people who would have been highly capable. Unequal education perpetuates inequality into the next generation.

**Migration.** Inequality between countries drives emigration, and inequality within a country drives internal migration to cities. Emigration relieves unemployment and generates remittances, and removes skilled workers, which is the brain drain.

**Life expectancy.** Lower incomes mean worse nutrition, housing, sanitation and access to health care, so life expectancy is lower and infant mortality higher among the poorest. More unequal societies tend to show worse average health outcomes than more equal ones at the same average income, partly because of the composition effect and partly because public health provision tends to be weaker.

</Chunk>

**Other consequences worth citing:** social and political instability; higher crime; lower social mobility; and lower aggregate demand, since the poorest have the highest marginal propensity to consume, so redistribution towards them raises consumption.

## Economic change, development and capitalism

**The Kuznets hypothesis** suggests inequality first **rises** and then **falls** as an economy develops: industrialisation initially benefits urban industrial workers and capital owners while rural incomes lag, and inequality later falls as education spreads, labour becomes scarcer and the state develops the capacity to redistribute. **The evidence is mixed**, and several developed economies have seen inequality rise again, so the inverted-U is a possible pattern rather than a law.

**The significance of the free market economy for inequality.** Capitalism produces inequality by design, and that is the mechanism by which it works: prices and profits signal where resources are needed, and the rewards to skill, risk and enterprise are what call forth the effort and innovation that raise output.

**The argument for accepting it:** incentives raise total output, so the poorest may be better off in absolute terms in a more unequal, faster-growing economy than in a more equal, slower-growing one. **The arguments against:** market outcomes depend on the initial distribution of assets and education, which is largely inherited rather than earned; monopoly, inheritance and unequal access to capital produce rewards unrelated to effort; markets do not provide public goods or merit goods adequately; and beyond some point inequality becomes self-reinforcing, entrenching itself rather than rewarding contribution.

**The conclusion that works in an essay:** some inequality is necessary for incentives and efficiency, so the question is never whether inequality should exist but **how much**, and whether it arises from contribution or from inherited position. That framing is worth a mark on its own.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A Lorenz curve or Gini interpretation from the source (4–6 marks); Explain (4) on one cause or impact; and a 20-mark essay on the causes or consequences of inequality, or on whether a government should reduce it.

**Method for the data question:**

1. **Read the Lorenz curve accurately**, quoting a specific point: the poorest x% receive y% of income.
2. **Compare the two curves or coefficients** if two are given, and state which is more unequal.
3. **State the limitation** of the measure you have used.

**Structuring "should the government reduce inequality?":**

1. **Measure it from the data**, using the Gini coefficient or the Lorenz curve.
2. **Identify the causes** present in the source.
3. **The case for reducing it**, chained: redistribution raises the incomes of households with a high marginal propensity to consume, which raises aggregate demand, output and employment; and better access to education raises human capital and productivity.
4. **The case against**, chained: higher marginal tax rates may reduce incentives to work, invest and take risk, and may reduce the savings that finance investment, with Laffer curve analysis and capital flight as the mechanisms.
5. **Distinguish the instruments.** Progressive tax, transfers, minimum wages, education and health spending, and inheritance taxes have very different effects on incentives.
6. **Evaluate:** some inequality is necessary; the source of the inequality matters; and the effect depends on the country's stage of development.
7. **Conclude** with a judgement and a condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Wealth is always more unequal than income**, and the two reinforce each other.
- **Two very different distributions can share the same Gini coefficient.**
- **Check whether the Gini is before or after taxes and benefits.**
- **Inequality may raise savings and reduce aggregate demand at the same time.**
- **Unequal education is inefficient as well as unjust**, because talent is wasted.
- **Some inequality is necessary for incentives**, so the question is how much.
- **What matters is whether the inequality reflects contribution or inherited position.**
- **Kuznets is a possible pattern, not a law.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing income with wealth.** One is a flow, the other a stock.
- **Saying a higher Gini coefficient means less inequality.**
- **Drawing the Lorenz curve above the line of equality.**
- **Reading the Lorenz curve without quoting a specific point.**
- **Treating inequality as identical to poverty.** A country can be very poor and very equal.
- **Listing the six impacts without giving both sides** of enterprise, incentives and savings.
- **Presenting the Kuznets curve as established fact.**
- **Arguing for complete equality**, which ignores incentives and is not a serious economic position.

</Mistakes>
