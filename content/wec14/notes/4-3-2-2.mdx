## The five influences

<Chunk title="Impact of emerging economies" open>

The single largest change in the pattern of world trade over recent decades.

- **Manufacturing shifted towards Asia**, as low labour costs, improving infrastructure and large-scale investment made it the location for assembly and then for more sophisticated production.
- **Commodity demand rose sharply.** Industrialisation and construction require metals, energy and food, so commodity exporters in Africa, Latin America and Australasia found their largest customers were no longer in the developed world. That redirected trade flows, not just their size.
- **New consumer markets** emerged as incomes rose, so developed economies began exporting consumer goods, services, education and luxury products to countries they had previously only imported from.
- **South-south trade grew**, meaning trade between developing and emerging economies, which was historically small and is now substantial.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Changes in comparative advantage">

Comparative advantage is not fixed. It moves through:

- **Wage growth.** A country that industrialises sees wages rise, so its advantage moves from labour-intensive assembly towards higher-value production. Lower-cost countries then take the assembly work.
- **Investment in education and skills**, which builds an advantage in services and technology.
- **Technology and capital accumulation.**
- **Resource discovery or depletion.**
- **Infrastructure investment**, which reduces the cost of getting goods to market.

**The pattern this produces** is a ladder: each cohort of industrialising economies moves up as the next takes over the lowest-value work. Recognising that dynamic is what distinguishes a good answer from a description.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Growth in trading blocs and bilateral agreements">

Blocs raise trade **between members** and divert it away from non-members behind a common external tariff. Bilateral agreements do the same on a smaller scale.

**The effect on the pattern rather than the volume** is the point: trade becomes more **regional**. A firm sources within the bloc rather than from the globally cheapest supplier, which is trade diversion, and the geographic pattern of trade shifts even where its total volume does not.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Changes in relative exchange rates">

A depreciation makes a country's exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so export volumes rise and import volumes fall, subject to elasticities and the time lags in **4.3.3.2**.

**The important qualification:** an exchange rate movement changes trade flows **without changing underlying costs or productivity**. So it shifts the pattern of trade temporarily, and a country whose competitiveness rests on a weak currency has not actually become more competitive. Inflation will typically erode the advantage over time.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Changes in protectionism">

Tariffs, quotas, non-tariff barriers and subsidies redirect trade. **Two effects to distinguish:**

- **Trade reduction:** the barrier lowers the total volume traded.
- **Trade redirection:** the barrier shifts trade to a different partner, so one country's exports fall and another's rise with little change in the total.

Recent tariff increases between major economies produced mainly the second effect: goods were rerouted through third countries rather than not traded at all.

</Chunk>

## Changes in trade flows, and the reasons

**Three well-established changes worth being able to describe:**

1. **Trade grew faster than world output** for several decades, so trade as a share of world GDP rose. The reasons are falling barriers, falling transport and communication costs, and the fragmentation of production into supply chains, which means a single finished good crosses borders several times and is counted each time.
2. **Trade in services grew faster than trade in goods.** Cheap reliable communication made software, design, finance, accounting and customer support tradable, and tourism and education grew with rising incomes.
3. **The direction shifted towards Asia and towards south-south trade**, following the growth of emerging economies.

**A fourth, more recent change:** the growth of trade relative to output slowed, and in some periods reversed, as supply chains were shortened, barriers rose and firms prioritised resilience over cost. Being able to mention that shows awareness that the trend is not automatic.

## Volume against value

The distinction carries marks in data questions. **Volume** is quantity; **value** is quantity multiplied by price.

A country's export **value** can rise while its export **volume** falls, if prices have risen faster than quantities have fallen. That is exactly what happens to a commodity exporter during a price boom, and it is why an examiner will give credit for noticing that a rise in export earnings may reflect price rather than quantity. Always check which the data shows.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one influence; Analyse (6) on a change in trade flows shown in the source; Assess (10–12) on the reasons for a change in a country's pattern of trade.

**Method for a data question:**

1. **Describe the change accurately first.** Direction, size and period, quoting the figures.
2. **Check volume against value**, and say which the data shows.
3. **Give two or three reasons**, selected from the five influences and matched to the evidence in the source.
4. **Chain each reason** to the observed change rather than listing it.
5. **Evaluate:** which reason is most important here, what else could explain it, and what further data you would want.

**Structuring an essay on changing patterns of trade:**

1. Identify the change from the source.
2. Two developed reasons, each chained to the data.
3. **Distinguish structural from temporary causes.** A change in comparative advantage is structural; an exchange rate movement is temporary.
4. Consider what will happen next, since comparative advantage continues to move.
5. Judge which cause dominates, with a condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Distinguish volume from value.** A rise in export earnings may be entirely a price effect.
- **Exchange rates shift trade without changing underlying competitiveness**, so the effect is temporary.
- **Blocs change the geographic pattern of trade even when the total does not change.**
- **Protectionism often redirects trade rather than reducing it.**
- **Comparative advantage is dynamic**, so a country moves up the ladder as wages rise.
- **Supply chain fragmentation inflates recorded trade**, because the same good is counted at each border.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing volume with value.**
- **Listing the five influences** rather than applying two or three to the evidence.
- **Treating comparative advantage as fixed.**
- **Claiming a depreciation makes a country more competitive** without noting that nothing real has changed.
- **Assuming protectionism reduces total trade**, when it often redirects it.
- **Describing the data without explaining it**, which caps the marks.
- **Ignoring the growth of trade in services**, which is one of the largest changes.

</Mistakes>
