## The three systems

| | Fixed | Managed | Floating |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Set by** | the authorities | market, with intervention | the market |
| **Certainty for traders** | high | moderate | low |
| **Need for reserves** | large | moderate | none |
| **Monetary policy** | tied to defending the peg | partly constrained | independent |
| **Automatic correction of a deficit** | no | partial | yes |
| **Main risk** | speculative attack, misalignment | uncertainty over policy | volatility |

**Fixed.** Certainty for traders and investors and a discipline against inflation, since inflation cannot be offset by depreciation. **The costs** are large reserves, the loss of monetary policy independence, vulnerability to speculative attack if the rate is thought wrong, and the absence of automatic adjustment, so an overvalued peg must be corrected by deflating the domestic economy instead.

**Floating.** Monetary policy is free, no reserves are needed, and a current account deficit is corrected automatically as the currency falls. **The costs** are volatility, which discourages trade and investment, and the loss of the anti-inflation discipline.

**Managed** attempts to capture the benefits of both and can end up with the disadvantages of both: the market does not know what the authorities will do, and reserves are still required.

## Revaluation, devaluation, appreciation, depreciation

**This is a two-mark definition that students routinely lose.**

- **Revaluation and devaluation** are **deliberate policy decisions** by the authorities, and therefore only possible under a fixed or managed system.
- **Appreciation and depreciation** are **market movements** under a floating system.

Use the right word for the system described in the question.

## Government intervention in currency markets

<Chunk title="Three methods" open>

**Foreign currency transactions.** To support the currency, the central bank buys its own currency using foreign exchange reserves, raising demand. To weaken it, it sells its own currency and accumulates reserves. **The limitation:** supporting a currency depletes finite reserves, so a determined speculative attack can exhaust them, while weakening a currency can be sustained almost indefinitely because reserves accumulate. That asymmetry explains why defending an overvalued currency so often fails.

**The use of interest rates.** Higher interest rates attract capital inflows seeking better returns, raising demand for the currency and appreciating it. **The cost:** interest rates then serve the exchange rate rather than the domestic economy, so a country may be raising rates into a recession, which is the loss of monetary independence in practice.

**Quantitative easing.** Creating money to buy assets increases the supply of the currency and lowers long-term interest rates, both of which tend to **depreciate** it. It is usually pursued for domestic reasons, with the currency effect a side effect, and it is widely criticised abroad as an indirect competitive devaluation.

</Chunk>

## Factors influencing a floating rate

<Figure caption="The exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of the currency">
<CurrencyMarketDiagram />
</Figure>

The specification names seven, and a diagram should be used to show each as a shift in demand or supply.

- **Relative interest rates.** Higher domestic rates attract inflows, raising demand for the currency, so it appreciates. This is the most immediate influence.
- **Relative inflation rates (purchasing power parity).** Higher domestic inflation makes the country's goods less competitive, so demand for its currency falls and it depreciates. PPP theory says rates adjust over the long run so that a given sum buys the same basket everywhere. **Its limitations:** many goods and most services are not traded, transport costs and barriers exist, and short-run rates are dominated by capital flows rather than by goods prices. So PPP is a long-run tendency at best.
- **The current account.** A deficit means more of the currency is supplied to buy imports than is demanded to buy exports, so it tends to depreciate.
- **Strength of the economy.** Strong growth attracts investment and raises demand for the currency.
- **Capital flight.** A loss of confidence produces a large and rapid outflow, sharply increasing the supply of the currency and causing a steep depreciation. It is self-reinforcing, because the fall itself prompts further outflows.
- **Expectations and speculation.** Traders buy a currency they expect to rise, which makes it rise. Speculative flows dwarf trade flows in volume, which is why short-run movements often seem disconnected from economic fundamentals.
- **Global factors**, such as a fall in commodity prices, which depreciates the currencies of commodity exporters, or a shift in global risk appetite, which moves money towards currencies regarded as safe.

## Impact of a change in the exchange rate

Take a **depreciation** and work through each effect the specification names.

<Chunk title="The current account: Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve" open>

A depreciation makes exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency. **Whether the current account improves depends on elasticities.**

<Formula name="Marshall-Lerner condition" note="If the sum of the two elasticities is less than 1, a depreciation makes the current account WORSE, because the price effect on imports outweighs the volume responses.">
PED for exports + PED for imports > 1
</Formula>

**Why.** The import bill in domestic currency rises immediately because prices have risen. That is offset only if volumes respond enough: export volumes rising and import volumes falling. If demand for both is very inelastic, the higher import prices dominate and the account deteriorates.

<Figure caption="The J-curve: the current account worsens before it improves">
<JCurveDiagram />
</Figure>

**The J-curve** explains the timing. Immediately after a depreciation, volumes cannot adjust: contracts are already signed, orders already placed, supply chains fixed, and consumers have not yet switched. So prices change while quantities do not, and the current account **worsens**. Over the following months and years, as export orders respond and importers find domestic substitutes, volumes adjust and the account **improves**, eventually moving above its starting point.

**The two concepts together give the complete answer:** the Marshall-Lerner condition states whether it will improve at all, and the J-curve states when. Elasticities are lower in the short run and higher in the long run, which is precisely why the condition may fail immediately and hold later.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="The other five effects">

**The capital and financial accounts.** Domestic assets become cheaper in foreign currency, so inward FDI and portfolio investment become more attractive, which is an inflow. **Against that**, a depreciating currency may signal weakness and prompt outflows, and foreign investors holding domestic assets have lost value in their own currency. The net effect depends on whether the depreciation is seen as a correction or as the start of a decline.

**Economic growth.** Exports rise and import substitution increases, so aggregate demand rises and output grows, with a multiplier effect. **Against that**, imported inputs and capital equipment cost more, which raises costs and can reduce investment, so the supply side may be harmed even as demand rises.

**Employment and unemployment.** Employment rises in export and import-competing industries. The effect is uneven, and industries dependent on imported inputs may cut employment.

**The rate of inflation.** Import prices rise directly, which is **cost-push** inflation, and it feeds through to the prices of finished goods and to imported inputs. Higher aggregate demand adds **demand-pull** pressure. Reduced competition from imports removes discipline on domestic producers' prices. **So a depreciation is inflationary**, and that is the central cost to set against the competitiveness gain. If the inflation is large enough it erodes the competitiveness gain entirely, returning the real exchange rate to where it started.

**FDI flows.** Cheaper assets attract inward FDI, and outward FDI becomes more expensive. Persistent volatility, however, deters FDI regardless of direction, because it makes returns unpredictable.

</Chunk>

## Competitive devaluation

Deliberately lowering the exchange rate to gain competitiveness, sometimes called a currency war.

**Why it is self-defeating:**

- **Competitiveness is relative**, so not every country can improve it simultaneously. One country's gain is another's loss.
- **Retaliation.** Other countries devalue in response, leaving relative rates roughly unchanged and everyone with higher import prices.
- **Inflation** rises in all the devaluing countries, so real incomes fall for no competitive gain.
- **It provokes protectionism**, since trading partners respond with tariffs against what they see as currency manipulation.
- **It removes the pressure to raise productivity**, which is the only durable source of competitiveness. A country that devalues repeatedly has substituted a currency adjustment for the structural reform it actually needs.
- **Confidence effects.** A country seen to be managing its currency down may face capital flight and higher borrowing costs.

**The judgement:** a devaluation can help an individual country with a genuinely overvalued currency, and as a general strategy it is a negative-sum game. That distinction is the point to make.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

The exchange rate regime matters unusually much for a small open economy, and the local case shows why.

**With almost all consumer goods, food, fuel and construction materials imported**, a depreciation feeds through into domestic prices very fast and very completely, because there are few domestic substitutes for import users to switch to. In elasticity terms, **demand for imports is highly inelastic**, so the Marshall-Lerner condition is unlikely to hold: a depreciation would raise the import bill without producing much volume adjustment.

**Meanwhile tourism revenue is earned largely in foreign currency**, so a depreciation raises the domestic currency value of earnings without necessarily raising visitor numbers, since prices are set in foreign currency in the first place.

**The conclusion that follows** is that exchange rate stability is worth more to such an economy than the competitiveness that a flexible rate might buy, which is a coherent economic argument for a fixed or managed regime and an excellent evaluation paragraph on exchange rate systems.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Definitions distinguishing devaluation from depreciation (2 marks); a diagram question on the currency market (4 marks); and a very common 20-mark essay: "Evaluate the likely effects of a depreciation of a country's currency" or "Assess the case for a fixed exchange rate."

**Structuring the depreciation essay, which is the highest-value template in this topic:**

1. **Define depreciation** and state the cause from the source.
2. **Draw the currency market diagram** and explain the shift in demand or supply.
3. **Effect one, the current account**, chained: exports cheaper abroad so volumes rise, imports dearer so volumes fall, therefore the trade balance improves. **Then immediately qualify it** with the Marshall-Lerner condition and the J-curve, and **draw the J-curve**.
4. **Effect two, growth and employment**, chained: higher net exports raise AD, so output and employment rise with a multiplier effect. **Use an AD/AS diagram.**
5. **Effect three, inflation**, chained: import prices rise, giving cost-push inflation, which erodes the competitiveness gain and reduces real incomes.
6. **Effect four, FDI and the financial account**, briefly.
7. **Evaluation:** elasticities, the time lag, the size of the depreciation, the degree of spare capacity in the economy, the import dependence of production, and whether the movement is permanent or temporary.
8. **Conclude** with a judgement and a condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve together** answer whether and when the current account improves.
- **Elasticities are lower in the short run**, so effects grow over time.
- **A depreciation is inflationary**, and enough inflation erases the competitiveness gain entirely.
- **It depends on spare capacity.** With no spare capacity, higher export demand produces inflation rather than output.
- **It depends on import dependence.** A country whose production relies on imported inputs gains much less.
- **Competitive devaluation is a negative-sum game**, because competitiveness is relative.
- **A depreciation substitutes for productivity improvement**, which is the only durable source of competitiveness.
- **Volatility deters FDI regardless of direction.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using devaluation and depreciation interchangeably.** One is policy, the other is the market.
- **Claiming a depreciation always improves the current account.** State the Marshall-Lerner condition.
- **Omitting the J-curve** when discussing the timing.
- **Forgetting the inflation cost**, which is the main argument against depreciation.
- **Drawing a diagram and not referring to it** in the text.
- **Treating higher interest rates as only a domestic policy.** They move the exchange rate too.
- **Assuming PPP determines the rate in the short run.** Capital flows dominate.
- **Ignoring spare capacity** when analysing the growth effect.
- **Saying a country can devalue its way to competitiveness** without noting retaliation and inflation.

</Mistakes>
