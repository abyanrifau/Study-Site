## The components

<Figure caption="The structure of the balance of payments">
<BalanceOfPaymentsDiagram />
</Figure>

**The current account has four parts:**

1. **Trade in goods**, sometimes called visible trade.
2. **Trade in services**, sometimes called invisible trade: tourism, transport, financial and business services.
3. **Primary income:** profit, interest, dividends and wages earned abroad, minus those paid abroad. **Repatriated profits of foreign-owned firms are a debit here**, which is why FDI has a permanent negative effect on this line.
4. **Secondary income:** transfers with nothing given in return, chiefly aid and **workers' remittances**, which are a major credit for several developing economies.

**Goods plus services is the balance of trade.** The current account is the balance of trade **plus** the two income balances, and a country can run a trade deficit and a current account surplus if income flows are large enough.

**The capital account** is small: transfers of non-financial assets and debt forgiveness.

**The financial account** records investment flows: FDI, portfolio investment, other investment such as bank lending, and changes in official reserves.

## Why the accounts sum to zero

This is the concept most often misunderstood and it earns marks.

**A current account deficit means the country is buying more from abroad than it sells.** The difference must be paid for, and it can only be paid for by selling assets or borrowing. Both are recorded as **credits on the financial account**. So a current account deficit is necessarily matched by a financial account surplus, which is an inflow of investment or borrowing.

**The implication worth stating:** a deficit is not automatically a problem, because it depends on **how it is financed**. Financed by long-term FDI into productive capacity, it may be entirely sustainable and even desirable in a developing economy building infrastructure. Financed by short-term borrowing or hot money, it is fragile: the inflow can reverse suddenly, forcing a sharp depreciation and a contraction. **How a deficit is financed matters more than its size**, and that sentence is worth learning.

<Worked title="Worked example">

| | $bn |
| --- | --- |
| Exports of goods | 118 |
| Imports of goods | (156) |
| Exports of services | 64 |
| Imports of services | (41) |
| Primary income balance | (14) |
| Secondary income balance | 9 |

`Trade in goods = 118 − 156 = −$38bn`

`Trade in services = 64 − 41 = +$23bn`

`Balance of trade = −38 + 23 = −$15bn`

`Current account = −15 − 14 + 9 = −$20bn`

With GDP of **$420bn**:

`Current account as a share of GDP = (20 ÷ 420) × 100 = 4.8% of GDP`

**Interpretation, which is where the marks are.** The deficit is 4.8% of GDP, which is large enough to matter. **The structure of it is more informative than the total:** a goods deficit of $38bn is partly offset by a services surplus of $23bn, so the economy is competitive in services and not in goods. The primary income deficit of $14bn suggests substantial foreign ownership of domestic assets, with profits flowing out. The secondary income surplus of $9bn suggests significant remittances or aid inflows. **A deficit of 4.8% financed by FDI is manageable; financed by short-term flows it is not**, and the answer should say which it would want to know.

</Worked>

## Causes of deficits and surpluses

<Chunk title="Causes of a current account deficit" open>

- **Poor international competitiveness:** low productivity, high unit labour costs, poor quality or weak non-price factors. This is the structural cause and the hardest to fix.
- **A strong exchange rate**, which makes exports dear and imports cheap.
- **High domestic demand.** Strong growth raises spending, and some of that spending goes on imports. A deficit therefore often accompanies a boom, which means it can be a symptom of strength rather than weakness.
- **Low savings relative to investment.** Since the current account balance equals national saving minus investment, a country investing more than it saves must import capital, which shows as a current account deficit. This is the most analytically powerful way to express it.
- **High relative inflation**, which erodes price competitiveness.
- **Rising import prices**, particularly of oil and food, for a commodity importer.
- **Declining industries**, which reduce export capacity over time.
- **Large foreign ownership**, producing a persistent primary income deficit.
- **Trade barriers abroad** restricting export markets.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Causes of a current account surplus">

- **High competitiveness** and strong export performance.
- **A weak or undervalued exchange rate.**
- **High savings and weak domestic demand**, which is the mirror image of the deficit case. Several large surpluses reflect suppressed domestic consumption rather than exceptional competitiveness.
- **Large income inflows** from investments held abroad.
- **Commodity exports** during a price boom.

**A surplus is not automatically good either.** It may indicate weak domestic demand and under-consumption; it may reflect an undervalued currency that reduces living standards by making imports expensive; and it contributes to the global imbalances discussed below.

</Chunk>

## Measures to reduce an imbalance

<Chunk title="Three categories" open>

**Expenditure reducing policies** lower total domestic demand, so import spending falls with it: higher interest rates, higher taxes, lower government spending.

- **For:** direct and reasonably fast.
- **Against:** it reduces output and employment as well as imports, so the current account improves at the cost of a recession. It does nothing about competitiveness, so the improvement lasts only while demand is suppressed.

**Expenditure switching policies** shift spending from imports to domestic goods: depreciation of the currency, tariffs and quotas.

- **For:** it does not require a contraction in demand.
- **Against:** depreciation raises import prices, which is inflationary and reduces real incomes; its effect depends on the **Marshall-Lerner condition** and is subject to the **J-curve** lag; and protection invites retaliation and is restricted by trade agreements.

**Supply-side policies** improve competitiveness so exports rise for structural reasons: education and training, infrastructure investment, incentives for research and investment, deregulation, and measures to raise productivity.

- **For:** it addresses the root cause and raises output rather than reducing it. It is the only approach that produces a permanent improvement.
- **Against:** it works over years rather than months, it is expensive, and the outcome is uncertain.

**The standard evaluative structure:** expenditure reducing and switching treat the symptom quickly and at a cost; supply-side measures treat the cause slowly. A serious answer recommends the supply-side route for the long run while acknowledging that a country facing an immediate financing crisis has to act faster than that allows.

</Chunk>

## Global trade imbalances

**The significance** is that large persistent surpluses in some economies mirror large persistent deficits in others, and the flows financing them are very large.

**Why they matter:**

- **The financial flows recycled from surplus to deficit countries** can depress interest rates and encourage excessive borrowing in the deficit countries, which contributed to the conditions before the 2008 financial crisis.
- **Deficit countries build up external liabilities**, which must eventually be serviced out of future income.
- **They create political pressure for protection**, since deficits are blamed on trading partners' policies, particularly currency management.
- **Sudden adjustment is disruptive.** If capital inflows into a deficit country stop, the currency falls sharply and demand must contract abruptly.
- **Surplus countries are not neutral.** A surplus built on suppressed domestic consumption exports demand deficiency to the rest of the world.

**The balanced judgement:** imbalances are a problem when they reflect distortions such as currency manipulation, suppressed consumption or unsustainable borrowing, and not when they reflect genuine differences in demographics, development stage and investment opportunities. A young, fast-growing economy importing capital to build infrastructure **should** run a deficit.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A calculation of the current account or the balance of trade (4 marks in Section B), an explanation of a cause (4 marks), and a 20-mark essay on how a country should reduce its current account deficit, or on whether a deficit matters.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Work down the components in order**, keeping debits negative.
2. **Give the balance of trade and the current account separately** if both are asked for.
3. **Express the deficit as a share of GDP** if GDP is given. This is what makes it interpretable.
4. **Comment on the structure**, not just the total: which component is in deficit, and what that suggests.

**Structuring the essay on reducing a deficit:**

1. **Establish the size and structure** from the data, as a share of GDP.
2. **Identify the cause** from the evidence. The cause determines the appropriate policy, and this step is what separates the top band.
3. **Policy one, expenditure reducing**, chained: higher interest rates reduce consumption and investment, which reduces import spending, which narrows the deficit; and output and employment fall.
4. **Policy two, depreciation**, chained through the Marshall-Lerner condition and the J-curve, with the inflation cost.
5. **Policy three, supply-side**, chained: training raises productivity, which lowers unit labour costs, which raises export competitiveness permanently.
6. **Evaluate:** short run against long run, symptom against cause, and the cost of each policy in terms of other objectives.
7. **Conclude** with a prioritised recommendation and the condition it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **How a deficit is financed matters more than its size.** FDI is sustainable; hot money is not.
- **A deficit can be a symptom of strong growth** rather than of weakness.
- **The current account balance equals saving minus investment**, so it is as much about domestic saving as about trade.
- **A surplus is not automatically good**, and may reflect suppressed consumption.
- **Expenditure reducing improves the account by causing a recession.**
- **Only supply-side policy addresses the cause**, and it takes years.
- **There is a conflict between macroeconomic objectives**, so improving the current account usually costs growth, employment or price stability.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Treating the current account as only the trade balance.** It includes primary and secondary income.
- **Putting FDI in the current account.** It is a financial account flow; the profits it generates are primary income.
- **Saying the accounts do not balance.** They must sum to zero by construction.
- **Assuming a deficit is always harmful** without considering how it is financed.
- **Recommending a depreciation without the Marshall-Lerner condition** or the J-curve.
- **Recommending protection without noting retaliation** and trade agreement constraints.
- **Quoting a deficit in money terms with no reference to GDP.**
- **Recommending a policy without identifying the cause of the deficit first.**

</Mistakes>
