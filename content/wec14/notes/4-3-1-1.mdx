## Trade as a proportion of GDP

<Formula name="Openness" note="Exports plus imports, not exports minus imports. This measures the SIZE of trade flows relative to the economy, not the balance between them.">
Openness = ((Exports + Imports) ÷ GDP) × 100
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example">

A country has GDP of **$420bn**, exports of **$118bn** and imports of **$146bn**.

`Openness = ((118 + 146) ÷ 420) × 100 = (264 ÷ 420) × 100 = 62.9%`

`Trade balance = 118 − 146 = −$28bn, a deficit`

**The two figures answer different questions.** Openness of 62.9% says the economy is heavily integrated into world trade, so it is exposed to global demand shocks and benefits from global growth. The deficit of $28bn says nothing about openness; it says imports exceed exports. Confusing the two is the commonest error in this topic.

**Interpreting openness:** it is typically high for small economies, which cannot produce everything, and lower for very large ones, which can trade internally. So a figure of 62.9% is unremarkable for a small economy and would be striking for a very large one. Comparison must allow for size.

</Worked>

## TNCs and FDI

**The increased importance of TNCs** is measured by their share of world output, employment and trade. A substantial proportion of world trade is now **intra-firm**: components and services moving between subsidiaries of the same company. That has two consequences worth remembering. It makes trade statistics harder to interpret, because a good crossing three borders during assembly is counted three times. And it creates the opportunity for **transfer pricing**, since the prices at which a company trades with itself are set internally.

**FDI against portfolio investment.** FDI is investment in productive assets with a controlling interest: a factory, an office, an acquisition. Portfolio investment is the purchase of shares or bonds for a financial return without control. **The distinction matters** because FDI is long-term, brings technology and management, and is hard to withdraw quickly, whereas portfolio investment is mobile and can leave overnight, which is the capital flight problem in **4.3.6.2**.

**Reasons for FDI** are developed in **4.3.1.2**: market access, lower costs, resources, avoiding trade barriers, and acquiring brands or technology.

## Migration

Migration is the third characteristic and the one most often left out of answers.

**Effects on the receiving country:** a larger labour force and higher total output; filling of skill shortages and of low-paid vacancies; possible downward pressure on wages in the occupations most affected; a younger population, which helps with an ageing dependency ratio; higher demand for housing and public services; and higher tax revenue from those in work.

**Effects on the sending country:** **remittances**, which for several economies exceed the value of aid received and are recorded as secondary income on the current account; reduced unemployment; and the loss of skilled workers, which is the **brain drain**, particularly damaging when it removes doctors, nurses, teachers and engineers whose training the country paid for.

**The balanced point:** migration raises world output, because labour moves to where it is more productive, and its costs and benefits are distributed very unevenly between and within countries. That distributional point is the one that earns evaluation marks.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A compact illustration of all three characteristics in one small economy.

**Openness** is extremely high: almost all food, fuel, construction materials and consumer goods are imported, while export earnings come from tourism and fish. Exports plus imports form a very large share of GDP, which means global fuel prices and global travel demand transmit directly into the domestic economy with very little to absorb the shock.

**TNCs and FDI** are central: international hotel groups own or operate a large share of resort capacity, so FDI is the mechanism by which the main industry was built, and profit repatriation is a permanent feature of the current account.

**Migration** runs in both directions: expatriate workers fill roles in construction and hospitality, while some skilled Maldivians work abroad. The lesson that generalises is that **for a small open economy, globalisation is not a policy choice but the environment**, which is a useful framing for an essay on a small economy.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** As a multiple-choice question in Section A; as a 2-mark definition or a 4-mark calculation in Section B; and as background in essays where you need to establish how globalised an economy is before analysing the effects.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Read what is being asked.** Openness uses exports **plus** imports; the trade balance uses exports **minus** imports.
2. **Check the units.** Billions against millions is an easy way to lose the mark.
3. **State the answer as a percentage with one decimal place** and say what it means in a short sentence.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Openness and the trade balance measure different things**, and the distinction is worth stating explicitly.
- **Small economies are naturally more open**, so comparisons must allow for size.
- **FDI is long-term and hard to withdraw; portfolio investment can leave overnight.** That distinction drives the vulnerability of an economy to capital flight.
- **Intra-firm trade inflates trade statistics** and creates the scope for transfer pricing.
- **Migration raises world output and distributes its costs and benefits unevenly.**
- **For a small open economy, globalisation is the environment rather than a choice.**

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using exports minus imports** to measure openness.
- **Confusing FDI with portfolio investment.**
- **Defining globalisation as just more trade**, which omits investment, migration and information flows.
- **Forgetting migration** as one of the three named characteristics.
- **Comparing openness between a very small and a very large economy** without noting that size drives the figure.
- **Treating remittances as part of the trade balance.** They are secondary income on the current account.
- **Quoting a percentage with no interpretation.**

</Mistakes>
