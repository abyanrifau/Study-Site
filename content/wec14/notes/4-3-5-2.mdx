## Direct and indirect taxes

**Direct taxes** are levied on income, profit or wealth: income tax, corporation tax, national insurance or social security contributions, capital gains tax, inheritance tax, property taxes.

**Indirect taxes** are levied on spending: VAT or a general sales tax, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, and import tariffs.

| | Direct | Indirect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Levied on** | income and wealth | spending |
| **Usually** | progressive | regressive |
| **Incentive effect** | on work, saving and investment | on the pattern of consumption |
| **Avoidable?** | harder to avoid for employees, easier for the wealthy and for firms | avoidable by not buying the good |
| **Visibility** | visible on a payslip | hidden in the price |
| **Administration** | needs records and enforcement capacity | easier to collect |

**Why developing countries rely more on indirect taxes:** collecting income tax requires records, formal employment and administrative capacity, none of which is available where much activity is informal. Indirect taxes and tariffs are collected at a smaller number of points. **The consequence is that the tax system in a poorer country tends to be less progressive**, not because of a policy preference but because of administrative capacity, and that is a good point in an inequality or development essay.

## Progressive, proportional and regressive

<Worked title="Worked example">

An income tax has a tax-free allowance of **$10,000**, then **20%** up to $40,000, then **40%** above that.

**Person A earns $25,000:**

`Taxable income = 25,000 − 10,000 = $15,000`

`Tax = 15,000 × 20% = $3,000`

`Average rate = (3,000 ÷ 25,000) × 100 = 12%`

**Person B earns $80,000:**

`Taxable = 80,000 − 10,000 = $70,000`

`Tax = (30,000 × 20%) + (40,000 × 40%) = 6,000 + 16,000 = $22,000`

`Average rate = (22,000 ÷ 80,000) × 100 = 27.5%`

**The average rate rises from 12% to 27.5% as income rises, so the tax is progressive.** Note that the **marginal** rate for B is 40% while the **average** rate is 27.5%: the marginal rate determines the incentive effect, and the average rate determines the burden. Confusing the two is a very common error.

**Now an indirect tax.** A 15% sales tax on all spending. Person A spends all $25,000 and pays $3,750, an average rate of **15% of income**. Person B spends $50,000 of the $80,000 and saves the rest, paying $7,500, an average rate of **9.4% of income**.

**The indirect tax is regressive**, because the poorest spend a larger proportion of their income. That arithmetic is the clearest way to demonstrate regressivity and is worth being able to reproduce.

</Worked>

## Economic effects of changes in tax rates

<Chunk title="Incentives to work" open>

**The standard argument:** a cut in income tax raises the reward from an extra hour worked, so people work more, participation rises and the unemployed have a stronger incentive to take work.

**The complications, which are where the marks are:**

- **Income and substitution effects pull in opposite directions.** A tax cut raises the reward per hour, which encourages more work, and it also raises income for the same hours, which may encourage people to take more leisure. Which dominates is an empirical question, and for higher earners the income effect can dominate.
- **Many people cannot choose their hours**, so the incentive has no channel through which to operate.
- **What matters is the marginal rate**, and specifically the combined effect of tax and benefit withdrawal. A low-income household losing benefits as earnings rise can face an effective marginal rate far above that of a high earner, which is the **poverty trap**.
- **Non-financial motivations** matter: career, status and satisfaction.

</Chunk>

At a rate of **0%** revenue is zero. At **100%** revenue is also zero, because nobody works for nothing. Between the two, revenue rises and then falls, so there is a **revenue-maximising rate**, t*.

**Why revenue falls beyond t*:** reduced work effort and participation, greater tax avoidance and evasion, emigration of high earners, relocation of business profit, and a shift of activity into the informal economy.

**The policy implication:** if the current rate is **above** t*, cutting it raises revenue. **If it is below t*, cutting it reduces revenue**, and that is the case in most countries most of the time.

**The evaluation, which matters more than the diagram:**

- **Nobody knows where t* is.** Estimates vary widely, and it differs by tax, by country, by how mobile the tax base is, and over time.
- **It is much lower for highly mobile bases**, such as corporate profit and the incomes of very high earners, than for the wages of ordinary employees, who cannot relocate. So the Laffer argument is far stronger for corporation tax than for income tax.
- **It is used politically to justify tax cuts** that in practice reduced revenue.
- **Better enforcement shifts the whole curve up**, so the choice is not only about rates.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="The other five effects">

**Income distribution.** More progressive direct taxes reduce inequality, particularly when combined with transfers. Indirect taxes raise it, unless necessities are exempt or zero-rated. **The evaluative point:** the distributional effect of the tax system cannot be judged from the taxes alone; it depends on what the revenue is spent on. A regressive tax funding services used mainly by the poorest can be redistributive overall.

**Real output and employment.** Lower taxes raise disposable income and therefore consumption, and lower corporation tax raises retained profit and therefore investment, so AD rises and output and employment rise. Supply-side effects on incentives raise AS. **Against that**, lower taxes mean either lower public spending, which reduces AD directly, or higher borrowing. The net effect depends on the multiplier and on spare capacity.

**The price level.** An increase in an indirect tax raises prices **directly**, and it is a one-off increase in the price level rather than continuing inflation unless it triggers wage responses. A cut in direct taxes raises demand, which is inflationary if the economy is near capacity.

**The trade balance.** Lower direct taxes raise disposable income, some of which is spent on imports, so the trade balance worsens. Lower corporation tax may raise investment and productivity, improving competitiveness over time. Indirect taxes fall on imports and domestic goods alike, so they are broadly neutral between them, whereas a tariff is not.

**FDI flows.** Lower corporation tax attracts FDI, which is why tax competition between countries exists and why a race to the bottom on corporate tax rates developed. **The counter-arguments:** investors also weigh infrastructure, skills and political stability, all of which need funding; the revenue lost may exceed the investment gained; and investment attracted purely by a low rate is footloose. International agreement on a minimum effective rate is the collective response, from **4.3.5.4**.

</Chunk>

## Principles of a good tax system

Useful for evaluating any proposed change: it should be **equitable**, both horizontally, treating like cases alike, and vertically, according to ability to pay; **efficient**, minimising distortion of behaviour; **certain and simple** to understand and comply with; **cheap to collect**; **flexible**, so it can be adjusted; and **convenient** to pay. A tax that scores well on revenue and badly on equity and simplicity is a real trade-off, and naming which principles conflict is a good evaluative structure.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** A calculation of average or marginal rates, or of whether a tax is progressive (4–6 marks); Explain (4) on one effect; and a 20-mark essay on the effects of a change in direct or indirect taxation, or on whether a government should shift from direct to indirect taxes.

**Method for the calculation:**

1. **Apply each band to the income within it**, not the whole income to the top rate.
2. **Deduct the allowance first.**
3. **Calculate the average rate for at least two income levels**, and compare them to classify the tax.
4. **Distinguish marginal from average**, and say which is relevant to the question asked.

**Structuring "should the country shift from direct to indirect taxes?":**

1. **State the current structure** from the data.
2. **The case for indirect taxes**, chained: easier to collect, harder to evade, does not directly reduce work incentives, and can be targeted at goods with negative externalities.
3. **The case against**, chained: regressive, so inequality and relative poverty rise; it raises the price level directly; and demand for necessities is inelastic so the burden cannot be avoided.
4. **Bring in the Laffer curve** where the question concerns direct tax rates, and evaluate its use rather than just drawing it.
5. **Evaluate:** administrative capacity, the stage of development, what the revenue funds, and whether necessities are exempt.
6. **Conclude** with a judgement and a condition.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Marginal and average rates do different work:** marginal drives incentives, average drives burden.
- **Income and substitution effects pull in opposite directions**, so a tax cut need not raise hours worked.
- **The effective marginal rate at the bottom can exceed that at the top**, because of benefit withdrawal.
- **Nobody knows where t* is**, and it is lower for mobile tax bases.
- **The distributional effect depends on what the revenue funds**, not only on who pays.
- **An indirect tax rise is a one-off price level increase**, not continuing inflation.
- **Indirect taxes can be made less regressive** by exempting necessities.
- **Developing countries rely on indirect taxes for capacity reasons**, not by preference.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Applying the top rate to the whole income** instead of banding it.
- **Confusing marginal with average rates.**
- **Calling a tax progressive because the amount paid rises with income.** It must rise as a **proportion**.
- **Claiming all indirect taxes are regressive** without noting that exempting necessities changes this.
- **Drawing the Laffer curve without evaluating it**, or asserting that a tax cut raises revenue without establishing that the rate is above t*.
- **Confusing avoidance with evasion.** One is lawful, the other is not.
- **Forgetting that lower taxes mean lower spending or higher borrowing.**
- **Judging the distributional effect of a tax in isolation** from the spending it funds.

</Mistakes>
