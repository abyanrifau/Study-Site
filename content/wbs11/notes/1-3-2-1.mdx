## Movement or shift?

Get this right and most of the topic follows.

- The **price of the good itself** changes → you **move along** the existing demand curve. The curve does not go anywhere.
- **Anything else** changes → the whole curve **shifts**.

Say it in the exam in those words. "The curve shifts right, so more is demanded at every price" is worth more than "demand goes up".

## What shifts demand

The specification lists seven. Learn them as a list, because a question may ask you to pick the one that applies.

<Chunk title="Prices of substitutes and complementary goods" open>

**Substitutes** are alternatives. If the price of one brand of coffee falls, demand for a rival brand falls, because customers switch. Its curve shifts left.

**Complements** are bought together. If the price of smartphones falls, demand for phone cases and data plans rises. Their curves shift right.

The trap: it is the price of the *other* good that shifts your curve. A change in your own price never does.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Consumer incomes">

Higher incomes mean more spending power. For most goods, called **normal goods**, demand shifts right as income rises.

For a few, called **inferior goods**, demand shifts *left* as incomes rise, because customers trade up to something better. Bus travel and own-brand basics are the standard examples.

This is measured by income elasticity of demand, covered in **1.3.2.5**.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Fashions, tastes and preferences">

Tastes change, sometimes fast. Rising health awareness shifted demand for sugary drinks left and demand for bottled water and low-sugar alternatives right across many countries over about a decade.

Fashion-driven demand is the most dangerous kind to depend on, because it can reverse with no warning and no reason you can act on.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Marketing, advertising and branding">

This is the one shifter a business directly controls. Effective advertising shifts the curve right and can also make demand less price sensitive, because a loyal customer is less willing to switch on price.

It is not free, and it does not always work. A campaign that costs more than the extra profit it generates has shifted the curve right and made the business worse off.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Demographics">

The structure of the population. An ageing population shifts demand for healthcare, mobility aids and cruises right, and demand for baby products left. Rapid urbanisation shifts demand for convenience food and small flats right.

Demographic change is slow but extremely predictable, which makes it the easiest shifter to plan for.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="External shocks">

Unexpected events outside the firm's control: a pandemic, a war, a natural disaster, a sudden currency collapse, a border closing. They can shift demand sharply in either direction, and by definition they cannot be forecast.

This links straight back to **uncertainty** in 1.3.1.1. A shock is uncertainty, not risk.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Seasonality">

A regular annual pattern. Ice cream in summer, umbrellas in the monsoon, gifts before a major festival, tourism in the dry season.

Unlike a shock, seasonality is predictable, so it can be managed: flexible staffing, stock built up in advance, off-season discounts, or a second product with the opposite season.

</Chunk>

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Tourist demand is strongly **seasonal**. The dry north-east monsoon season attracts far more visitors than the wet season, and the pattern repeats every year.

Because it is predictable, it is a risk rather than an uncertainty, and resorts manage it: rates rise in peak season, staff are hired on seasonal contracts, and maintenance is scheduled for the quiet months.

Now contrast that with an **external shock**. When global travel stopped in 2020, demand did not dip seasonally, it collapsed, and no amount of seasonal planning covered it. Same industry, two completely different kinds of demand change, and being able to tell them apart is exactly what an examiner is looking for.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Complements.** As smartphone prices fell across South Asia, demand for mobile data, power banks and screen protectors all shifted right.
- **Incomes.** Rising middle-class incomes in Indonesia and Vietnam shifted demand for branded packaged food right, while demand for the cheapest unbranded staples shifted left, behaving as inferior goods.
- **Demographics.** Japan's ageing population has shifted demand towards elderly care and away from schools and children's products, over decades rather than years.
- **Tastes.** Demand for plant-based milk has shifted right in Western Europe on the back of a change in preferences, not a change in its price.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.**

- **Define** (2 marks) — demand, substitute, complement, external shock.
- **Construct** (4 marks) — draw a demand curve and show a shift. Label the axes **Price** and **Quantity**, label the original curve **D** and the new one **D₁**, and show the direction clearly. Unlabelled axes cost marks.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — why a named factor changes demand for this business. One factor, one clear chain.
- **Analyse** (6 marks) — a chain of reasoning from the change through to the effect on the business, using the source data.

**Build the chain properly.** This is the pattern that earns analysis marks:

> Incomes in the region rose by 6% → the product is a normal good → demand shifts right → more is demanded at every price → the business can raise price or sell more volume → revenue rises → assuming it has the capacity to meet the extra demand.

Each arrow is a link. Six-mark answers want three or four links, in context, not a list of factors.

**Getting evaluation marks** in bigger questions: say how *large* the shift is likely to be, how *long* it will last, and whether the business can actually respond. A demand shift the firm cannot supply is worth very little to it.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing a movement with a shift.** The most punished error on this topic. A change in the good's own price moves you *along* the curve. Everything else shifts it.
- **Shifting the curve up instead of right.** For demand, "an increase" is a shift to the **right**. Describe it as right, not up.
- **Forgetting to label axes and curves.** Price on the vertical axis, Quantity on the horizontal, D and D₁ on the curves.
- **Getting substitutes and complements the wrong way round.** Substitute price falls → your demand falls. Complement price falls → your demand rises.
- **Assuming every good is normal.** Inferior goods move the opposite way when incomes change, and questions deliberately use them.
- **Treating seasonality as a shock.** Seasonality is predictable and can be planned for. A shock cannot.
- **Stopping the chain too early.** "Demand rises so revenue rises" is two links. Say why demand rises, what the curve does, and what the business can then do about it.

</Mistakes>
