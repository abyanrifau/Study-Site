## Why choices exist

Every business has finite resources: cash, time, staff, space, management attention. Wants are not finite. So resources have to be allocated, and allocating them to one thing means not allocating them to another.

That is the whole basis of the topic, and it is why opportunity cost appears in almost every evaluation.

## Opportunity cost

<Formula name="Opportunity cost" note="It is a benefit forgone, not a sum of money spent. The $40,000 is the cost; what you could have done with it is the opportunity cost.">
Opportunity cost = the value of the next best alternative given up
</Formula>

Three things to get right:

1. It is the **next best** alternative, not all the alternatives added together.
2. It is the **benefit forgone**, not the cash spent. Spending $40,000 on a delivery van has a financial cost of $40,000 and an opportunity cost of whatever the next best use of that $40,000 would have returned.
3. It applies to **time and attention** as well as money. A founder who spends three months opening a second site has not spent money on that time, and has given up whatever those three months could have achieved at the first site.

<Worked>

A guesthouse owner has **$40,000** available and three options:

| Option | Expected return |
| --- | --- |
| A. Refurbish four existing rooms | $12,000 a year |
| B. Add a small restaurant | $9,000 a year |
| C. Pay down the loan, saving interest | $3,400 a year |

**Choosing A** means giving up the best of the rest, which is B at **$9,000**. So the opportunity cost of refurbishing is $9,000 a year, not $12,400 and not $40,000.

**Reading the answer:** A is the better choice by $3,000 a year against its opportunity cost. But note what the numbers do not capture: B diversifies income so the business is less dependent on room nights, and C reduces risk by cutting debt. A choice can be right on return and wrong on risk, and saying so is exactly what an evaluation mark rewards.

</Worked>

## Trade-offs

A trade-off is accepting less of one thing for more of another. The common ones in this unit:

- **Price against volume.** Raise the price, sell fewer. That is PED in **1.3.2.4**.
- **Quality against cost.** Better materials cost more. That is the design mix in **1.3.3.2**.
- **Growth against control.** Taking investment or delegating means giving up some control.
- **Margin against market share.** Discounting buys share at the cost of margin.
- **Short run against long run.** Cutting training raises profit now and productivity falls later.
- **Profit against other objectives.** Employee welfare and social objectives both cost money.

Recognising which trade-off a question is really about tells you what to argue.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse owner with one successful property faces the clearest version of this. Opening a second on another island promises more revenue. The opportunity cost is not just the capital; it is the owner's own presence at the first property, which is what its reviews were built on.

That is a genuine trade-off between growth and control, and it explains why many owners deliberately stay at one property. It is not a lack of ambition; it is a judgement that the opportunity cost of their divided attention exceeds the extra revenue.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Rarely as a whole question. It appears as Define (2) or Explain (4), and far more often as **the tool that earns your evaluation marks everywhere else**.

**How to use it.** Whenever you recommend anything in any question, add one sentence on what the business gives up:

> "The business should invest the $40,000 in refurbishment, though the opportunity cost is the restaurant, which would have diversified its income away from room nights alone."

That single sentence is application, analysis and evaluation at once, and it works on almost every recommendation you will ever make in this paper.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Name the specific alternative given up**, using the source, not "they could have done something else".
- **The best financial return is not automatically the best choice.** Risk, diversification and the owner's objectives matter.
- **Time and attention are scarce resources too**, especially for a small business.
- **Some trade-offs reverse over time.** A choice that costs money now may pay later.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying the opportunity cost is the money spent.** The money is the financial cost. The opportunity cost is the benefit forgone.
- **Adding up all the alternatives.** It is only the next best one.
- **Forgetting time as a resource.** A founder's attention is often the scarcest thing they have.
- **Naming a vague alternative.** Use the actual options in the source.
- **Assuming the highest return always wins.** Risk and the owner's objectives can override it.
- **Treating a trade-off as a mistake.** It is the normal condition of every business decision.

</Mistakes>
