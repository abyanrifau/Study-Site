## What PED measures

If you raise the price by 10%, do you lose 2% of your customers or 40% of them? PED puts a number on that.

<Formula name="Price elasticity of demand" note="Work out both percentage changes first, then divide. Do not try to do it in one step.">
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

% change = (Change ÷ Original value) × 100
</Formula>

PED is almost always **negative**, because price up means quantity down. Everyone in practice ignores the minus sign and compares the size of the number to 1.

| Value (ignoring the sign) | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity responds a lot. Customers are price sensitive. |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary | Quantity changes by the same proportion as price. |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity barely responds. Customers are not price sensitive. |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity does not change at all. |

<Worked title="Worked example 1: calculating PED">

A café raises the price of a coffee from **$4.00** to **$4.40**. Sales fall from **500** a week to **440** a week.

**Step 1 — percentage change in price**

`Change = $4.40 − $4.00 = +$0.40`

`(0.40 ÷ 4.00) × 100 = +10%`

**Step 2 — percentage change in quantity demanded**

`Change = 440 − 500 = −60`

`(−60 ÷ 500) × 100 = −12%`

**Step 3 — divide**

`PED = −12 ÷ +10 = −1.2`

**Interpretation:** ignoring the sign, 1.2 is greater than 1, so demand is **price elastic**. A 1% rise in price causes a 1.2% fall in quantity demanded. Customers here are price sensitive, which suggests close substitutes nearby.

</Worked>

## PED and total revenue

This is the part that matters to a business, and the part examiners push hardest.

<Formula name="Total revenue">
Total revenue = Selling price × Quantity sold
</Formula>

A price rise pulls revenue two ways: more money per unit, but fewer units. Which wins depends entirely on PED.

| If demand is | Raising price | Lowering price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inelastic** (0 to 1) | Revenue **rises** | Revenue **falls** |
| **Elastic** (above 1) | Revenue **falls** | Revenue **rises** |
| **Unitary** (= 1) | Revenue unchanged | Revenue unchanged |

The rule in one line: **when demand is inelastic, put prices up. When it is elastic, be very careful about putting prices up.**

<Worked title="Worked example 2: does the price rise pay?">

Take the café above.

**Revenue before:** `500 × $4.00 = $2,000 a week`

**Revenue after:** `440 × $4.40 = $1,936 a week`

Revenue **fell by $64 a week**, which is what a PED of 1.2 predicts: demand was elastic, so raising price cost more in lost volume than it gained per cup.

**But now consider profit.** Suppose each coffee costs **$1.50** to make.

**Profit before:** `500 × ($4.00 − $1.50) = 500 × $2.50 = $1,250`

**Profit after:** `440 × ($4.40 − $1.50) = 440 × $2.90 = $1,276`

**Profit rose by $26**, even though revenue fell. Selling 60 fewer coffees also saved 60 lots of ingredients.

**This is the highest-value point in the whole topic.** Revenue and profit are not the same thing, and an elastic price rise can still be the right decision. Very few students make this point, and it moves an answer into the top band.

</Worked>

## What determines PED

<Chunk title="The factors" open>

- **Availability of close substitutes.** The single biggest factor. Lots of easy alternatives means elastic demand.
- **Whether it is a necessity or a luxury.** Necessities tend to be inelastic; people buy them anyway. Luxuries are elastic.
- **Brand strength and loyalty.** A strong brand makes demand less elastic, which is precisely why businesses invest in branding.
- **Proportion of income spent on it.** A cheap item is inelastic because a price rise is barely noticed. A big purchase is elastic.
- **Habit or addiction.** Habitual purchases are inelastic.
- **Time period.** Demand is more inelastic in the short run. Given time, customers find alternatives, so demand becomes more elastic.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Why it matters to a business">

- **Pricing strategy.** Inelastic demand supports skimming and premium pricing. Elastic demand pushes you towards competitive or penetration pricing.
- **Passing on cost rises.** If an indirect tax or a supplier price rise hits you, inelastic demand lets you pass most of it to customers. Elastic demand means you absorb it and lose margin.
- **Where to invest.** Branding, USPs and differentiation all exist partly to make demand less elastic.
- **Forecasting.** PED lets a business estimate the volume effect of a planned price change before making it.

</Chunk>

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

**Inelastic:** bottled water and basic groceries on a local island with one or two shops. There is no realistic substitute and no alternative supplier within reach, so a price rise loses few sales. That is also why governments watch staple prices in small island economies closely.

**Elastic:** a mid-range guesthouse stay. A traveller comparing options online can see twenty near-identical alternatives and filter by price. Raise the nightly rate 15% and bookings can drop far more than 15%, because switching costs the customer nothing but a click.

**And a warning about applying the rule blindly.** A high-end resort has fairly inelastic demand, so the revenue rule says raise the price. But repeated increases can reposition the brand, invite new competitors and damage repeat bookings. PED describes what happens to demand now, not what happens to the business over five years.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Fuel.** Petrol demand is inelastic in the short run, since people still have to get to work. That is exactly why governments tax it: revenue stays high even as the price rises.
- **Tobacco.** Highly inelastic through habit and addiction, which is why excise duties raise substantial revenue but change consumption slowly.
- **Airline seats.** Very elastic on a route with several carriers. Price comparison sites have made demand for many products far more elastic than it was twenty years ago.
- **Salt.** A classic inelastic good: cheap, a necessity in cooking, tiny share of income, no substitute.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** This is one of the most heavily examined topics in WBS11, and there is nearly always a calculation.

- **Define** (2 marks) — PED, price elastic demand, total revenue.
- **Calculate** (4 marks) — PED from a table, or the revenue effect of a price change. Write the formula, work out both percentage changes, divide, state the answer **with the sign or with the word "elastic"**.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — why demand for this product is elastic or inelastic. Name a factor and apply it to this product.
- **Assess** (10 marks) — should this business raise its price? This is the big one.

**How to structure the Assess answer:**

1. Calculate or state the PED and say what it means in words.
2. Apply the revenue rule and give the figures.
3. **Then go further:** revenue is not profit. Work out the effect on profit if costs are given.
4. Evaluate: how reliable is the PED figure? Over what time period? What about the brand, competitor reaction and customer loyalty?
5. Conclude with a judgement.

**Getting evaluation marks.** The three best available points:

- **PED is estimated from the past.** It may not hold for a price change of a different size or in a different market condition.
- **PED rises over time.** Short-run inelastic can become long-run elastic as customers find substitutes.
- **Revenue is not profit.** The worked example above shows how an elastic price rise can still raise profit.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing the wrong way round.** It is **quantity over price**. Putting price on top is the most common arithmetic error on this topic.
- **Dividing the change by the new value.** Percentage change always divides by the **original** figure.
- **Comparing PED to zero instead of to 1.** A PED of −0.4 is inelastic; a PED of −3 is elastic. Both are negative. The cut-off is 1, not 0.
- **Calling a negative PED "inelastic" because it is negative.** Nearly all PEDs are negative. Ignore the sign and look at the size.
- **Forgetting to state what the number means.** A bare "−1.2" often loses the final mark. Add "so demand is price elastic".
- **Confusing revenue with profit.** Write the words out. Revenue = price × quantity. Profit takes costs off as well.
- **Saying an elastic price rise is always wrong.** It lowers revenue, but it may still raise profit, and that nuance is where the top marks are.
- **Treating PED as fixed.** It changes with the time period, with the size of the price change, and as competitors enter or leave.

</Mistakes>
