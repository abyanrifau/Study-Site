## What YED measures

Incomes in a country rise by 5%. Does demand for your product rise by 1%, by 15%, or actually fall? YED answers that.

<Formula name="Income elasticity of demand" note="Same method as PED: work out both percentage changes first, then divide. The difference is that here the SIGN carries the meaning, so never drop it.">
YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income

% change = (Change ÷ Original value) × 100
</Formula>

## Reading the answer

Two questions, in this order.

**First, the sign.**

<Figure caption="For a normal good, demand rises as income rises, so YED is positive. For an inferior good, demand falls as income rises, so YED is negative.">
  <YedDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Positive** → **normal good**. Income and demand move the same way. Most goods.
- **Negative** → **inferior good**. Income and demand move opposite ways, because as customers get richer they trade up to something better. Bus travel, own-brand basics, very cheap cuts of meat.

**Second, the size** (ignoring the sign).

| YED | Type | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above +1 | Normal, **income elastic** — a **luxury** | Demand rises faster than income. Highly exposed to the economic cycle. |
| 0 to +1 | Normal, **income inelastic** — a **necessity** | Demand rises, but more slowly than income. Fairly recession-proof. |
| Exactly 0 | Perfectly income inelastic | Demand does not respond to income at all. |
| Below 0 | **Inferior** | Demand falls as income rises. Does well in a recession. |

<Worked title="Worked example 1: a luxury">

Average incomes in a country rise by **4%**. A resort's bookings rise from **12,000** to **13,440** a year.

**Step 1 — percentage change in quantity**

`Change = 13,440 − 12,000 = +1,440`

`(1,440 ÷ 12,000) × 100 = +12%`

**Step 2 — percentage change in income:** given as **+4%**

**Step 3 — divide**

`YED = +12 ÷ +4 = +3.0`

**Interpretation:** positive, so a **normal good**. Greater than 1, so **income elastic**, which makes it a **luxury**. A 1% rise in income raises demand by 3%.

**What that means for the business:** it is three times as sensitive to the economy as incomes are. Excellent in a boom. Dangerous in a recession, because a 4% fall in incomes would cut bookings by about 12%.

</Worked>

<Worked title="Worked example 2: an inferior good">

Incomes rise by **5%**. Sales of a supermarket's cheapest own-brand rice fall from **40,000** bags to **38,000**.

**Step 1 — percentage change in quantity**

`Change = 38,000 − 40,000 = −2,000`

`(−2,000 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = −5%`

**Step 2 — percentage change in income:** **+5%**

**Step 3 — divide**

`YED = −5 ÷ +5 = −1.0`

**Interpretation:** negative, so an **inferior good**. Customers are trading up to branded rice as they get richer.

**Notice the trap:** the answer is −1.0. If you had thrown away the minus sign and just said "unitary", you would have completely missed that this is an inferior good. **The sign is the point.**

</Worked>

## What determines YED

- **Whether the good is a necessity or a luxury.** The main determinant. Nobody buys three times as much salt because they got a pay rise.
- **The income level of the customers.** A motorbike may be a luxury to a low-income household and an inferior good to a high-income one that would rather have a car. The same product can have a different YED in different segments.
- **Availability of a better alternative.** A good only becomes inferior if there is something to trade up *to*.
- **How much of income it takes up.** Big-ticket items tend to be income elastic.

## Why a business cares

- **Judging exposure to the economic cycle.** A high positive YED means the business will be hit hard by a downturn and will boom in an upturn.
- **Planning a product portfolio.** Holding products with different YEDs spreads the risk. A supermarket selling both a premium range and a value range is hedged in either direction, because the value range is where customers go in a recession.
- **Deciding where to expand.** A luxury business should target markets with rising incomes. A necessity is less dependent on that.
- **Forecasting.** If income growth is forecast at 3% and YED is +2, expect demand to rise about 6%.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Tourism is close to a pure **luxury** in YED terms: a long-haul holiday is one of the first things households cut when incomes fall, and one of the first things they add when incomes rise. So the Maldivian economy is unusually sensitive to income growth in its *source markets* rather than at home.

That produces a specific strategic problem. If most visitors come from a small number of wealthy countries, a recession in just those countries hits demand hard. **Spreading across more source markets is the same risk-reduction logic as holding products with different YEDs**, applied to geography instead of products.

And the guesthouse segment behaves differently from the resort segment. It is still a normal good, but less income elastic, because it serves budget travellers who are less likely to cancel the trip and more likely to trade down within it. In a downturn, guesthouses may take share from resorts even as the total market shrinks.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Luxury cars and designer goods.** Strongly income elastic. Sales in these categories swing far more than GDP does in both directions.
- **Staple food.** Rice, bread and cooking oil are income inelastic everywhere. Demand barely moves with income, which is why food retailers are relatively recession-resistant.
- **Inferior goods in practice.** Supermarket value ranges across Europe consistently gain share during recessions and lose it during recoveries, which is negative YED visible in real sales data.
- **Public transport.** Often an inferior good in higher-income countries, as households buy cars when they can afford to, but a normal good in lower-income countries where the alternative to the bus is walking.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.**

- **Define** (2 marks) — YED, normal good, inferior good, luxury.
- **Calculate** (4 marks) — YED from a table. Write the formula, both percentage changes, divide, and then **state what the sign and size mean**. That final sentence is usually a mark of its own.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — why demand for this product is income elastic, or why it is an inferior good.
- **Assess** (10 marks) — usually about how exposed the business is to a forecast change in incomes, and what it should do about it.

**Answer the "should it expand?" question like this:**

1. Calculate the YED and interpret it: normal or inferior, elastic or inelastic, luxury or necessity.
2. Apply it to the forecast in the source: if incomes are forecast to fall 3% and YED is +2.5, demand falls about 7.5%.
3. Say what that does to revenue and to capacity.
4. Evaluate, then conclude.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Income is only one factor.** Price, competitors, tastes and marketing all act at the same time. YED isolates one of them.
- **YED is backward-looking.** It is calculated from past data and may not hold through a large or sudden income change.
- **Which incomes?** For an exporter or a tourism business, the incomes that matter are in the source market, not the home market. Very few students spot this, and it scores well.
- **A high YED is not simply bad.** It means volatility, which cuts both ways. The judgement is about whether the business can survive the downswing.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dropping the minus sign.** With PED you can ignore it. With **YED you must not**, because the sign is what tells you normal from inferior. This is the single biggest error on this topic.
- **Dividing the wrong way round.** Quantity on top, income on the bottom.
- **Dividing by the new value.** Percentage change uses the **original** figure.
- **Saying an inferior good is a poor-quality good.** It is defined only by its negative YED, meaning demand falls as income rises. Nothing about quality.
- **Assuming every product has one YED.** It differs by segment and by income level, and the same product can be a luxury in one market and a necessity in another.
- **Confusing YED with PED.** PED is about the good's own price. YED is about consumer income. Papers deliberately supply the data for both.
- **Stopping at the number.** "+3.0" alone is incomplete. Say: positive so normal, above 1 so income elastic, therefore a luxury.

</Mistakes>
