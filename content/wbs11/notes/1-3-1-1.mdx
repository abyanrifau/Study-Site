## Mass markets and niche markets

Every market sits somewhere between two extremes.

A **mass market** sells one broadly similar product to as many people as possible. Think bottled water, mobile phone contracts or instant noodles. A **niche market** sells to a small group with a particular need. Think gluten-free bakery goods, left-handed guitars or diving holidays.

<Figure caption="Niche businesses accept fewer customers in exchange for a higher profit on each sale. Mass-market businesses accept a thin profit per unit because they sell so many.">
  <MassNicheDiagram />
</Figure>

<Chunk title="Mass market: strengths and weaknesses" open>

**Strengths**

- Huge potential sales volume, so high total revenue.
- Producing in bulk lowers the cost per unit, which is **economies of scale**.
- Easier to get stocked by large retailers.

**Weaknesses**

- Heavy competition, so prices get pushed down.
- Large advertising budgets are needed just to stay visible.
- Little chance to charge a premium, so profit per unit is thin.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Niche market: strengths and weaknesses">

**Strengths**

- Customers with specific needs will pay **premium prices**, so profit per unit is high.
- Less competition, at least at first.
- Marketing can be tightly focused, so it costs less and wastes less.
- A small firm can survive comfortably in a niche that is too small to interest a giant.

**Weaknesses**

- The market is small, so total sales are capped.
- All the eggs are in one basket. If tastes move, there is no fallback.
- Success attracts attention. A large rival can enter and outspend you.
- Few economies of scale, so the cost per unit stays high.

</Chunk>

A common trap: **niche does not mean small business**. A large company can run a niche brand alongside its mass-market ones, and often does, because the niche brand carries a much better margin.

### Where brands fit

A **brand** is what makes one product recognisable against near-identical rivals. In a mass market, branding is often the only real point of difference, which is why so much is spent on it. In a niche, the brand signals that the product genuinely understands a specific need.

Branding is covered properly in **1.3.3.3 Promotion and branding**. Here, you only need to know that brands help a business differentiate, build loyalty and charge more.

## Market size, share and growth

These three get muddled constantly, so hold the picture in your head: **size is the whole pot, share is your slice, growth is the pot changing size.**

<Figure caption="Market share always adds up to 100%. Gaining share means taking it from somebody else.">
  <MarketShareDiagram />
</Figure>

Market size can be measured two ways:

- **By value** — total revenue, e.g. "the market is worth $40 million a year".
- **By volume** — total units, e.g. "2 million units are sold a year".

These can move in opposite directions. If prices fall sharply, volume can rise while value falls. Examiners like that point.

<Formula name="The three calculations you need" note="Always show your working. A correct method with an arithmetic slip still earns marks; a bare wrong answer earns nothing.">
Market share (%) = (Sales of one business ÷ Total market sales) × 100

Market growth (%) = ((New size − Old size) ÷ Old size) × 100

Market size (value) = Volume sold × Average price
</Formula>

<Worked>

A guesthouse operator sells **4,800** room nights in a year at an average of **$95** a night. Across the whole island, all guesthouses together sell **$6.8 million** of room nights. Last year the island total was **$5.9 million**.

**1. The operator's sales by value**

`4,800 × $95 = $456,000`

**2. Its market share**

`($456,000 ÷ $6,800,000) × 100 = 6.7%`

**3. Market growth over the year**

`(($6.8m − $5.9m) ÷ $5.9m) × 100 = 15.3%`

**Reading the answer:** the operator holds a small share of a market growing at over 15% a year. It could grow sales strongly and still *lose* share, if rivals grow faster. That difference between growing sales and growing share is exactly the kind of point that earns analysis marks.

</Worked>

## Dynamic markets

Almost no market stays still. A **dynamic market** is one that is changing, and businesses that fail to notice tend not to survive.

<Figure caption="Four ways a market moves, and the problem each one sets the business.">
  <DynamicMarketDiagram />
</Figure>

<Chunk title="What drives the change" open>

**Online retailing.** Selling online removes the need for expensive premises and reaches customers a shop never could. It also drops the barriers to entry, so competition rises, and price comparison makes demand more price sensitive.

**Innovation and market growth.** A genuinely new product can create a market that did not exist. Mobile money is the standard example: in several African economies, people who never had a bank account moved straight to paying by phone, and an entire market appeared in under a decade.

**Changing incomes and tastes.** As incomes rise, spending shifts from basics towards services and premium goods, so some markets grow while others fade.

**Social and demographic change.** An ageing population grows the market for healthcare and shrinks the market for baby products.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Adapting to change">

Businesses respond by:

- **Changing the product** — new features, new variants, a new design.
- **Changing the channel** — adding online sales, or delivery, or an app.
- **Repositioning the brand** — aiming at a different group of customers.
- **Entering a new market** — a new segment, or a new country.
- **Leaving** — closing a line before losses mount.

Adapting costs money and takes time, and that is the tension every evaluation question is really about.

</Chunk>

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Tourism in the Maldives was for decades almost purely a **niche luxury market**: a small number of visitors paying very high prices for a whole private island. When the law was changed to allow guesthouses on inhabited islands, a **second, far cheaper segment** opened up alongside it.

That is a market **fragmenting**. The same destination now serves two very different customers, at very different prices, with very different cost structures. A resort competing on service quality and a guesthouse competing on price are barely in the same market at all, even though both sell "a holiday in the Maldives".

It is a useful example because it shows something examiners reward: a market can grow in total while the *nature* of the demand shifts underneath.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Mass market, many countries.** Unilever and Coca-Cola sell broadly standardised products in well over a hundred countries, adapting little beyond pack size and price.
- **Online retailing reshaping a market.** In South East Asia, mobile-first marketplaces took a large part of retail spending from physical shops in only a few years, in economies where high-street retail had never been dominant to begin with.
- **A market disappearing.** DVD rental had a healthy market in the 2000s and essentially none now. Streaming did not compete with it; it replaced it.
- **A small economy, a concentrated market.** In a country with a few hundred thousand people, a market may only support two or three firms. Market shares are large, rivalry is intense and direct, and one new entrant can change everything. Population size itself limits market size.

</RealWorld>

## How competition affects the market

More competition generally means:

- **Lower prices**, as firms undercut each other.
- **Better quality and more choice**, as firms differentiate to avoid competing on price alone.
- **Thinner profit margins**, and pressure to cut costs.
- **More innovation**, because standing still means losing share.

Less competition means the opposite: higher prices, weaker incentive to improve, fatter margins. A business with a dominant share has real pricing power.

The link to elasticity matters. Where there are many close substitutes, demand for any one firm's product is **price elastic**, so raising price loses a lot of customers. That connects straight to **1.3.2.4 Price elasticity of demand**.

## Risk and uncertainty

This is a small piece of the specification that comes up often, and it is easy to score on.

<Figure caption="The test is whether you can attach a probability to it. If you can, it is risk. If you cannot, it is uncertainty.">
  <RiskUncertaintyDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Risk** — you know what could happen and roughly how likely each outcome is. Past data lets you put a number on it, so you can plan for it, price it in, or insure against it. A shop knows roughly what percentage of stock gets damaged each year.
- **Uncertainty** — you cannot know. A new competitor, a change in the law, a currency collapse, a pandemic. No sensible probability can be attached, so no insurer will price it.

Businesses handle risk with data and insurance. They handle uncertainty with **flexibility**: keeping cash in reserve, avoiding long fixed commitments, spreading across several markets, and planning for more than one scenario.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Rarely as a whole essay. Almost always as:

- **Define** (2 marks) — a term such as niche market, market share or uncertainty. Give the definition, then one short example. Do not waffle; two marks is two sentences at most.
- **Calculate** (4 marks) — market share or market growth from a table in the source. Write the formula, substitute the numbers, show the working, state the answer **with its unit** (% or $).
- **Explain** (4 marks) — one reason with a developed chain, e.g. "why might operating in a niche market be risky for this business". One point, properly developed, beats three undeveloped ones.
- **As application inside bigger questions.** In an Assess (10 marks) or Evaluate (20 marks) answer, this content is how you show AO2. Quote the share figures from the source and build on them.

**Getting the evaluation marks.** On a question about whether to target a mass or a niche market, the marks come from *weighing*, not listing:

- **It depends on the size of the business.** A start-up with limited finance rarely survives a price war in a mass market.
- **It depends on the time frame.** A niche gives high margins now; a mass market gives scale later.
- **It depends on how defendable the niche is.** If it can be copied cheaply, the advantage is temporary.
- **It depends on the data in front of you.** If the source shows the mass market growing at 2% and the niche at 20%, say so and use it.

Then commit to a judgement and justify it. A balanced answer with no conclusion caps out below full marks.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Mixing up market size and market share.** Size is the whole market in $ or units. Share is one firm's percentage of it. Read the question twice.
- **Confusing growth in sales with growth in share.** A firm can grow sales by 10% and still lose share in a market growing 25%.
- **Assuming niche means small firm.** Large companies run niche brands deliberately, for the margin.
- **Dropping the × 100, or the unit.** An answer of "0.067" when the question wants a percentage throws away a mark. So does "6.7" with no % sign.
- **Treating risk and uncertainty as the same thing.** They are separately examinable. The difference is whether a probability can be attached.
- **Describing the market in general terms when a source is provided.** "The market is quite large" earns nothing. "The market is worth $6.8m and grew 15.3%" earns the application mark.
- **Listing without judging in evaluation questions.** Four advantages and four disadvantages, then no conclusion, is a mid-band answer.

</Mistakes>
