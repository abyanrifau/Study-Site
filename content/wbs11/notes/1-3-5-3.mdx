## Survival first

For a new business, survival is the objective, because everything else requires still existing. In practice it means **managing cash**, not profit: a business with orders and profit on paper fails if it cannot pay wages this month.

Survival also becomes the objective again in a crisis, at any age of business. During a sharp downturn, mature companies drop growth and profit targets and focus on liquidity.

## Profit maximisation

The largest possible profit. It is what most stakeholders, especially shareholders, expect, and it funds investment and reinvestment.

Its limits, which is where the exam marks are:

- It is usually the **long-run** objective, and short-run actions that maximise profit now can damage it later. Cutting quality raises this year's margin and loses next year's customers.
- It can conflict with every other objective on the list.
- It assumes the owner wants it. See satisficing in **1.3.5.2**.

## The other objectives

<Chunk title="What each one is, and why a business would choose it" open>

**Sales maximisation.** Selling the most possible, even where it is not the most profitable mix. Why: to build market presence, achieve economies of scale, or meet a distribution requirement. Risk: high volume at a low margin can be less profitable than lower volume at a better one.

**Market share.** Increasing your proportion of the market. Why: share brings power over suppliers, negotiating strength with retailers, brand recognition and a platform for later price increases. Risk: share bought with discounts costs margin now for a benefit that may not arrive.

**Cost efficiency.** Lowest possible cost per unit. Why: it enables competitive pricing and protects profit in a price-led market. Risk: cutting cost eventually cuts quality, and in a service business it cuts the product itself.

**Employee welfare.** Looking after staff. Why: it is an end in itself for many owners, and it reduces turnover and raises productivity, so it often pays. Risk: it raises costs now for a return that is slow and hard to measure.

**Customer satisfaction.** Meeting or beating expectations. Why: repeat custom is cheaper than new custom, satisfied customers recommend you, and in an online market reviews are decisive. Risk: satisfaction can always be bought with more cost, so it needs a limit.

**Social objectives.** Environmental or community benefit. Why: the owner's values, customer expectation, or regulation. Risk: it may reduce profit, and stakeholders who wanted profit will object.

</Chunk>

## Objectives change and conflict

**They change over time.** Survival at start-up, growth and share in the growth phase, profit and efficiency in maturity, and possibly survival again in decline. If a question gives you the age or stage of the business, use it.

**They conflict.** This is the most useful thing to know about the topic:

| Objective | Conflicts with |
| --- | --- |
| Profit maximisation | employee welfare, social objectives, customer satisfaction |
| Market share (via low prices) | profit maximisation |
| Cost efficiency | quality, customer satisfaction, employee welfare |
| Sales maximisation | profit, if the extra volume is low margin |
| Employee welfare | cost efficiency |

So a business cannot pursue all of them at once, and choosing one has a cost in the others. That trade-off is what an Assess question is really asking about.

## What makes an objective useful

It has to be **measurable** and have a **deadline**. "Improve customer satisfaction" cannot be judged. "Raise the average review score from 4.1 to 4.5 within twelve months" can. Businesses often use the SMART idea here: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A new guesthouse in its first year has one real objective: survive the first wet season. Bookings are thin for months while the mortgage, staff and electricity still have to be paid, so cash, not profit, is what decides whether it reaches the next dry season.

Three years later the objectives change. Now it is market share on the booking platforms, because the ranking that share produces brings the bookings, and customer satisfaction, because the review score is what determines that ranking. Both cost money and both reduce this year's profit, and both are the right call.

Notice the conflict in practice: discounting to win reviews and ranking sacrifices margin now for position later. Whether that is worth it is exactly the kind of judgement a 10-mark question wants.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on why a business has a particular objective; Assess (10) on which objective a business should pursue.

**Structuring the Assess:**

1. Identify the stage the business is at from the source. New, growing, mature, struggling.
2. Recommend an objective and chain the reasoning to what it does for the business.
3. Name the conflict: what this objective costs in another one.
4. Judge, and say what it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Objectives conflict**, so naming the cost of the chosen one is close to automatic evaluation.
- **It depends on the stage of the business.** Survival for a start-up, share for a grower, profit for a mature firm.
- **It depends on the owner's motives.** A satisficer will not chase maximum profit.
- **Short run against long run.** Maximising profit now can destroy it later.
- **Is it measurable?** An objective nobody can test is not an objective.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Assuming profit maximisation is always the objective.** It is one of several, and the source usually hints at which.
- **Confusing survival with profitability.** Survival is about cash. A profitable business can still fail by running out of it.
- **Confusing sales maximisation with profit maximisation.** More sales at a thin margin can mean less profit.
- **Listing objectives without naming the conflicts.** The conflict is where the marks are.
- **Forgetting objectives change with the stage of the business.**
- **Proposing an objective that cannot be measured.**
- **Ignoring the owner's own motives**, which may be independence or a social purpose rather than money.

</Mistakes>
