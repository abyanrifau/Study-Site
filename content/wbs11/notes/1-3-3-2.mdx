## The design mix

<Figure caption="Every design decision pulls against the other two. Improve the materials and cost rises; cut the cost and either function or appearance suffers.">
  <DesignMixDiagram />
</Figure>

**Function** — does it do the job? Reliability, durability, ease of use, safety, performance. For a kettle, does it boil water quickly and not leak.

**Aesthetics** — does it appeal? Look, feel, weight, finish, smell, even sound. This is what allows differentiation when function is much the same across a market.

**Cost / economic manufacture** — can it be made cheaply enough to sell at a profit? A brilliant design that costs more to make than customers will pay is not a design, it is a prototype.

The examinable idea is the **trade-off**. You cannot maximise all three. Which one leads depends on the market:

| Market | Usually leads on |
| --- | --- |
| Budget mass market | cost |
| Luxury and premium | aesthetics |
| Industrial and B2B | function |
| Safety-critical | function, with cost a distant second |

## Social trends in design

The specification singles out two, and they are increasingly examined.

<Chunk title="Resource depletion" open>

Finite resources are being used faster than they are replaced, so businesses design to use less. Three routes:

- **Waste minimisation** — use less material in the first place. Lighter packaging, fewer components, less offcut.
- **Re-use** — refillable containers, returnable crates, repairable products.
- **Recycling** — design so the materials can be recovered, for example a single-material bottle rather than a mix that cannot be separated.

The business case is real: less material means lower cost, so sustainable design and cheap design often point the same way. That is a useful evaluation point, because it undercuts the idea that ethics always costs money.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Ethical sourcing">

Buying materials in a way that respects the people and places they come from: fair pay, safe conditions, no child labour, no illegal deforestation.

Why businesses do it: consumer pressure, retailer requirements, avoiding the reputational damage of a supply chain scandal, and increasingly law.

Why it is hard: it usually costs more per unit, supply chains are long and opaque so verification is expensive, and a business may not know who its supplier's supplier is.

</Chunk>

## Why design matters commercially

Good design adds value, which links straight to **1.3.1.3**. It can:

- justify a premium price;
- create a USP that is hard to copy quickly;
- reduce unit cost through simpler manufacture;
- reduce returns and warranty claims through better function;
- make the product recognisable, which supports the brand.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Cost-led design in emerging markets.** Products designed specifically for lower-income markets, such as single-use sachets of shampoo across South Asia or very low-cost motorbikes, lead on economic manufacture and accept lower specification.

**Design for a climate.** Products sold into hot, humid, salt-air island environments have to be designed around corrosion. A boat fitting or air conditioning unit that lasts ten years in Europe may last two in the Maldives, so function, not aesthetics, decides which supplier wins.

**Aesthetics as the whole product.** In fashion and fragrance, function is nearly identical across the market and almost all the added value comes from appearance and brand.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on design mix, aesthetics or ethical sourcing; Explain (4) on why a business would change one element of the mix; Assess (10) on whether a business should invest in redesigning a product or change to ethical sourcing.

**Structure an Assess answer around the trade-off.** Name which element of the mix the change improves, then name what it costs in the other two, then judge which matters most in this market.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **It depends on the market.** A cost-led redesign is right for a budget product and wrong for a premium one.
- **It depends on whether customers will pay.** Ethical sourcing only pays for itself if customers notice and care.
- **Sustainable design often lowers cost.** Less material is cheaper, so the two are not always in conflict.
- **It depends on how quickly it can be copied.** A design advantage is temporary unless protected.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing the three elements without the trade-off.** The trade-off is the whole point.
- **Treating aesthetics as unimportant for functional products.** Tools, appliances and machinery all compete partly on finish.
- **Assuming ethical sourcing always costs more overall.** It can reduce waste and protect revenue by avoiding a scandal.
- **Confusing waste minimisation with recycling.** Minimisation uses less material; recycling recovers it afterwards.
- **Saying "customers want sustainable products" with no evidence.** Say whether they will pay more for them, which is a different question.
- **Forgetting cost is part of the mix.** A design that cannot be made profitably has failed on a design criterion, not just a financial one.

</Mistakes>
