## Why supply slopes upwards

At a higher price, each unit earns the producer more, so producing more becomes worthwhile. Existing firms raise output and new firms enter. So quantity supplied rises with price.

Same rule as demand: a change in the good's **own price** moves you *along* the curve. Anything else shifts the whole curve.

</Figure>

A useful shortcut: **cheaper to produce, supply shifts right. Dearer to produce, supply shifts left.** Every one of the five shifters below is really that idea.

## What shifts supply

<Chunk title="Changes in the costs of production" open>

The biggest one. Raw materials, wages, energy, rent, transport, imported components.

- Costs **rise** → producing is less profitable at every price → supply shifts **left**.
- Costs **fall** → supply shifts **right**.

For an importing business, a weakening currency raises the cost of every imported input and shifts supply left even though nothing about the product changed.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Introduction of new technology">

New technology raises output per worker or per machine, which lowers cost per unit. Supply shifts **right**.

It is rarely instant. The machinery has to be bought, staff retrained, and productivity often dips before it improves. In an exam, saying that the shift takes time is a genuine evaluation point.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Indirect taxes">

A tax on the good itself, such as VAT or an excise duty on fuel, tobacco or sugary drinks. The producer has to hand part of the selling price to the government, so supplying is less profitable at every price. Supply shifts **left**.

Because supply shifts left, the equilibrium price rises and quantity falls. Part of the tax gets passed on to the customer, and how much depends on price elasticity of demand. That link to **1.3.2.4** is worth making.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Government subsidies">

The reverse. A payment to producers lowers their effective cost per unit, so supply shifts **right**, price falls and quantity rises.

Governments use subsidies to support a strategic industry, keep a staple food affordable, or encourage something with a wider benefit such as renewable energy.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="External shocks">

Unexpected events hitting the ability to produce: a drought wiping out a harvest, a factory fire, a port closure, a shipping route disrupted, a pandemic stopping work.

Supply shifts **left**, usually sharply and with no warning. Like demand shocks, these are uncertainty rather than risk.

</Chunk>

## Cost changes in numbers

<Formula name="Total costs" note="Fixed costs do not change with output; variable costs do. Both shift supply if they change, but a variable cost rise affects every extra unit produced.">
Total costs = Fixed costs + Variable costs
</Formula>

<Worked>

A bakery produces **2,000** loaves a month. Fixed costs are **$1,600** a month. Variable costs are **$0.70** a loaf.

**Now:**

`Variable costs = 2,000 × $0.70 = $1,400`

`Total costs = $1,600 + $1,400 = $3,000`

`Cost per loaf = $3,000 ÷ 2,000 = $1.50`

**Flour prices then rise, pushing variable cost to $0.95 a loaf:**

`Variable costs = 2,000 × $0.95 = $1,900`

`Total costs = $1,600 + $1,900 = $3,500`

`Cost per loaf = $3,500 ÷ 2,000 = $1.75`

**Reading the answer:** cost per loaf rose by 25 cents, about 17%. At any given selling price the bakery now earns less per loaf, so it is willing to supply less at that price. **The supply curve shifts left.** If it wants to protect its margin it must raise price, which under elastic demand would cost it volume.

</Worked>

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Almost everything a Maldivian business uses is imported: flour, fuel, building materials, vehicles. That makes local supply unusually exposed to two things it cannot control.

**Shipping and fuel costs.** When global freight rates spiked, the landed cost of inputs rose for every importing business at once. Supply across many markets shifted left together, and prices rose even where local demand had not changed at all.

**Import duties,** which are indirect taxes. A duty raises the cost of each imported unit, shifts supply left and pushes the retail price up. When a government reduces duty on a staple food, it works exactly like a subsidy: supply shifts right and the price falls.

The wider point is that in a small import-dependent economy, supply-side shocks matter more than in a large economy that produces its own inputs.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Technology.** Solar panel manufacturing costs fell dramatically over two decades through improved production methods, shifting global supply right and cutting prices.
- **Indirect tax.** Sugar taxes in Mexico, the UK and South Africa raised producers' costs on sugary drinks, shifted supply left and pushed prices up, which was the policy's whole purpose.
- **Subsidies.** Fuel and fertiliser subsidies in several South Asian economies hold supply further right than it would otherwise be, keeping prices below the market level.
- **External shock.** Drought in coffee-growing regions of Brazil has repeatedly shifted world coffee supply left and raised prices worldwide within months.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.**

- **Define** (2 marks) — supply, indirect tax, subsidy, costs of production.
- **Construct** (4 marks) — draw and shift a supply curve. Axes labelled **Price** and **Quantity**, curves labelled **S** and **S₁**, direction clear.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — why a named factor changes supply for this business.
- **Analyse** (6 marks) — the chain from the cost change through to the effect on the business.

**The chain that earns marks:**

> An import duty is imposed → the cost of each imported unit rises → producing is less profitable at every price → supply shifts left → the equilibrium price rises and quantity falls → the business passes some of the cost to customers → if demand is price elastic it loses significant volume, so its revenue may fall.

**Getting evaluation marks:** how big is the cost rise as a proportion of total costs? Can the business absorb it, or must it pass it on? Can it switch supplier, or is it locked in? Is the change permanent or temporary? A 2% rise in one minor input is not the same as a 30% rise in the main one.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Shifting supply the wrong way.** An **increase** in supply is a shift to the **right**. Rising costs *decrease* supply, so the curve goes **left**. Students very often reverse this because "costs went up" feels like "up".
- **Confusing a movement with a shift.** A change in the good's own price moves you along the curve. Costs, tax, technology, subsidies and shocks shift it.
- **Treating a subsidy as a tax.** A subsidy lowers costs and shifts supply right. A tax raises costs and shifts it left.
- **Confusing indirect tax with corporation tax.** An indirect tax is on the good itself and shifts supply. A tax on profits does not shift the supply curve in this model.
- **Saying the producer pays the whole tax, or the customer does.** It is shared, and the split depends on elasticity.
- **Assuming new technology helps immediately.** There is a cost, a delay and usually a dip first.
- **Forgetting the source data.** If the paper gives you a cost per unit, use it and calculate. Application marks are the easiest ones on the paper.

</Mistakes>
