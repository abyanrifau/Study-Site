## The six strategies

<Chunk title="Cost plus" open>

Work out the unit cost, add a mark-up.

<Formula name="Cost plus price" note="Watch the wording: a 40% mark-up on a $5 cost gives $7. A 40% margin would give $8.33, because margin is a percentage of the selling price.">
Unit cost = Total costs ÷ Units produced

Price = Unit cost + (Unit cost × Mark-up %)
</Formula>

**Strengths:** simple, quick, guarantees a profit on each unit if you sell them.

**Weaknesses:** it ignores customers entirely. If the resulting price is above what they will pay, you sell nothing; if it is below, you have left money on the table. It also depends on the output figure, and unit cost changes with output.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Price skimming">

Launch high, lower it over time. Used where the product is new and genuinely differentiated, often in technology.

**Why it works:** early adopters pay a premium, so development costs are recovered fast, and a high launch price signals quality.

**Why it fails:** it invites competitors in at a lower price, volumes are small at first, and customers who paid the launch price resent the later cut.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Penetration pricing">

Launch low to buy market share, then raise the price once you have it.

**Why it works:** builds volume and habit quickly, achieves economies of scale, discourages entry.

**Why it fails:** margins are thin or negative at the start so it needs deep pockets, it anchors customers at the low price so the later rise loses some of them, and it can position the brand as cheap.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Predatory, competitive and psychological">

**Predatory** — price below cost to drive rivals out, then raise it. Note two things in an exam: it needs deep reserves to sustain losses, and it is **illegal in many countries** as an abuse of market power. Never recommend it without saying so.

**Competitive** — price at or just below rivals. Safe in a crowded market with little differentiation, but it surrenders pricing control and can start a price war nobody wins.

**Psychological** — $9.99 rather than $10, or a deliberately round premium price. Cheap to implement and it genuinely affects perception, but it is a tactic rather than a strategy and will not save a badly positioned product.

</Chunk>

## Choosing the strategy

The specification lists six factors, and a good answer picks the two or three that matter here rather than listing all six.

| Factor | Points towards |
| --- | --- |
| Number of USPs / differentiation | more differentiation, more pricing freedom, so skimming or premium |
| Price elasticity of demand | elastic means competitive or penetration; inelastic allows skimming or premium |
| Level of competition | crowded market pushes towards competitive pricing |
| Strength of brand | a strong brand supports a premium |
| Stage in the product life cycle | skimming at launch, competitive in maturity, discounting in decline |
| Costs and the need to make a profit | sets the floor: you cannot price below cost for long |

## Social trends

**Online sales and price comparison sites.** Customers can now compare every seller in seconds, at no cost. That has made demand for many products far **more price elastic** than it was, which pushes businesses towards competitive pricing and squeezes margins.

The strategic response is not to win the price war but to escape it: differentiate so that comparison is not like for like, or bundle so that the headline price is not directly comparable.

<Worked>

A guesthouse works out its costs for a room night:

Fixed costs **$4,000** a month, variable costs **$18** a room night, and it expects to sell **400** room nights a month.

**Unit cost**

`Fixed per unit = $4,000 ÷ 400 = $10`

`Unit cost = $10 + $18 = $28`

**Cost plus price at a 150% mark-up**

`Price = $28 + ($28 × 1.50) = $28 + $42 = $70`

**Now notice the weakness.** If occupancy falls to 250 room nights, fixed cost per unit becomes $16, so unit cost is $34 and the same mark-up gives $85. The cost plus price rises exactly when demand is weakest, which is the opposite of what the market wants. That is the point to make in an evaluation.

</Worked>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Skimming.** New smartphone and console launches price high for the first months, then cut as rivals respond and volume matters more than margin.

**Penetration.** Ride-hailing and food delivery apps entered market after market at deliberately loss-making prices to build habit and share, funded by investors rather than profits.

**Comparison sites changing elasticity.** Hotel and flight booking platforms made those markets far more price elastic. A Maldivian guesthouse is now compared side by side with twenty others on one screen, which is precisely why differentiation and reviews matter more than a slightly lower rate.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Calculate (4) a cost plus price or unit cost; Explain (4) why one strategy suits this business; Assess (10) which strategy it should adopt.

**On the calculation:** work out unit cost first and show it, then apply the mark-up. Watch whether the question says mark-up (percentage of cost) or margin (percentage of price). Confusing the two is the most common error.

**Structuring an Assess answer:**

1. Rule out what clearly does not fit, with a reason. "Predatory pricing is not viable: it is illegal in many markets and the business does not have the reserves."
2. Argue for one strategy using two of the six factors, applied to this business's data.
3. Weigh the main risk of that strategy.
4. Decide, and say what it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:** the cost plus weakness above (price rises when demand falls) is a strong, specific point. So is the time frame: penetration is a long game that needs cash now for share later.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing mark-up with margin.** Mark-up is on cost, margin is on price.
- **Confusing penetration with predatory.** Penetration is low pricing to win share and is legal. Predatory is below cost to destroy rivals and is often illegal.
- **Recommending predatory pricing** without mentioning that it is illegal in many countries.
- **Treating psychological pricing as a strategy.** It is a tactic applied on top of one.
- **Forgetting unit cost depends on output.** A cost plus price calculated at one output level is wrong at another.
- **Listing all six factors instead of applying two or three.** Depth beats breadth.
- **Ignoring the source data.** If costs and rival prices are given, use them.
- **Saying skimming "means high prices" and stopping.** The defining feature is that the price starts high and comes down.

</Mistakes>
