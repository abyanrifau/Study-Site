## The equilibrium

The demand curve shows what buyers will take at each price. The supply curve shows what sellers will offer. Only one price satisfies both.

## Why the market gets there

This is the bit that earns explanation marks, and most students skip it.

**If price is set above the equilibrium** — buyers want less than sellers offer, so there is **excess supply**. Stock builds up unsold. Sellers cut prices to shift it, and as price falls demand rises and supply falls, until the two meet at the equilibrium.

**If price is set below the equilibrium** — buyers want more than sellers offer, so there is **excess demand**. Products sell out and queues or waiting lists form. Sellers realise they can charge more, and as price rises demand falls and supply rises, until they meet.

Price is doing two jobs at once: it **signals** where to move resources, and it **rations** what is scarce.

## When a curve shifts

<Figure caption="Demand shifts right, so at the old price there is excess demand. Price is bid up until a new equilibrium is reached at a higher price and a higher quantity.">
  <DemandShiftEffectDiagram />
</Figure>

Learn these four outcomes. They come up constantly and you should be able to state them without drawing first.

| What shifts | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand increases (right) | rises | rises |
| Demand decreases (left) | falls | falls |
| Supply increases (right) | falls | rises |
| Supply decreases (left) | rises | falls |

Notice the pattern: with a **demand** shift, price and quantity move **the same way**. With a **supply** shift, they move **opposite ways**. That one sentence is worth memorising.

<Chunk title="What if both curves shift?" open>

Papers sometimes give you a change that shifts both. Then one outcome is certain and the other is not.

For example, demand rises **and** costs rise, so supply shifts left:

- Price: demand-right pushes it up, supply-left pushes it up. **Price definitely rises.**
- Quantity: demand-right pushes it up, supply-left pushes it down. **Quantity depends on which shift is bigger.**

Saying "the effect on quantity is uncertain and depends on the relative size of the two shifts" is a strong, high-level answer. Do not invent a definite answer where none exists.

</Chunk>

## Reading a diagram in the exam

A reliable method:

1. **Draw the starting position.** Axes labelled Price and Quantity, curves labelled D and S, mark the original equilibrium P and Q with dashed guide lines.
2. **Decide which curve moves, and which way.** Ask: has the good's own price changed? If yes, it is a movement, not a shift.
3. **Draw the new curve** as D₁ or S₁ and label it.
4. **Mark the new equilibrium** as P₁ and Q₁ with dashed lines.
5. **Write one sentence** saying what happened to price and quantity, using the labels.
6. **Then explain what it means for the business** — revenue, margin, capacity.

Step 6 is where most of the marks are and where most answers stop too soon.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Peak-season hotel rooms are a textbook case, because **supply is almost fixed in the short run**. You cannot build extra rooms for December by November.

Demand shifts right as the season starts. Supply cannot follow. At the old price there is heavy excess demand, so the price is bid up sharply and quantity rises only slightly, limited by rooms available.

That is why nightly rates in high season can be multiples of the low-season rate for an identical room. The price is doing the **rationing**: it decides who gets the scarce rooms. And because quantity cannot respond much, almost the whole adjustment happens through price.

Off season the demand curve shifts back left, there is excess supply of rooms, and rates fall until enough demand returns to fill them.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Supply shock.** Drought in a major wheat-exporting country shifts world supply left. Price rises, quantity traded falls, and importing countries feel it as food inflation within months.
- **Demand collapse.** When global air travel stopped in 2020, demand for jet fuel shifted sharply left. Price fell and quantity fell together, exactly as the table predicts.
- **Technology on the supply side.** Falling battery costs shifted the supply of electric vehicles right. Price fell and quantity rose, which then attracted still more demand.
- **Price set below equilibrium.** Where governments cap fuel or bread prices below the market level, the predictable result is excess demand: shortages, queues and rationing.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** More by diagram than any other topic in the unit.

- **Construct** (4 marks) — draw a supply and demand diagram showing a stated change. The marks are for: both axes labelled, both curves labelled, the shift in the correct direction, and the new equilibrium marked. Practise until you can do it in two minutes.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — why excess demand pushes price up, or why a shift changes the price.
- **Analyse** (6 marks) — the chain from the cause through the diagram to the consequence for a named business.
- **Assess** (10 marks) — usually about how a business should respond.

**The chain that earns marks:**

> The peak season begins → demand shifts right → at the existing rate quantity demanded exceeds rooms available, so there is excess demand → the resort raises its rate → the equilibrium price rises while quantity rises only slightly, because supply is fixed in the short run → revenue rises sharply, since almost the entire adjustment comes through price rather than volume.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **How fast can supply respond?** If it cannot, price does all the work. This is the strongest point available on this topic.
- **How long will the shift last?** A temporary shift may not justify investing in capacity.
- **What does elasticity do to the outcome?** Inelastic demand means a bigger price change and a smaller quantity change.
- **Is a higher price actually good for the business?** Higher prices can damage long-term customer relationships, and invite new entrants.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Shifting both curves when only one has changed.** Read the question and identify the single cause first.
- **Shifting a curve when the good's own price changed.** That is a movement along the curve. Nothing shifts.
- **Unlabelled diagrams.** Axes, curves, and both equilibria. A perfect but unlabelled diagram scores very little.
- **Muddling excess demand and excess supply.** Price **above** equilibrium gives excess **supply**. Price **below** equilibrium gives excess **demand**.
- **Getting the direction of the price change wrong.** Use the table: demand shifts move price and quantity the same way, supply shifts move them opposite ways.
- **Inventing a definite answer when both curves shift.** If the effect on quantity is genuinely ambiguous, say so and explain what it depends on. That is rewarded, not penalised.
- **Stopping at the diagram.** The diagram is the working, not the answer. Always finish with what it means for the business.

</Mistakes>
