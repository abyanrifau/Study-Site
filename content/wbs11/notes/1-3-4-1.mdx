## Asset or cost?

The same wage bill, two completely different ways of thinking about it.

**Staff as an asset.** People are the source of quality, service and ideas, so you invest: train them, pay above the minimum, give security, promote from within. The return comes as higher productivity, lower labour turnover and better customer service. Costs more now, and the payback is slow and hard to measure.

**Staff as a cost.** Labour is an expense on the income statement, so you minimise it: low pay, minimal training, flexible contracts, outsourcing. Cheap immediately, and the bill arrives later as high turnover, weak service and constant retraining.

Most businesses sit somewhere between. The examinable point is that the right position depends on what the business competes on: a budget airline and a luxury resort both make a defensible choice, and they make opposite ones.

## The flexible workforce

<Chunk title="The six forms in the specification" open>

- **Multi-skilling** — staff trained across several roles, so they can be moved to where the work is. Cuts idle time and covers absence, and it needs investment in training.
- **Part-time and temporary** — hours matched to demand, easy to scale, cheaper than full-time cover. Less commitment, more coordination.
- **Zero-hour contracts** — no guaranteed hours, so the business pays only for what it uses. Maximum flexibility for the employer, minimum security for the worker, which damages loyalty and attracts criticism.
- **Flexible hours and home working** — start and finish when suits, or work remotely. Widens the pool of applicants, improves retention, and needs trust and systems.
- **Outsourcing** — someone else does the work. Converts a fixed cost into a variable one and buys expertise, and loses control of quality and of the customer relationship.

</Chunk>

**Why businesses want flexibility:** demand is rarely steady. Seasonality, week-to-week variation and unexpected orders all mean a fixed workforce is either idle or overstretched.

**What it costs them:** flexible workers are less loyal, know the business less well, need more supervision and leave more often. In a service business, where the employee *is* the product, that shows up directly in what the customer experiences.

## Dismissal and redundancy

A small distinction that is regularly examined.

| | Dismissal | Redundancy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reason | the person: conduct, capability | the job: it no longer exists |
| About performance? | yes | no |
| Usually paid compensation? | no | yes |
| Can the role be refilled? | yes | no, that is the point |

Re-advertising a role you made redundant is the clearest sign it was not a genuine redundancy.

## Employer and employee relations

**Individual approach** — the business negotiates with each employee. Flexible, rewards individual performance, and time-consuming with many staff and open to inconsistency.

**Collective bargaining** — pay and conditions negotiated for a group at once, usually through a union. One negotiation covers everyone, which is efficient and gives employees real bargaining power. It also means the business loses the ability to differentiate, and a dispute affects everybody at once.

Good relations matter because they affect motivation, retention, productivity and the risk of industrial action.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Resort employment shows both approaches at once. Core staff, chefs, dive instructors, managers, are treated as assets: trained, retained, often housed on site, because guest experience depends on them and replacing them mid-season is very costly.

Around that core sits genuine flexibility. Demand is strongly seasonal, so seasonal contracts, multi-skilling across departments and outsourced functions such as laundry or maintenance let the resort scale down in the wet season.

The tension is real: the same flexible contracts that make the seasonal model work also make it harder to keep experienced staff, and staff who return every season are worth far more than new ones. That is a good, concrete evaluation point.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on any of the terms, especially dismissal against redundancy; Explain (4) on one benefit or drawback of a flexible workforce; Assess (10) on whether a business should treat staff as an asset or use more flexible contracts.

**Structuring the Assess:**

1. What does this business compete on? Service and quality points towards asset; price points towards cost.
2. How variable is its demand? Highly seasonal demand makes flexibility genuinely necessary, not just cheap.
3. Use the figures. If labour turnover or training costs are in the source, calculate with them.
4. Judge: most answers should land on a mix, with the reason for the split.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **In a service business the employee is the product**, so cutting labour cost cuts the thing being sold.
- **The costs of a cost-minimising approach are real but delayed**: turnover, retraining, weak service.
- **It depends on the labour market.** Where skilled staff are scarce, treating them as a cost simply loses them.
- **Flexibility is not free.** Multi-skilling needs training; outsourcing needs managing.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing dismissal and redundancy.** Dismissal is about the person, redundancy about the job.
- **Assuming "asset" is always right.** It costs money now and a price-led business may not be able to afford it.
- **Treating a flexible workforce as purely a cost saving.** It also matches staffing to demand, which is a separate benefit.
- **Saying zero-hour contracts are always bad.** They suit some workers, particularly students, and they carry real reputational risk.
- **Forgetting that outsourcing transfers control as well as cost.**
- **Describing collective bargaining as simply "unions".** It is the practice of negotiating for a group at once.
- **Not applying to the business in the source.** Seasonal, service-based and price-led businesses each need a different answer.

</Mistakes>
