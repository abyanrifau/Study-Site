## Why it matters

Motivated employees work harder and better. The chain, which is what earns analysis marks:

> Motivation rises → productivity rises and labour turnover falls → unit costs fall and service quality improves → the business becomes more competitive → profit rises.

Note it runs through **two** routes: cost (productivity, less turnover) and revenue (service, quality). Saying both is stronger than saying one.

## The four theories

<Chunk title="Taylor: scientific management" open>

Workers are motivated by money. So break the job into simple repeated tasks, find the most efficient method, train people in it, and pay per unit produced.

**Where it still applies:** piecework in manufacturing and agriculture, commission in sales, delivery and gig work paid per job.

**Why it is limited:** it treats people as machines, ignores everything non-financial, produces boring work and high turnover, and encourages quantity over quality. Someone paid per unit has every reason to rush.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Mayo: human relations">

From the Hawthorne studies. Productivity rose when workers were given attention and consulted, and it rose even when conditions were made worse, because someone was taking an interest.

**Conclusion:** people are motivated by social needs, teamwork, communication and being involved.

**In practice:** team working, social facilities, consultation, regular one-to-ones.

**Limitation:** it can be read as manipulation, and social needs alone will not compensate for genuinely poor pay.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Maslow: hierarchy of needs">

</Figure>

The practical use is diagnostic: **work out which level is unmet, and act there.** Offering someone recognition and challenge when they cannot pay their rent achieves nothing. Offering a pay rise to someone who is well paid but bored also achieves nothing.

**Limitations:** the levels are not rigid, people vary, and the theory is hard to test. Some people pursue self-actualisation while lower needs go unmet.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Herzberg: two factors">

<Figure caption="Two separate scales, not one. Fixing hygiene factors moves you from dissatisfied to neutral. Only motivators move you from neutral to motivated.">
  <HerzbergDiagram />
</Figure>

**Hygiene factors** — pay, conditions, supervision, job security, company policy. Get them wrong and people are dissatisfied. Get them right and people are simply *not dissatisfied*. They do not become motivated.

**Motivators** — achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement. These are what actually create motivation.

**The single most useful exam idea in this topic:** a pay rise buys a short-lived removal of dissatisfaction, not motivation. If you want motivation, you have to change the work, through job enrichment.

Herzberg is therefore the theory to reach for whenever a question proposes solving a motivation problem with money.

</Chunk>

## Financial methods

| Method | How it works | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Piecework | paid per unit produced | quantity over quality, no teamwork, unsuitable for service work |
| Commission | percentage of sales value | aggressive selling, mis-selling, unstable income |
| Bonus | extra payment for hitting a target | expected next year, and demotivating if the target is missed by a whisker |
| Profit share | a share of business profits | individual effort is barely visible in the total, so the link is weak |
| Performance-related pay | more pay for meeting agreed targets | needs fair, measurable targets, and it can damage teamwork |

The common thread: financial methods work best where **output is measurable and individual**. In a service business where quality matters more than volume, they distort behaviour.

## Non-financial methods

- **Delegation** — passing authority down. Shows trust, develops people, and it is the manager's accountability that stays.
- **Consultation** — asking before deciding. Cheap and effective, and it must be genuine, because consulting and then ignoring the answer is worse than not asking.
- **Empowerment** — authority to decide about your own work. Strongly motivating, and it needs training and clear boundaries.
- **Team working** — Mayo in practice. Builds belonging and peer support, and it can hide poor individual performance.
- **Flexible working** — hours or location to suit. Improves retention, especially for parents and carers.
- **Job enrichment** — more challenging, more responsible work. This is the Herzberg motivator and the most powerful of the three "job" methods.
- **Job rotation** — different tasks at the same level. Reduces boredom, adds variety, and does not add responsibility.
- **Job enlargement** — more tasks at the same level. Be careful: done badly this is just more work, and employees see through it.

**Learn the distinction:** enrichment is *deeper* (more responsibility), enlargement is *wider* (more tasks), rotation is *sideways* (different tasks). Only enrichment is a Herzberg motivator.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Resort work concentrates the problem. Staff often live on site, far from family, for months at a time. In Maslow's terms, physiological and safety needs are handled by the employer, accommodation and food are provided, so pay alone is not the binding constraint. **Social needs become the pressure point**, which is why staff facilities, team activities and time off matter so much to retention.

In Herzberg's terms, better staff quarters remove dissatisfaction but do not motivate. What motivates is progression: training a waiter towards a supervisory role, or a boat crew member towards a dive qualification. That is job enrichment, and it also solves the recruitment problem in **1.3.4.2**, because it grows scarce skills internally.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Heavily, and often as the 20-mark essay. Define (2) on any theory or method; Explain (4) on one method; Assess (10) or Evaluate (20) on how a business should improve motivation.

**How to write the 20-mark essay:**

1. **Diagnose first.** Use the source. Is turnover high, is productivity low, are staff leaving after three months? Name the problem before proposing a solution.
2. **Apply one theory properly**, not four badly. Herzberg or Maslow is usually the most useful.
3. **Propose two methods**, one financial and one non-financial, and develop each chain to the effect on the business.
4. **Weigh them.** Cost, how fast they work, whether they fit this workforce.
5. **Judge**, and say what it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Herzberg's core point**: money removes dissatisfaction rather than creating motivation, so a pay rise is often the expensive answer to the wrong question.
- **It depends on the workforce.** Low-paid staff have unmet lower needs, so Maslow says start there. Well-paid bored staff need enrichment.
- **Financial incentives distort behaviour.** Piecework raises output and can lower quality.
- **Cost against benefit.** Compare the cost of the scheme with the saving from lower turnover, using the figures if given.
- **The theories are old and were tested on narrow groups**, so they are a guide rather than a formula.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing enrichment, enlargement and rotation.** Deeper, wider, sideways. Only enrichment adds responsibility.
- **Saying pay motivates, without qualification.** Herzberg's whole point is that it prevents dissatisfaction instead.
- **Describing all four theories and applying none.** One theory applied to the source beats four summarised.
- **Recommending piecework for a service business.** It rewards volume, and service is judged on quality.
- **Forgetting the cost.** Every motivation method costs money or management time.
- **Treating Maslow's levels as rigid.** They are a guide, and people differ.
- **Claiming motivated staff automatically raise profit.** Develop the chain through productivity, turnover and service quality, and note what has to hold for it to work.
- **Confusing delegation with empowerment.** Delegation passes a specific task down; empowerment gives ongoing authority over your own work.

</Mistakes>
