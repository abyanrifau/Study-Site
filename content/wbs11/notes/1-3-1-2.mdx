## What research is for

The specification gives three uses, and examiners reward answers that name them.

- **Identify and anticipate customer needs and wants.** Not just what customers want now, but where they are heading.
- **Quantify likely demand.** Put a number on how many would buy, and at what price. This is what makes a business plan credible to a lender.
- **Gain insight into consumer behaviour.** Understand *why* people choose, so the marketing mix can be built around it.

Research reduces risk. It does not remove it, and that distinction earns evaluation marks.

<Chunk title="Methods of primary research" open>

- **Surveys and questionnaires** — reach a lot of people cheaply. Good for quantitative data. Badly worded questions produce useless answers.
- **Focus groups and consumer panels** — a small guided discussion. Rich qualitative insight into *why*. Small numbers, and a dominant voice can sway the group.
- **Face-to-face and telephone interviews** — allows follow-up questions and body language. Slow, costly, and the interviewer can influence answers.
- **Product trials and test marketing** — real behaviour rather than stated intention, which is far more reliable. Reveals the product to competitors early.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Methods of secondary research">

- **Websites and social media** — free, current, and huge. Comments and reviews are genuine unprompted opinion. Quality varies wildly.
- **Newspapers, magazines, TV and radio** — useful for market trends and competitor news.
- **Reports** — market reports and government statistics. Reliable and broad, but the paid ones are expensive and none of it is specific to you.
- **Databases** — a business's own sales records and customer data. Cheap, accurate, and already about your own customers. It only tells you about people who already buy from you.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Quantitative against qualitative">

Do not confuse this split with primary and secondary. They are two separate questions, and a method can be any combination of the two.

| | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gives you | numbers | reasons |
| Answers | how many, how much | why |
| Example | 62% would pay $5 | they distrust the packaging |
| Strength | measurable, comparable | depth and insight |
| Weakness | does not explain | hard to generalise from |

Strong businesses use both. The numbers size the opportunity; the words explain it.

</Chunk>

## Sampling

You cannot ask everybody, so you ask a sample and treat it as representative of the whole.

- **Random** — everyone has an equal chance of selection. Free of researcher bias, but can miss whole groups by luck, and you need a full list of the population.
- **Quota** — split the population into groups, then interview a set number from each. Quick and cheap. The researcher chooses who, so bias creeps in.
- **Stratified** — split the population into segments, then select at random within each, in proportion to that segment's size. The most representative of the three, and the most complex and costly.

Two things wreck a sample:

- **Sample size too small.** Ten people cannot tell you about a city. The smaller the sample, the wider the margin of error.
- **Bias.** Surveying only your existing customers, only people who happen to be in one street, or only those willing to stop and talk, all skew the result.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse owner on a local island wants to know whether to add a dive package.

**Secondary first,** because it is nearly free: national tourist arrival statistics, competitor websites, and the reviews left on booking platforms for nearby guesthouses. Reviews are unprompted qualitative data and often mention exactly what guests wished had been available.

**Then primary,** narrowly aimed: a short questionnaire given to guests at checkout, which is cheap and gets a decent response rate because they are already there.

Note the sampling problem. Asking only current guests tells you about people who already chose a guesthouse without diving. The customers who would have come *because* of diving are exactly the ones missing from the sample. That kind of point is worth an evaluation mark.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Test marketing at scale.** Large consumer goods firms routinely launch in one city or one country first. It is expensive research, but it uses real purchases rather than what people say they would do.
- **Databases in emerging markets.** Mobile money and loyalty apps have given firms in several African and South East Asian economies detailed transaction data on customers who were previously invisible to formal research.
- **Where secondary data is thin.** In a small economy, published market reports may not exist at all. Primary research stops being a luxury and becomes the only option, which raises the real cost of entering that market.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Usually as:

- **Define** (2 marks) — primary research, qualitative data, stratified sampling.
- **Explain** (4 marks) — one advantage or drawback of a named method, developed with a reason and an example.
- **Assess** (10 marks) — whether a particular business should use a particular method. This is where the marks are.

**Getting the evaluation marks.** Never judge a method in the abstract. Judge it for *this* business:

- **Cost against the size of the decision.** A $200,000 launch justifies real research. A new flavour does not.
- **The size of the business.** A start-up with no cash uses secondary data and its own records first.
- **How fast the market moves.** In a fast-changing market, data six months old may already be wrong.
- **Whether the sample can be trusted.** Comment on sample size and bias if the source gives you numbers. This is the single most reliable way to pick up evaluation marks on this topic.

Then conclude: which method, and why that one *given the constraint that matters most here*.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Mixing up primary/secondary with quantitative/qualitative.** They are two different questions. A questionnaire is primary and usually quantitative; a focus group is primary and qualitative; a government report is secondary and quantitative.
- **Saying secondary research is "less accurate".** It is often more reliable than a small survey. The real weakness is that it was collected for somebody else's purpose, so it may not fit your question and may be out of date.
- **Claiming research removes risk.** It reduces it. Plenty of well-researched products still fail, and saying so is how you show judgement.
- **Listing methods without applying them.** "They could use surveys, focus groups and interviews" earns very little. One method, matched to this business, developed properly, earns much more.
- **Ignoring the sample details in the source.** If the paper tells you 40 people were surveyed outside one shop, the examiner has put that there deliberately. Use it.
- **Treating what people say as what they will do.** Stated intention and actual purchase differ, which is exactly why test marketing exists.

</Mistakes>
