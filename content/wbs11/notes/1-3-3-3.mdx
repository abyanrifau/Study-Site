## Types of promotion

- **Advertising** — paid messages through TV, radio, print, outdoor, online, search. Broad reach, but expensive and easy to ignore.
- **Sales promotion** — money off, buy one get one free, coupons, loyalty points. Fast effect on volume, but it trains customers to wait for the discount and it cuts the margin.
- **Personal selling** — a person selling directly. Essential in B2B and for complex or expensive products. Costly per customer.
- **Public relations** — unpaid coverage, press releases, events, sponsorship. Cheap and credible, but you do not control what is said.
- **Direct marketing** — email, post, messaging straight to named customers. Measurable and targeted, and easily seen as spam.
- **Social media** — cheap reach, two-way, and shareable. It is also public and unforgiving when something goes wrong.

The choice depends on who you are selling to, how much you have, and how complex the product is. Mass consumer product: advertising and social media. Industrial machinery: personal selling.

## Types of branding

- **Manufacturer brand** — owned by the producer, sold through many retailers. Coca-Cola, Samsung.
- **Own-label brand** — owned by the retailer, sold only in its stores. Supermarket value and premium ranges.
- **Generic** — effectively unbranded, competing on price alone.

There is also **corporate branding**, where the company name is the brand across everything it sells, against **individual branding**, where each product has its own identity. Corporate branding is cheaper and spreads reputation; it also means one scandal damages everything.

## Why a strong brand is worth building

The specification names three benefits, and you should be able to chain each one.

<Chunk title="The three benefits, developed" open>

**Added value.** Customers pay more for the branded version of a near-identical product, so the gap between price and input cost widens. Link to the added value formula in **1.3.1.3**.

**Ability to charge premium prices.** The brand is the justification for the higher price. Without it, a price above rivals simply loses customers.

**Reduced price elasticity of demand.** Loyal customers are less willing to switch, so a price rise loses fewer of them. This is the most valuable benefit to state, because it connects branding to the pricing decision in **1.3.2.4** and protects the margin.

Add two more that follow: a brand makes launching new products easier, because customers already trust the name, and it makes retailers more willing to stock you.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Ways to build a brand">

- **USPs and differentiation** — the most durable route, because it is about the product itself.
- **Advertising** — builds awareness and association. Expensive and slow.
- **Sponsorship** — borrows the audience and the values of an event or team.
- **Social media** — cheap, fast, and the only route that is genuinely two-way.

Note that all four cost money or attention, and none guarantees a return.

</Chunk>

## Changes to reflect social trends

- **Viral marketing** — content designed to be shared. Enormous reach for almost no spend, but you cannot reliably make something go viral, and it can go viral for the wrong reason.
- **Social media** — has shifted power to consumers. A complaint is now public and permanent.
- **Emotional branding** — selling identity and values rather than features: sustainability, belonging, authenticity. Effective, and dangerous if the business does not live up to the claim, because the gap becomes the story.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Emotional branding and the risk.** Brands across Europe and North America have built campaigns on sustainability and then faced accusations of greenwashing when supply chain practices did not match. The reputational cost was larger than the campaign's benefit.

**Own-label gaining ground.** European supermarket own-label ranges have taken share from manufacturer brands during periods of squeezed incomes, which is negative income elasticity of demand showing up as a branding shift.

**Social media as the whole channel.** For small Maldivian guesthouses and dive schools, an Instagram feed of real photographs plus reviews on booking platforms does the work that advertising budgets do elsewhere. Reach costs almost nothing; the constraint is content, not cash.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on branding, USP, viral marketing; Explain (4) on one benefit of a strong brand or one drawback of a promotional method; Assess (10) on whether a business should spend a given sum on a particular promotion.

**Answering "should they spend $X on promotion?"** Always weigh the spend against the return:

1. What will it do to demand? Shift the curve right, and make it less elastic.
2. What will that be worth? Use the figures: extra units times contribution per unit.
3. Compare with the cost of the campaign. If it costs $50,000 and generates $30,000 of extra profit, the demand curve shifted and the business lost money.
4. Then evaluate: can a small business afford it at all, will competitors respond, is there a cheaper route such as social media, and how long will the effect last?

That cost-versus-return structure is the single most reliable way to score on promotion questions.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Treating promotion as free.** Every method costs money or time. The comparison with the return is where the marks are.
- **Confusing branding with advertising.** Advertising is one way to build a brand; the brand is the identity itself.
- **Saying a strong brand "means more sales" and stopping.** Develop it: added value, premium price, lower elasticity.
- **Claiming viral marketing can be planned.** You can make content shareable; you cannot make it go viral.
- **Ignoring the risk in emotional branding.** If the claim and the practice do not match, the gap becomes the story.
- **Recommending TV advertising for a small business.** Match the method to the budget and the market.
- **Forgetting sales promotions cut the margin** and can train customers to wait for discounts.

</Mistakes>
