## What the entrepreneur actually does

Four things, and it is worth being able to name them rather than talking vaguely about "taking risks":

1. **Spots the opportunity** — sees an unmet need, or a better way of meeting an existing one.
2. **Organises the resources** — finance, premises, equipment, suppliers, people.
3. **Takes the risk** — usually personally, through savings, a loan, or unlimited liability.
4. **Makes the decisions** — and lives with them.

## Creating and setting up

The start-up phase. Research the market, write the business plan, raise finance, register the business, find premises and suppliers, recruit, launch.

The business plan matters here for a specific reason: it is what convinces a lender or investor to part with money. It also forces the entrepreneur to test their own assumptions in writing, which is where optimistic sales forecasts usually meet reality.

## Running and expanding

Different work, and it is where many founders struggle. Now it is about cash flow, staff, quality, customers and growth, day after day. The excitement of starting is replaced by the discipline of operating.

This is the same transition as the entrepreneur-to-leader problem in **1.3.4.5**: the skills that get a business started are not the skills that make it bigger.

## Intrapreneurship

Entrepreneurial behaviour inside an existing business: an employee developing a new product, process or market using the company's resources.

**Why businesses want it:** it produces innovation without the employee taking the personal financial risk, and it keeps ambitious people from leaving to start something themselves.

**Why it is hard:** large organisations have procedures, budgets and approval processes that are designed to prevent unplanned activity. Intrapreneurship requires deliberately protecting people from that, which some businesses do by giving small teams their own budget and freedom.

## Barriers to entrepreneurship

- **Finance.** The most common barrier. Lenders want security and a track record, and a start-up has neither.
- **Lack of skills.** Most founders are good at one thing and have to learn accounting, marketing, employment law and operations.
- **Fear of failure.** Both personal and cultural. Where business failure carries a lasting stigma, fewer people try.
- **Regulation.** Licences, permits, registration and tax compliance all take time and money, and the burden falls hardest on the smallest businesses.
- **Competition.** Established firms have brands, scale and supplier relationships a new entrant does not.
- **Lack of ideas, or of a market.** An opportunity has to actually exist.

## Anticipating risk and uncertainty

Back to **1.3.1.1**, and examiners like the link.

**Risk** can be quantified from data, so it is managed with forecasting, insurance, contingency funds and diversification.

**Uncertainty** cannot be quantified, so it is managed with flexibility: keeping cash in reserve, avoiding long fixed commitments, and planning for more than one scenario.

A good entrepreneur does not avoid risk, which would mean not starting at all. They take risks they have understood and priced.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

The guesthouse sector is a clear case of opportunity created by a change in the rules. When the law allowed tourist accommodation on inhabited islands, an entire market opened for local entrepreneurs who could not have built an island resort.

The barriers were exactly the standard ones: finance, since a bank wants security a first-time operator does not have; skills, since running a guesthouse means marketing, accounting and food safety, not just hospitality; and regulation, since licensing and registration take time.

And the uncertainty was real. Nobody starting in that first wave could have priced the possibility that international travel would stop for a year. That is uncertainty rather than risk, and the operators who survived it were the ones who had kept cash and avoided heavy fixed commitments.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on entrepreneur, enterprise, intrapreneurship; Explain (4) on one barrier facing this entrepreneur; Assess (10) on whether someone should start a business, or on the main barrier they face.

**When asked about barriers:** identify the barrier that actually binds for *this* person from the source, rather than listing all six. If the source mentions savings of $8,000 and start-up costs of $40,000, finance is the barrier and you should say so with the numbers.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Barriers differ by person and place.** Finance is harder without collateral; regulation is heavier in some countries.
- **Some barriers can be reduced rather than removed.** Starting smaller, leasing instead of buying, keeping a job while starting.
- **Risk is not the same as uncertainty**, and the two need different responses.
- **A business plan does not make it happen.** It tests the idea and persuades a lender, and plenty of well-planned businesses fail.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying an entrepreneur just "takes risks".** Name all four parts of the role.
- **Confusing entrepreneurship with intrapreneurship.** The intrapreneur uses the company's resources and does not take the personal financial risk.
- **Listing every barrier instead of identifying the binding one.**
- **Treating risk and uncertainty as the same.** They are separately examinable.
- **Assuming a business plan guarantees success.** It reduces risk; it does not remove it.
- **Forgetting the second phase.** Running and expanding is in the specification and is where most founders struggle.
- **Ignoring the numbers in the source** when discussing finance as a barrier.

</Mistakes>
