## Market and product orientation

Two opposite starting points.

**Market orientation** — research first, then build what customers said they want. Lower risk of launching something nobody wants, and easier to justify to a lender. But research costs money and takes time, and customers cannot describe a product that does not exist yet.

**Product orientation** — build what the business is good at, then find buyers. This is how genuinely new categories appear, because no survey would have asked for them. It is also how most expensive failures happen.

In practice almost every business is somewhere between the two. The examinable point is the **trade-off**: market orientation reduces risk but rarely produces breakthroughs; product orientation can create a whole new market but stakes a lot on a hunch.

## Market mapping

A market map plots every product in a market against two characteristics. Price against quality is the usual pair, but it could be traditional against modern, or basic against luxury.

What a map is good for:

- **Spotting gaps.** An empty space may be an unserved need.
- **Spotting crowding.** A cluster shows where competition is fiercest, and where a new entrant would struggle.
- **Checking your own position.** Are you where you think you are?

The catch, and it is the standard evaluation point: **an empty space is not automatically an opportunity.** It may be empty because nobody wants a product there, or because it cannot be made profitably at that price. A gap on paper is a hypothesis, not a plan.

## Segmentation

Segmentation divides customers into groups that behave similarly, so each group can be given the right product, price, promotion and place.

Common bases:

- **Demographic** — age, gender, income, family size, occupation.
- **Geographic** — country, region, urban or rural, climate.
- **Psychographic** — lifestyle, values, attitudes, personality.
- **Behavioural** — how often they buy, how loyal they are, what occasion they buy for.

Why it helps: marketing is less wasteful, the product fits better, customers will often pay more for something aimed at them, and a small firm can dominate one segment instead of losing across the whole market.

Why it can go wrong: serving several segments means more product versions and higher costs, segments can be too small to be worth it, and over-segmenting fragments your own brand.

## Competitive advantage, differentiation and added value

These three connect, and examiners like to see the chain.

**Differentiation** is the cause. Make the product genuinely distinct: better design, better service, faster delivery, ethical sourcing, a stronger brand, a feature nobody else has.

**Competitive advantage** is the result. Being different in a way customers care about means they choose you over rivals. There are broadly two routes: be cheaper than everyone, or be different enough to justify a higher price. Trying to do both at once usually achieves neither.

**Added value** is how it shows up in the numbers.

<Formula name="Added value" note="Per unit. Note it is not profit: it takes off only bought-in materials and services, not the business's own wages, rent or marketing.">
Added value = Selling price − Cost of bought-in materials and services
</Formula>

<Worked>

A café buys the ingredients for a sandwich for **$1.20** and sells it for **$4.50**.

`Added value = $4.50 − $1.20 = $3.30 per sandwich`

Now it moves to a beachfront location, uses local tuna, plates it properly and sells the same sandwich for **$7.00**. Ingredients now cost **$1.90**.

`Added value = $7.00 − $1.90 = $5.10 per sandwich`

**Reading the answer:** added value rose by $1.80 a unit even though ingredient costs went up. But the rent on a beachfront site and the extra staff time are not in this calculation at all, so **added value rising does not prove profit rose**. Spotting that is worth an analysis mark.

</Worked>

Ways to add value: convenience, speed, branding, packaging, customer service, product range, quality, location, and a good story about where it came from.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Two businesses sell "a night's stay" and sit at opposite ends of the same market map.

An **island resort** is positioned premium and high price. Its added value per guest is enormous, because the raw inputs are food, fuel and staff time while the nightly rate is many hundreds of dollars. The differentiation is privacy, an entire island, and service.

A **local island guesthouse** is positioned basic and low price. Added value per guest is far smaller, so it has to compete on volume, location and reviews.

Neither is better positioned. They are aimed at different segments. The interesting question, and a good evaluation question, is what happens to a guesthouse that tries to move up the map towards the resort position: it takes on much higher costs while still lacking the one thing the resort sells, which is exclusivity.

</RealWorld>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

- **Differentiation on ethics.** Fairtrade-certified coffee from Kenya or Colombia sells at a premium in European supermarkets on the strength of how it was sourced, not how it tastes.
- **Segmentation by income in one country.** Indian consumer goods firms commonly sell the same brand in single-use sachets and in large family packs, aimed at very different income segments in the same market.
- **Repositioning.** Several airlines have moved from full-service to low-cost positions, and the hard part was never the pricing. It was shedding the cost base that the old position was built on.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.**

- **Define** (2 marks) — added value, market orientation, segmentation, USP.
- **Construct** (4 marks) — draw a market map. Label both axes with what they measure and both ends of each axis, plot the products named in the source, and mark the gap. Marks are lost mostly on unlabelled axes.
- **Calculate** (4 marks) — added value per unit, or added value across a given output.
- **Assess** (10 marks) — whether the business should target a particular segment or reposition.

**Getting the evaluation marks.** On a positioning question:

- **Is the gap real?** Say explicitly that an empty space may mean no demand, not an opportunity.
- **Can the business actually get there?** A firm positioned on low price rarely has the brand, the skills or the cost base to go premium.
- **What does the source say about rivals?** If two competitors already sit nearby, the gap is smaller than it looks.
- **Added value is not profit.** If you have used the formula, say what it leaves out.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing added value with profit.** Added value subtracts only bought-in materials and services. Profit subtracts everything.
- **Drawing a market map with unlabelled axes.** It earns almost nothing. Label what each axis measures and both extremes.
- **Assuming every gap is an opportunity.** The most common weak answer on this topic. Always question whether demand exists there.
- **Confusing segmentation with positioning.** Segmentation is splitting up the customers. Positioning is where your product sits against rivals in those customers' minds.
- **Saying market orientation is simply "better".** It lowers risk but costs money and rarely produces anything genuinely new. Both approaches have a place.
- **Listing ways to add value without linking to price.** Added value only exists if the customer will actually pay more, or buy more often, because of it.
- **Claiming a USP lasts.** Most can be copied. Saying how long the advantage might survive is exactly the kind of judgement that scores.

</Mistakes>
