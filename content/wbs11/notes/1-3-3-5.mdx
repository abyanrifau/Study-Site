## The three channels

<Figure caption="Each stage added widens reach but takes a share of the margin and a degree of control away from the producer.">
  <DistributionDiagram />
</Figure>

**Four stage: producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer.** The traditional route for food, drink and household goods. The wholesaler buys in bulk, breaks the bulk down and sells on to many small retailers.

**Three stage: producer → retailer → consumer.** Common where retailers are large enough to buy in bulk themselves, so the wholesaler adds nothing. Supermarkets buy this way.

**Two stage: producer → consumer.** Direct selling: a factory shop, a farmers' market, a website, a guesthouse taking its own bookings.

## The trade-off

| | More stages | Fewer stages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach | wide, into many outlets | limited to your own channels |
| Margin kept | lower, each intermediary takes a cut | higher, the whole margin is yours |
| Control of price and presentation | weak | strong |
| Customer relationship and data | owned by the retailer | owned by you |
| Cost and effort | the intermediary does the work | you do storage, delivery, payments, returns |
| Cash flow | one large payment from a few buyers | many small payments |

The examinable judgement is that **a shorter channel is not automatically better**. Cutting out the wholesaler keeps their margin but transfers their job to you, and if you cannot do that job as cheaply, you are worse off.

## Choosing a channel

- **Type of product.** Perishable or fragile favours short channels; low-value everyday goods need wide distribution.
- **Size of the business.** A small producer cannot supply thousands of shops directly and needs a wholesaler.
- **The market position.** A premium brand cannot sell through discount outlets without damaging the position. That is distribution targeting.
- **Geography.** Serving scattered islands or a large country needs intermediaries unless you build the logistics yourself.
- **What customers expect.** People buy some things online and some in person, and forcing the other one loses sales.

## Changes in distribution

**Online retailing** has made the two stage channel available to almost anyone. A small producer can now reach customers directly without shelf space, which:

- keeps the full margin;
- gives direct customer data and the relationship;
- lowers the barriers to entry, so competition rises;
- transfers delivery, returns and customer service to the producer.

**Multichannel distribution** is now the norm: sell direct online *and* through retailers. That maximises reach, and it creates a tension, because your own website competes with the retailers stocking you.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Geography makes distribution unusually hard. Goods arrive at one port, then have to reach islands scattered over hundreds of kilometres by boat. That naturally produces **long channels**: importer, then wholesaler, then a small island retailer, with each stage taking a margin and adding delay.

It also explains why prices rise the further an island is from the capital. The product is identical; the distribution cost is not.

Tourism runs the other way. A guesthouse can sell through international booking platforms, which is effectively a retailer taking a commission of roughly 15 to 20%, or take direct bookings through its own site and keep it all. Most do both: the platform brings the customers who would never have found them, and the direct channel keeps the margin on repeat guests. That is multichannel distribution and the tension in it, in one business.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Usually Define (2) or Explain (4), and as part of a wider marketing mix Assess (10). Rarely a topic on its own for 20 marks.

**When asked whether to sell direct:** the strong answer works out what the intermediary actually does and whether the business can do it more cheaply. If a platform takes 18% but supplies most of the customers, cutting it out saves 18% and loses the bookings.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **It depends on whether the business can absorb the intermediary's job**, including storage, delivery and returns.
- **It depends on market position.** The wrong outlet damages a premium brand more than the extra sales are worth.
- **It depends on where customers already look.** Distribution has to be where the demand is.
- **Multichannel creates internal competition** between your own site and your retailers.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Assuming shorter is always better.** The intermediary's margin is payment for work that someone still has to do.
- **Counting the stages wrongly.** Four stage has a wholesaler; three stage does not; two stage is direct.
- **Ignoring the cost of doing it yourself.** Delivery, packaging, payment processing and returns all cost money.
- **Forgetting market position.** Distribution is part of the mix and must match the price and the brand.
- **Treating online as free.** A website, photography, payment fees and delivery are real costs.
- **Not using the numbers.** If the source gives a retailer's margin or a platform commission, the calculation is where the application marks are.

</Mistakes>
