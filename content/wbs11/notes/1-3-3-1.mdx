## Objectives, strategy and mix

Three levels, and examiners notice when students confuse them.

- **Objective** — the target. "Raise market share from 6% to 9% in two years."
- **Strategy** — the long-term plan to get there. "Move into the premium segment and sell online."
- **Mix** — the tools used day to day. Product, price, promotion, place.

The specification names three common marketing objectives: **increase market share**, **increase revenue**, and **build a brand**. Good objectives are measurable and have a deadline, otherwise you cannot tell whether the strategy worked.

## The product life cycle

<Chunk title="What happens at each stage" open>

| Stage | Sales | Cash flow | What marketing does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Development | none | heavily negative | research and design, no revenue yet |
| Introduction | low | negative | heavy promotion, often penetration or skimming pricing |
| Growth | rising fast | turning positive | widen distribution, build the brand |
| Maturity | high, flat | strongly positive | defend share, compete on price, extension strategies |
| Decline | falling | falling | cut costs, harvest, or withdraw |

The examinable point is that **cash flow and sales are not the same shape**. A product in growth is selling well and may still be consuming cash, because it needs stock, staff and promotion.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Extension strategies">

Ways to delay decline:

- A new variant or flavour.
- New packaging or a redesign.
- A new target market, including a new country.
- A new use for the same product.
- More advertising, or a price cut.

Extension is usually cheaper than developing a new product, which is why businesses reach for it. But it only works if the underlying demand is still there. If tastes have genuinely moved on, extension is money spent delaying the inevitable.

</Chunk>

## The Boston Matrix

<Figure caption="Two axes only: how fast the market is growing, and how big your share is relative to rivals. Each quadrant implies a different decision.">
  <BostonMatrixDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Star** — high share, high growth. Needs investment to hold position, but should become a cash cow as the market matures.
- **Cash cow** — high share, low growth. Generates surplus cash. Milk it and use the cash to fund stars and question marks.
- **Question mark** — low share, high growth. Could become a star with investment, or a dog without. A genuine decision point.
- **Dog** — low share, low growth. Usually withdrawn, though it may be kept if it completes a range.

**What a balanced portfolio looks like:** cash cows funding stars, a few question marks being tested, dogs pruned. A business that is all cash cows has no future; one that is all question marks has no cash.

<Chunk title="Limitations worth knowing">

- It uses only two measures and ignores everything else: brand, profitability, how products support each other.
- "Dog" is not automatically a withdrawal. A low-share product may be strategically necessary or highly profitable.
- Market growth and share have to be measured, and the answer depends on how narrowly you define the market.
- It is a snapshot. Positions move, and the matrix does not tell you how fast.

</Chunk>

## Strategy for different markets

- **Mass market** — compete on brand, availability and price. Broad promotion, wide distribution, thin margins, high volume.
- **Niche market** — compete on fit and expertise. Focused promotion, premium pricing, high margins, low volume.
- **B2B** — fewer, larger customers. Relationships, technical specification, negotiated prices, personal selling rather than mass advertising. Buying decisions are rational and often made by committee.
- **B2C** — many small customers. Branding, emotion, convenience and advertising matter far more.

The trap is applying B2C thinking to a B2B market. A business buying industrial equipment is not swayed by a social media campaign; it wants reliability, service and total cost of ownership.

## Customer loyalty

Loyal customers cost less to sell to, buy more often, are less price sensitive and recommend you. Businesses build loyalty through consistent quality, service, loyalty schemes, community and brand identity.

This connects to **1.3.2.4**: loyalty makes demand less price elastic, which is what protects the margin.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Extension in practice.** Coca-Cola has extended a product first sold in the 1880s with diet, zero-sugar and flavoured variants, new pack sizes and constant repositioning. The core product is in permanent maturity and has been kept there deliberately.

**Portfolio thinking in a small economy.** A Maldivian dive operator might have a cash cow in standard day trips, a star in liveaboard trips as that segment grows, a question mark in freediving courses, and a dog in a boat-hire line it keeps only because a few resorts expect it. Same business, four different decisions.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on any of the terms; Explain (4) on why a product needs an extension strategy; Assess (10) on whether a business should invest in a particular product or drop it.

**Answering a Boston Matrix question:**

1. Classify the product using the data given. Say which quadrant and why, using the share and growth figures.
2. State the standard implication for that quadrant.
3. Then challenge it. The marks are in the challenge, not the classification.

**Getting evaluation marks.** Use the limitations above, and add the portfolio view: the right decision for one product depends on what else the business sells. Dropping a dog that funds nothing is easy; dropping one that keeps a key retailer stocking your range is not.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing objective, strategy and mix.** The objective is the target, the strategy is the plan, the mix is the tools.
- **Drawing the product life cycle with cash flow identical to sales.** They differ, and that difference is examinable.
- **Assuming a dog must be dropped.** Give a reason it might be kept.
- **Treating the Boston Matrix as a decision rather than a starting point.** It has only two axes.
- **Saying maturity means the product is failing.** Maturity is usually the most profitable stage.
- **Applying B2C tactics to a B2B market**, or forgetting the question said business customers.
- **Extension strategies without a cost.** Every extension costs money and may only delay decline.

</Mistakes>
