## Management against leadership

Both are needed, and they are not the same thing.

| Management | Leadership |
| --- | --- |
| Organises and controls | sets direction and inspires |
| Works within the existing plan | decides what the plan should be |
| Monitors, measures, corrects | persuades, motivates, changes |
| Authority comes from the position | influence comes from the person |
| Asks how and when | asks what and why |

A business with strong management and weak leadership is efficient at going in the wrong direction. The reverse has a compelling direction and never delivers.

<Chunk title="Autocratic" open>

The leader decides and instructs. Little or no consultation.

**Works when:** decisions must be fast, in a crisis, where safety is critical, where staff are inexperienced or the task is simple and repeated.

**Costs:** demotivating for capable people, no use made of their ideas, and everything depends on one person being right. High turnover follows.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Paternalistic">

The leader still decides, but explains why and takes employees' welfare into account. Often described as parental: "I know best, and I have your interests at heart."

**Works when:** staff need direction but also reassurance, and in businesses with a strong sense of duty to employees.

**Costs:** it is still the leader deciding, so capable staff can find it patronising, and it depends on their trust being warranted.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Democratic">

Employees are involved in the decision. Ideas are gathered and discussed before the leader decides, or the group decides together.

**Works when:** staff are skilled and experienced, the decision affects them, and there is time.

**Costs:** slow, and it can produce compromise rather than the best answer. In a crisis it is the wrong tool. And consulting then overruling is worse than never asking.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Laissez-faire">

The leader sets the goal and steps back. Employees decide how, and largely when.

**Works when:** staff are expert, self-motivated professionals, in creative or research work.

**Costs:** without capable people it becomes drift, with no coordination, missed deadlines and duplicated effort. It is often mistaken for weak leadership, and sometimes it is.

</Chunk>

## Choosing a style

There is no best style, and saying so is worth a mark. What decides it:

- **How urgent the decision is.** No time means autocratic.
- **How capable and experienced the staff are.** Skilled staff can be trusted with democratic or laissez-faire; new staff need direction.
- **How important the decision is.** High-stakes decisions tend to move up the hierarchy.
- **How much staff buy-in matters.** Changes that staff have to implement willingly need involvement.
- **The culture and the sector.** Safety-critical operations are more directive by necessity.

Good leaders **switch styles** to the situation. The same resort manager who consults on shift patterns will be entirely autocratic during a fire evacuation, and both are correct.

## From entrepreneur to leader

The specification names this explicitly, and it comes up often.

An entrepreneur starting a business does everything: sells, buys, hires, keeps the books, fixes the problems. That works up to maybe ten employees. Beyond that it becomes the constraint on growth.

Why the transition is hard:

- **Letting go.** The business is their creation and nobody else will care as much. Delegating feels like risk.
- **Different skills.** Spotting an opportunity and running a hundred-person organisation are different jobs.
- **Becoming the bottleneck.** If every decision needs the founder, growth stops at the founder's capacity.
- **Needing structure.** Informal arrangements that worked with five people fail with fifty, so hierarchy, roles and systems become necessary, and founders often resist them as bureaucracy.
- **Trusting specialists** who know more than they do about finance, marketing or operations.

This links straight to **1.3.4.3**: growing beyond the founder means building a structure and decentralising, which the founder has to be willing to do.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse founder who runs a six-room property personally, taking bookings, greeting guests, handling suppliers, can do it all. Growing to three properties across two islands makes that impossible: they cannot be on two islands at once.

The transition is exactly the one above. They have to appoint managers, set standards those managers apply without being asked, and accept decisions they would have made differently. Founders who cannot make that shift tend to stay at one property, which is a legitimate choice, and one that should be recognised as a choice rather than a failure.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on any style or on the management/leadership distinction; Explain (4) on why a style suits a situation; Assess (10) on which style a business should use; and sometimes a 20-mark question on whether a founder can lead a larger business.

**Answering "which style should they use?"**

1. Never answer in the abstract. Identify the situation from the source: urgency, staff capability, stakes.
2. Recommend a style and chain the reasoning to the effect on the business.
3. Note when a different style would be needed instead.
4. Judge, and say a good leader adapts rather than having one fixed style.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **No style is universally best**, and the best leaders switch.
- **Democratic leadership depends on staff capability.** With inexperienced staff it produces poor decisions slowly.
- **Autocratic is not automatically bad.** In a crisis it is correct.
- **The cost is time.** Involvement is slower, and sometimes that is decisive.
- **Style has to match the culture**, and changing style suddenly unsettles people.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying democratic leadership is always best.** It is slow and needs capable staff.
- **Confusing paternalistic with democratic.** Paternalistic still means the leader decides; they just explain and care.
- **Treating laissez-faire as no leadership.** It is a deliberate choice that works with experts and fails with novices.
- **Confusing management with leadership.** Management organises what exists; leadership sets direction.
- **Recommending a style with no reference to the situation.** The situation is the answer.
- **Forgetting the entrepreneur-to-leader content.** It is named in the specification and often examined.
- **Saying a founder "must" change.** Staying small is a valid choice, and saying so shows judgement.

</Mistakes>
