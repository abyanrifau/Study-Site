## Characteristics and skills

Two lists, and it is worth separating them because a question may ask for either.

**Characteristics** — the traits:

- **Determination and resilience.** Most businesses hit a serious problem in the first two years. The willingness to keep going is what distinguishes those that continue.
- **Willingness to take risks.** Not recklessness: accepting a priced, understood risk.
- **Initiative.** Seeing what needs doing and doing it, with nobody to tell you.
- **Self-confidence.** Needed to sell, to hire, to borrow, and to be told no repeatedly.
- **Ability to spot an opportunity.** Noticing the unmet need that others walk past.
- **Innovation and creativity.** Finding a better way, not just a copy.

**Skills** — the learnable capabilities:

- **Communication**, for customers, staff, suppliers and lenders.
- **Financial literacy**, especially cash flow, because profitable businesses still fail by running out of cash.
- **Organisation** and time management.
- **Decision-making**, usually with incomplete information.
- **Negotiation**, with suppliers, landlords and customers.

The examinable distinction: characteristics are largely who you are; skills can be acquired, which is why a founder lacking financial literacy can hire or learn rather than fail.

## Financial motives

**Profit maximisation** — the largest possible profit. Simple, and it is what economic theory assumes.

**Profit satisficing** — enough profit to be satisfied. This is where the interesting exam material is, because it explains behaviour that maximisation cannot.

A guesthouse owner who could open a second property and chooses not to is not being irrational. They are trading potential profit for time, simplicity and less stress. A family business that refuses outside investment is protecting control. Satisficing is a deliberate choice, and recognising it earns evaluation marks when a question assumes every business wants to grow.

## Non-financial motives

The specification names four.

- **Ethical stance.** Trading in line with a moral position even where it costs money: paying suppliers fairly, refusing certain customers or materials.
- **Social entrepreneurship.** The business exists to solve a social or environmental problem, with profit as the means rather than the end. Profits are largely reinvested into the purpose.
- **Independence.** Being your own boss. Consistently one of the most commonly given reasons for starting a business, and often more important than money.
- **Home working.** Fitting work around family or avoiding a commute, which for many founders is the whole point.

Notice that all four can conflict with profit. A business with a strong ethical stance accepts a lower margin. A social enterprise reinvests instead of distributing. That trade-off is the heart of most questions on this topic.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Social entrepreneurship.** Microfinance institutions in Bangladesh and across South Asia were set up to give credit to people banks would not serve, with sustainability rather than maximum profit as the goal. They are businesses, and their objective is social.

**Satisficing in practice.** Across the world most small businesses stay small by choice. A single successful guesthouse, restaurant or workshop can provide a good living, and expanding means debt, staff and risk that the owner may simply not want. Treating this as a failure of ambition misreads the objective.

**Ethical stance with a commercial edge.** Fairtrade-certified producers accept the cost of certification because it opens premium markets, so an ethical position and a commercial strategy can point the same way. That is a useful nuance: ethics does not always cost.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2), especially satisficing and social entrepreneurship; Explain (4) on one motive or characteristic; Assess (10) on whether an entrepreneur has the characteristics needed, or whether they should pursue growth.

**The highest-value move on this topic:** when a question assumes a business wants to maximise profit, ask whether it does. If the source says the owner values their weekends, or set the business up to solve a problem, then the "obvious" profit-maximising recommendation may be the wrong advice for this owner.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Objectives differ, so the right decision differs.** Recommending expansion to a satisficer is bad advice.
- **Skills can be acquired or bought in; characteristics are harder to change.**
- **Non-financial motives usually cost money**, and the owner may accept that knowingly.
- **An ethical stance can also be commercial**, by opening premium markets and protecting reputation.
- **The business still has to be viable.** A social enterprise that cannot cover its costs helps nobody.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing profit satisficing with making a loss.** Satisficing still means a satisfactory profit, just not the maximum.
- **Confusing a social enterprise with a charity.** A social enterprise trades and earns revenue; its purpose is social.
- **Assuming every business wants to grow.** Independence and satisficing are legitimate objectives.
- **Mixing up characteristics and skills.** Resilience is a trait; financial literacy is a skill.
- **Listing traits with no application.** Say why that trait matters for this entrepreneur's situation.
- **Treating ethics as automatically costly.** Sometimes it opens markets and protects revenue.
- **Recommending profit maximisation when the source shows other motives.** Read what the owner actually wants.

</Mistakes>
