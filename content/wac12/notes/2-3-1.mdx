## The signs, which is where most marks are lost

**Non-cash adjustments.** Depreciation was deducted in arriving at operating profit but no cash left the business, so **add it back**. A **loss** on disposal is likewise added back; a **profit** on disposal is **deducted**, because the whole of the cash received appears separately under investing activities and would otherwise be double counted.

**Working capital movements.** The reliable way to think about it: cash moves in the **opposite** direction to an asset and the **same** direction as a liability.

| Movement | Effect on cash | Sign in the statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory **up** | cash spent on stock | negative |
| Inventory **down** | stock sold, not replaced | positive |
| Receivables **up** | customers owe more, paid less | negative |
| Receivables **down** | customers have paid | positive |
| Payables **up** | suppliers not yet paid | positive |
| Payables **down** | suppliers paid off | negative |

**Dividends and interest.** Dividends **paid** go in financing. Interest **paid** is shown within operating activities, after cash generated from operations. Both must be the amounts actually **paid** in cash, not the amounts charged or proposed, so adjust for opening and closing accruals if the question gives them.

<Worked title="Worked example: a profitable company losing cash">

Operating profit for the year **$210,000**. Depreciation charged **$85,000**. Profit on disposal of equipment **$9,000**, with proceeds of **$34,000**. New equipment bought for **$260,000**. A debenture issue raised **$150,000**. Dividends paid **$60,000**. Interest paid **$18,000**. Tax paid **$42,000**. Opening cash **$26,000**.

| | Opening $ | Closing $ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory | 140,000 | 196,000 |
| Trade receivables | 118,000 | 175,000 |
| Trade payables | 92,000 | 104,000 |

**Step 1 — operating activities.**

`Operating profit 210,000`

`Add depreciation +85,000`

`Less profit on disposal −9,000`

`Inventory rose by 56,000 → −56,000`

`Receivables rose by 57,000 → −57,000`

`Payables rose by 12,000 → +12,000`

`Cash generated from operations = 210,000 + 85,000 − 9,000 − 56,000 − 57,000 + 12,000 = $185,000`

`Interest paid −18,000; Tax paid −42,000`

`Net cash from operating activities = $125,000`

**Step 2 — investing activities.**

`Proceeds of sale +34,000; Purchase of equipment −260,000 = −$226,000`

**Step 3 — financing activities.**

`Debentures +150,000; Dividends −60,000 = +$90,000`

**Step 4 — the total.**

`Net change = 125,000 − 226,000 + 90,000 = −$11,000`

`Closing cash = 26,000 − 11,000 = $15,000`

**What the statement reveals.** The company made $210,000 of operating profit and its cash balance **fell** by $11,000. Two things caused it. Working capital absorbed **$101,000**, because inventory and receivables together rose by $113,000 against only $12,000 more credit from suppliers. And the company spent $226,000 net on non-current assets while raising only $150,000 of long-term finance, so **$76,000 of long-term investment was funded out of short-term resources**. That is a financing mismatch, and naming it is what earns the evaluation marks.

</Worked>

## Why liquidity matters so much

**A company fails when it runs out of cash, not when it makes a loss.** A profitable business with no cash cannot pay wages, suppliers or interest, and a supplier who is not paid stops supplying. Profit can be reported while cash is absent, because profit is recognised when a sale is made and cash arrives later, and because some expenses, such as depreciation, never involve cash at all.

**The classic patterns to recognise:**

- **Overtrading.** Sales grow fast, inventory and receivables grow with them, and the cash to fund that growth has to come from somewhere. Rapid growth consumes cash, which is counter-intuitive and very commonly examined.
- **Financing mismatch.** Long-life assets bought out of the overdraft, as in the worked example.
- **Unsustainable dividends.** Dividends paid out of past reserves while operating cash flow is negative.
- **Cash from asset sales.** Positive overall cash flow produced by selling non-current assets is not repeatable and often shrinks the capacity to earn.
- **Deferred payables.** A large rise in payables improves cash this year and is simply postponement.

**Why cash flow information is more reliable than profit.** Profit depends on judgements about depreciation rates, inventory valuation and provisions. Cash received and paid is a matter of record. Hence the saying worth having in mind: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact.

**Limitations of the statement.** It is historical, it explains what happened rather than what will happen, a single year can be distorted by timing around the year end, and it contains no forecast. A cash budget, from topic 2.4, is the forward-looking tool.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Frequently a Section A part or a Section B question: prepare the statement (14–20 marks), then comment on liquidity or advise the directors (8–12 marks).

**Method:**

1. **Compare the two statements of financial position** and write down every movement before you start the statement itself.
2. **Do the working capital movements as a small table**, with the sign, before transferring them.
3. **Reconstruct the non-current asset figure** if the purchase is not given directly: opening net book value + additions − depreciation − net book value of disposals = closing net book value. Solve for additions.
4. **Work out the net book value of the disposal** as proceeds − profit on disposal, or proceeds + loss on disposal.
5. **Check the closing cash figure** against the statement of financial position. If it does not agree, the error is almost always a sign.

**Structuring the comment on liquidity:**

1. **State whether operating cash flow is positive**, and compare it with operating profit. The gap is the story.
2. **Identify what absorbed the cash**, quoting the figures.
3. **Check how the investment was financed**, and name any mismatch between long-term assets and short-term finance.
4. **Check whether the dividend was covered** by operating cash flow.
5. **Judge, and recommend:** tighter credit control, inventory reduction, longer-term finance for long-term assets, or a smaller dividend.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Growth consumes cash**, so rising profit and falling cash are entirely consistent.
- **Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact.**
- **Distinguish repeatable from one-off cash flows.** Asset sales and a debenture issue cannot be repeated each year.
- **Long-term assets should be funded by long-term finance.**
- **A single year is not a trend**, and a cash budget would tell you more about the future than any historical statement.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Deducting depreciation instead of adding it back.**
- **Adding a profit on disposal** instead of deducting it, which double counts the proceeds.
- **Getting the working capital signs the wrong way round.** An increase in inventory reduces cash.
- **Putting dividends paid in operating activities.** They belong in financing.
- **Using the dividend proposed rather than the dividend paid.**
- **Including only the nominal value of a share issue.** The premium is cash too.
- **Forgetting the proceeds of disposals** under investing activities.
- **Failing to reconcile to the closing cash balance** in the statement of financial position.
- **Concluding that the company is fine because profit is high**, which ignores the entire purpose of the statement.
- **Describing the statement instead of interpreting it.** The marks are in explaining why cash moved.

</Mistakes>
