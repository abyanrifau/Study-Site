## What budgeting is for

- **Planning.** It forces management to think about the future in quantified terms rather than in intentions.
- **Forecasting.** It predicts the resources needed and, crucially, when cash will be short.
- **Coordination.** It makes departments consistent with each other: the production budget must match what sales expects to sell.
- **Communication.** It tells each manager what is expected of them.
- **Motivation.** A target that is challenging and achievable raises effort. One that is impossible destroys it.
- **Control.** Comparing actual with budget shows where to investigate, which leads into variance analysis in topic 2.5.
- **Authorisation.** An approved budget is permission to spend up to that limit.

## The order of preparation

The sequence matters because each budget is an input to the next. Start with the **principal budget factor**, the thing that limits activity. That is normally sales demand, but if a material is scarce or capacity is full, that becomes the starting point instead.

1. **Sales budget** — units and revenue.
2. **Production budget** — units, adjusting sales for the change in finished goods inventory.
3. **Materials usage budget** — material needed for that production.
4. **Purchases budget** — usage adjusted for the change in material inventory.
5. **Labour and overhead budgets.**
6. **Capital budget** — non-current asset purchases.
7. **Trade receivables and trade payables budgets.**
8. **Cash budget** — drawing receipts and payments from all of the above.
9. **Master budget** — budgeted statement of comprehensive income and statement of financial position.

**The two adjustments that carry the marks** are steps 2 and 4, and they use the same logic: what you need to have, plus what you want to be left holding, less what you already hold.

<Worked title="Worked example: production and purchases budgets">

A company expects to sell **24,000** units. Opening finished goods inventory is **2,000** units and it wants **3,500** units at the end.

`Production = 24,000 + 3,500 − 2,000 = 25,500 units`

Each unit uses **4 kg** of material. Opening material inventory is **9,000 kg** and closing is to be **12,000 kg**.

`Material needed for production = 25,500 × 4 = 102,000 kg`

`Purchases = 102,000 + 12,000 − 9,000 = 105,000 kg`

At **$3.50** per kg, `purchases cost = 105,000 × 3.50 = $367,500`.

**Notice the direction.** Building inventory means producing and buying **more** than you sell and use. Running inventory down means producing and buying **less**. Getting this backwards is the most common error in the whole topic.

</Worked>

## The cash budget

The cash budget records money when it **moves**. Three consequences follow, and all three are examined:

- **Credit sales appear in the month the cash is received**, not the month of the sale. If customers pay in the month after sale, January's sales are February's receipts.
- **Non-cash items never appear.** Depreciation is not in a cash budget. Neither is a provision, nor a bad debt written off.
- **Capital items appear in full** in the month of payment, even though they are charged to profit over years.

<Worked title="Worked example: three-month cash budget">

Sales: January **$80,000**, February **$95,000**, March **$110,000**. **30%** of sales are for cash; the remaining **70%** is received in the month following sale. December sales were **$70,000**.

Purchases are **60%** of the following month's sales, paid in the month after purchase. December purchases were **$48,000**.

Wages **$14,000** a month, paid in the month. Other overheads **$9,000** a month, of which **$2,000** is depreciation. New equipment costing **$40,000** is paid for in February. Opening cash at 1 January is **$12,000**.

**Receipts.**

`January: cash 80,000 × 30% = 24,000; credit from December 70,000 × 70% = 49,000 → 73,000`

`February: 95,000 × 30% = 28,500; from January 80,000 × 70% = 56,000 → 84,500`

`March: 110,000 × 30% = 33,000; from February 95,000 × 70% = 66,500 → 99,500`

**Payments to suppliers** — paid one month after purchase, so January pays for December's $48,000; February pays for January's purchases, which are 60% of February sales = 95,000 × 60% = $57,000; March pays for February's purchases = 110,000 × 60% = $66,000.

**Overheads paid in cash** = 9,000 − 2,000 depreciation = **$7,000** a month.

| | Jan $ | Feb $ | Mar $ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening balance | 12,000 | 15,000 | (18,500) |
| Receipts | 73,000 | 84,500 | 99,500 |
| **Total available** | 85,000 | 99,500 | 81,000 |
| Payments to suppliers | (48,000) | (57,000) | (66,000) |
| Wages | (14,000) | (14,000) | (14,000) |
| Overheads paid | (7,000) | (7,000) | (7,000) |
| Equipment | – | (40,000) | – |
| **Total payments** | (69,000) | (118,000) | (87,000) |
| **Closing balance** | **15,000** | **(18,500)** | **(24,500)** |

**What the budget is telling management.** Sales are rising every month and the cash position is getting worse every month. Two causes: the equipment was bought outright in a single month out of trading cash, and growth means payments to suppliers rise a month ahead of the receipts from the sales they support. **Depreciation was correctly excluded.**

**What to do about it:** finance the equipment over its life with a loan or lease rather than paying cash, arrange an overdraft facility before March, tighten the credit terms or offer a settlement discount, and negotiate longer credit from suppliers. Notice that none of these remedies involves selling more.

</Worked>

## Flexible budgets

A fixed budget set for 10,000 units tells you almost nothing about performance if 12,000 units were actually made, because variable costs **should** have been higher. Comparing actual costs at 12,000 units against a budget built for 10,000 is not a fair comparison, and it produces variances that are merely arithmetic.

A **flexible budget** restates the budget at the actual activity level: variable costs are flexed in proportion to activity, fixed costs are not flexed at all, and semi-variable costs are split into their fixed and variable parts before flexing only the variable part.

<Worked title="Worked example: flexing a budget">

Budget at **10,000** units: materials **$60,000**, labour **$40,000**, rent **$25,000**, maintenance **$18,000** of which **$8,000** is fixed. Actual at **12,000** units: materials **$74,400**, labour **$49,000**, rent **$25,000**, maintenance **$20,500**.

**Flexing.** Materials and labour are variable, so per unit they are $6.00 and $4.00. Rent is fixed. Maintenance is semi-variable: $8,000 fixed plus $10,000 variable over 10,000 units, so $1.00 per unit.

| | Flexed budget at 12,000 $ | Actual $ | Variance $ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Materials (12,000 × 6.00) | 72,000 | 74,400 | 2,400 adverse |
| Labour (12,000 × 4.00) | 48,000 | 49,000 | 1,000 adverse |
| Rent (fixed) | 25,000 | 25,000 | – |
| Maintenance (8,000 + 12,000 × 1.00) | 20,000 | 20,500 | 500 adverse |
| **Total** | **165,000** | **168,900** | **3,900 adverse** |

**Compare that with the naive comparison** against the original $143,000 budget, which would have shown a meaningless $25,900 adverse variance, almost all of it caused simply by making more units. The flexed comparison isolates the **$3,900** that is genuinely about cost control, and that is the whole purpose of flexing.

</Worked>

## Evaluating budgeting

**Benefits:** it quantifies the plan, coordinates departments, identifies cash shortages early enough to arrange finance, provides a basis for control and delegation, and can motivate through clear targets.

**Problems:**

- **It rests on forecasts.** A budget built on a wrong sales forecast is wrong throughout, because everything else derives from it.
- **Time and cost.** Preparation absorbs a great deal of management time.
- **Budgetary slack.** Managers who will be judged against a budget have an incentive to make it easy, which is an argument against pure participation.
- **Dysfunctional behaviour.** Spending the remainder of a budget in the final month to protect next year's allocation; cutting maintenance or training to meet a short-term cost target.
- **Rigidity.** A budget set nine months earlier may be irrelevant if conditions change, which is the argument for rolling budgets.
- **Demotivation.** An imposed or unattainable target reduces effort rather than raising it.
- **Conflict between departments** competing for the same funds.

**The balanced judgement:** participation improves both accuracy and commitment, because the managers closest to the work know the most, and it opens the door to slack. The usual answer is participation combined with independent review by the budget committee.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Very commonly a Section A 55-mark question. Typical parts: production and purchases budgets (8–10 marks), a three-month cash budget (14–18 marks), a flexible budget (8–10 marks), then an evaluation of 10–12 marks on the usefulness of budgeting or on how the company should deal with the forecast deficit.

**Method for a cash budget:**

1. **Draw the grid first**, one column per month, with rows for opening balance, each receipt, each payment, total payments and closing balance.
2. **Do the receipts separately on a working**, month by month, splitting cash and credit sales and lagging the credit portion.
3. **Cross out the depreciation** in the question as soon as you see it, so you cannot include it by accident.
4. **Carry the closing balance forward** as the next month's opening balance. An error here cascades, so check each transfer.
5. **Include capital payments in full** in the month of payment.
6. **If the balance goes negative, say so explicitly** and quote the worst month and amount.

**Structuring the evaluation:**

1. **State the problem with figures:** the deficit, the month it arises and its size.
2. **Explain the cause.** Distinguish a timing problem from a profitability problem, because the remedies are completely different.
3. **Give two or three specific remedies** and chain each to its effect on the cash budget.
4. **Note the cost of each remedy:** interest on an overdraft, the cost of a settlement discount, the damage to supplier relations from stretching payment.
5. **Recommend**, and state the condition it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Growth consumes cash**, so a rising sales budget can produce a worsening cash budget.
- **A budget is only as good as the sales forecast** it starts from.
- **Long-term assets should not be bought out of trading cash.**
- **Participation improves accuracy and invites slack.**
- **Flexing makes control fair**, and without it variances measure volume rather than efficiency.
- **A cash problem is not a profit problem**, and saying which one the company has is the sharpest single point available.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Including depreciation in the cash budget.**
- **Reversing the inventory adjustment** in the production or purchases budget.
- **Recording credit sales in the month of sale** instead of the month of receipt.
- **Forgetting the opening receivables and payables**, which produce the first month's receipts and payments.
- **Spreading a capital payment over several months** instead of showing it in full when paid.
- **Flexing fixed costs**, or failing to split a semi-variable cost before flexing.
- **Failing to carry the closing balance forward** as the next opening balance.
- **Comparing actual results with an unflexed budget** and calling the difference a cost-control variance.
- **Concluding that a cash deficit means the company is unprofitable.**
- **Listing the benefits of budgeting with no reference to the figures** in the question.

</Mistakes>
