## The four cost behaviours

<Figure caption="Fixed, variable, semi-variable and semi-fixed costs against output">
<CostBehaviourDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Fixed.** Unchanged as output changes: rent, insurance, salaried staff, depreciation on a straight-line basis. **Fixed cost per unit falls** as output rises, which is where economies of scale come from.
- **Variable.** Changes in proportion to output: raw materials, piece-rate labour, packaging. **Variable cost per unit stays the same.**
- **Semi-variable.** Both elements together: a machine hire charge plus a running cost per hour, or a standing charge plus usage. **It must be split before it can be used**, which is what the high-low method is for.
- **Semi-fixed (stepped).** Fixed over a range and then jumping: one supervisor per 20 workers, or renting a second unit when capacity is reached. Each step invalidates the previous break-even calculation, because fixed costs have changed.

<Worked title="Worked example: splitting a semi-variable cost">

Maintenance cost was **$34,000** at an output of **9,000** units and **$46,000** at **15,000** units.

`Variable cost per unit = (46,000 − 34,000) ÷ (15,000 − 9,000) = 12,000 ÷ 6,000 = $2.00`

`Variable cost at 9,000 units = 9,000 × 2.00 = $18,000`

`Fixed element = 34,000 − 18,000 = $16,000`

**Check at the high level:** 16,000 + (15,000 × 2.00) = 16,000 + 30,000 = $46,000. Correct.

So maintenance is **$16,000 fixed plus $2.00 per unit**. Only the $2.00 goes into contribution; the $16,000 joins fixed costs.

</Worked>

## The core calculations

<Formula name="Break-even and margin of safety" note="Break-even in UNITS uses contribution per unit. Break-even in REVENUE uses the contribution to sales ratio. Mixing the two is the commonest single error.">
Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit

Break-even (units) = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Contribution to sales ratio = Contribution per unit ÷ Selling price

Break-even (revenue) = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution to sales ratio

Margin of safety (units) = Actual output − Break-even output

Profit = (Contribution per unit × Units sold) − Fixed costs
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: the full set">

Selling price **$45** per unit. Variable costs: materials $16, labour $9, variable overhead $5. Fixed costs **$180,000** a year. Current output **14,000** units.

**Step 1 — contribution.**

`Variable cost per unit = 16 + 9 + 5 = $30`

`Contribution per unit = 45 − 30 = $15`

`Contribution to sales ratio = 15 ÷ 45 = 0.333, or 33.3%`

**Step 2 — break-even.**

`Break-even (units) = 180,000 ÷ 15 = 12,000 units`

`Break-even (revenue) = 180,000 ÷ 0.333 = $540,000`

**Check:** 12,000 units × $45 = $540,000. The two methods agree.

**Step 3 — margin of safety.**

`Margin of safety = 14,000 − 12,000 = 2,000 units`

`As a percentage = (2,000 ÷ 14,000) × 100 = 14.3%`

**Step 4 — profit.**

`Profit = (15 × 14,000) − 180,000 = 210,000 − 180,000 = $30,000`

**Step 5 — output needed for a target profit of $75,000.**

`Output = (180,000 + 75,000) ÷ 15 = 255,000 ÷ 15 = 17,000 units`

**Interpretation, which is where the marks are.** The margin of safety is only **14.3%**, so a fall in sales of about one seventh wipes out the profit entirely. For a business whose sales are seasonal or dependent on a few customers, that is uncomfortably thin. Reaching the $75,000 target needs output to rise by **21%**, which raises an immediate question the source usually answers: is there spare capacity, and can the market absorb 3,000 more units without a price cut?

**And note the leverage.** Each extra unit adds $15 of profit once fixed costs are covered, so a 10% rise in volume from 14,000 to 15,400 adds $21,000 of profit, a **70% rise in profit** from a 10% rise in sales. High fixed costs magnify movements in both directions, and that is the point the angle of incidence expresses on the chart.

</Worked>

## The chart

<Figure caption="The break-even chart with the margin of safety, areas of profit and loss, and the angle of incidence">
<BreakEvenFullDiagram />
</Figure>

**How to draw and label it.** Output in units on the horizontal axis; revenue and costs in dollars on the vertical.

1. **Fixed costs:** a horizontal line at the fixed cost level.
2. **Total costs:** a straight line starting where the fixed cost line meets the vertical axis and sloping up at the variable cost per unit.
3. **Sales revenue:** a straight line from the origin, steeper than total costs. It starts at the origin because no sales means no revenue.
4. **Break-even point:** where sales revenue crosses total costs. Drop a line to read the output, across to read the revenue.
5. **Area of loss:** the wedge between the two lines to the **left** of the crossing point.
6. **Area of profit:** the widening wedge to the **right**.
7. **Margin of safety:** the horizontal distance between break-even output and actual output.
8. **Angle of incidence:** the angle between the revenue and total cost lines at the crossing point.

**Reading the angle of incidence.** A **wide** angle means revenue pulls away from costs quickly, so profit grows fast once break-even is passed. That happens when contribution per unit is high relative to the selling price. A **narrow** angle means profit accumulates slowly, so the business has to sell a lot beyond break-even before the profit is worth having. It is the visual equivalent of the contribution to sales ratio, and saying so is a good way to earn a mark for understanding rather than description.

## Uses and limitations

**Uses.** Setting a minimum sales target, judging how much risk is in the cost structure, assessing the effect of a price change or a cost increase, deciding whether to accept an order, supporting a loan application, and choosing between a labour-intensive and a capital-intensive method.

**Limitations, which the evaluation must use:**

- **Everything is assumed linear.** In reality bulk discounts reduce variable cost per unit at higher volumes, and overtime raises it.
- **Fixed costs are assumed constant**, yet they step up. A break-even point beyond the next step is simply wrong.
- **One selling price is assumed**, so discounting for volume is not captured.
- **It assumes everything produced is sold**, so it ignores inventory.
- **A single product is assumed**, or a constant sales mix, which is unrealistic for most businesses.
- **Costs and the price are estimates**, so the answer is only as good as the inputs.
- **It is static**, a picture of one set of conditions.
- **Non-financial factors are absent:** quality, reputation, staff capacity and customer relationships.
- **It is only valid over the relevant range** of output for which the cost estimates were prepared.

**The balanced judgement:** break-even analysis is a useful first check and a poor basis for a final decision on its own. It is at its most reliable for a single-product business over a narrow range of output and over a short period.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Commonly a Section B 30-mark question and frequently a part of a Section A question. Typical parts: split a semi-variable cost (4 marks), calculate contribution, break-even and margin of safety (8–10 marks), draw or label a chart (6–8 marks), recalculate after a change in price or costs (4–6 marks), then evaluate (8–12 marks).

**Method:**

1. **Build the variable cost per unit first**, adding every variable element including the variable part of any semi-variable cost.
2. **Collect all the fixed costs**, including the fixed part of any semi-variable cost.
3. **Calculate contribution per unit before anything else.** Every other formula depends on it.
4. **Round break-even output UP** to the next whole unit, because a part unit does not break even.
5. **State the units:** units, dollars or a percentage.
6. **If the question changes the price or a cost, recalculate contribution first** and then rework everything. Adjusting the old break-even figure directly does not work.
7. **On a chart, label all eight features.** Marks are given for the labels, not for artistic quality.

**Structuring the evaluation:**

1. **Quote the margin of safety** and say whether it is comfortable, with a reason drawn from the source such as seasonality or dependence on one customer.
2. **Explain the leverage:** with high fixed costs a small change in volume produces a large change in profit, in both directions.
3. **Test the proposal.** Recalculate break-even under the change and compare.
4. **Check feasibility.** Is there capacity for the required output, and will the market absorb it?
5. **Bring in two or three limitations that actually matter here**, not a memorised list.
6. **Recommend**, and state the condition it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The margin of safety measures risk**, and its adequacy depends on how volatile demand is.
- **High fixed costs magnify both profit and loss.**
- **Fixed costs step up**, so a break-even point beyond the next step is invalid.
- **The angle of incidence expresses the contribution to sales ratio visually.**
- **The analysis assumes all output is sold**, which a business building inventory is not doing.
- **A single-product assumption** weakens it badly for a business with a range.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing fixed costs by the selling price** instead of by contribution per unit.
- **Using total contribution rather than contribution per unit** in the break-even formula.
- **Forgetting to split a semi-variable cost**, so the fixed part is treated as variable or the reverse.
- **Using the contribution to sales ratio to find break-even in units**, or contribution per unit to find break-even revenue.
- **Rounding break-even output down.**
- **Adjusting an existing break-even figure after a price change** instead of recalculating contribution.
- **Starting the sales revenue line above the origin** on the chart.
- **Drawing the total cost line from the origin** instead of from the fixed cost level.
- **Omitting the angle of incidence or the areas of profit and loss** when the question asks for a full chart.
- **Quoting a margin of safety with no judgement** about whether it is adequate.

</Mistakes>
