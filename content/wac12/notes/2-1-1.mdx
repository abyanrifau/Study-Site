## The three IAS 1 statements

<Chunk title="Statement of comprehensive income" open>

Standard order, for the year ended:

| | $ |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | X |
| Cost of sales | (X) |
| **Gross profit** | X |
| Distribution costs | (X) |
| Administrative expenses | (X) |
| **Operating profit** | X |
| Finance costs | (X) |
| **Profit before tax** | X |
| Taxation | (X) |
| **Profit for the year** | X |
| Other comprehensive income: gain on revaluation | X |
| **Total comprehensive income** | X |

**Points that cost marks.** Debenture interest is a **finance cost**, not an expense of administration. Directors' fees are an administrative expense. **Dividends are not an expense** and never appear in this statement; they belong in the statement of changes in equity. A revaluation gain is **other comprehensive income**, below profit for the year.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Statement of changes in equity">

This statement reconciles opening to closing equity, one column per component:

| | Share capital | Share premium | Revaluation reserve | General reserve | Retained earnings | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Balance at start | X | X | X | X | X | X |
| Total comprehensive income | | | X | | X | X |
| Share issue | X | X | | | | X |
| Bonus issue | X | (X) | | | | – |
| Transfer to general reserve | | | | X | (X) | – |
| Dividends paid | | | | | (X) | (X) |
| **Balance at end** | X | X | X | X | X | X |

**The two rows that must net to nil** are the bonus issue and the reserve transfer, because both move value between columns without changing total equity. If your bonus issue row has a total, you have made an error.

</Chunk>

| | $ |

**Preference shares** that are redeemable are shown as a liability in substance; irredeemable preference shares sit within equity. Check how the question describes them and say which treatment you have used.

## Reserves

<Chunk title="The six named reserves, and which are distributable" open>

| Reserve | Type | How created | How used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Retained earnings** | Revenue | Accumulated profits not yet distributed | Dividends, transfers to other reserves, funding a bonus issue |
| **General reserve** | Revenue | A transfer out of retained earnings | Can be transferred back and distributed |
| **Foreign exchange reserve** | Capital | Gains and losses on translating overseas operations | Absorbs future translation differences |
| **Share premium** | Capital | Issuing shares above nominal value | Bonus issue, writing off share issue expenses |
| **Revaluation reserve** | Capital | Revaluing a non-current asset upwards | Bonus issue; realised on disposal |
| **Capital redemption reserve** | Capital | Redeeming shares out of profits, to keep capital intact | Bonus issue only |

**The examinable principle:** capital reserves are **not distributable as cash dividends**, because they do not represent realised trading profit. They can be used for a bonus issue, which distributes shares rather than cash and leaves total equity unchanged. That single sentence answers a large family of questions.

</Chunk>

## Share capital, bonus issues and rights issues

**Authorised** is the ceiling, **issued** is what has actually been sold, and **called-up** is the part shareholders have been asked to pay. Only issued and called-up capital appears in the statement of financial position; authorised capital is a disclosure note.

<Formula name="Share issue journals" note="Always split the proceeds between nominal value and premium. Failing to do so is the single most common error in this topic.">
Issue at a premium:
Dr Bank (total cash received)
Cr Ordinary share capital (nominal value × number of shares)
Cr Share premium (premium × number of shares)

Bonus issue:
Dr Share premium / revaluation reserve / retained earnings
Cr Ordinary share capital

Rights issue: treated exactly like an ordinary issue at a premium.
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: rights issue then bonus issue">

A company has **400,000** ordinary shares of **$0.50** each, share premium of **$90,000** and retained earnings of **$260,000**.

**Step 1 — a 1 for 4 rights issue at $1.20 per share.**

Shares issued = 400,000 ÷ 4 = **100,000**

`Cash received = 100,000 × $1.20 = $120,000`

`Share capital = 100,000 × $0.50 = $50,000`

`Share premium = 100,000 × ($1.20 − $0.50) = $70,000`

Journal: Dr Bank $120,000; Cr Share capital $50,000; Cr Share premium $70,000.

Share premium is now 90,000 + 70,000 = **$160,000**. Shares in issue are **500,000**.

**Step 2 — a 1 for 5 bonus issue, using share premium first.**

Shares issued = 500,000 ÷ 5 = **100,000**, nominal value 100,000 × $0.50 = **$50,000**

Journal: Dr Share premium $50,000; Cr Share capital $50,000.

**No cash changes hands.**

**Closing position:**

| | $ |
| --- | --- |
| Share capital (600,000 × $0.50) | 300,000 |
| Share premium (160,000 − 50,000) | 110,000 |
| Retained earnings | 260,000 |
| **Total equity** | **670,000** |

**Check:** total equity rose by exactly $120,000, the cash from the rights issue. The bonus issue changed the mix and not the total.

</Worked>

**Why a company makes a bonus issue:** to bring the share capital into line with the assets actually financing the business, to use up a reserve that cannot be paid as a dividend, and to lower the share price into a more marketable range. **The drawback:** it uses up reserves that might otherwise have funded dividends, and it raises no money.

**Why a rights issue rather than a public offer:** it is cheaper, it needs no prospectus, and it protects existing shareholders from dilution. **The drawback:** shareholders who cannot afford to take up their rights are diluted, and the discount reduces the cash raised per share.

## Shares and debentures compared

| | Ordinary shares | Preference shares | Debentures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Holder is | an owner | an owner | a lender |
| Return | dividend, variable | fixed dividend, paid first | fixed interest, contractual |
| Paid if there is no profit | no | no | **yes** |
| Voting rights | yes | usually no | no |
| Treatment of the return | appropriation of profit | appropriation of profit | **expense: finance cost** |
| On liquidation | paid last | before ordinary shares | before all shareholders |
| Tax | dividends are not deductible | not deductible | interest **is** deductible |

**The two distinctions that earn marks:** debenture interest must be paid whether or not the company makes a profit, which is where the risk of gearing comes from; and interest is deductible against tax while dividends are not, which makes debt cheaper than its headline rate suggests.

## Capital gearing

<Formula name="Capital gearing" note="Examiners accept more than one version. State your formula, then interpret the number. An answer with no interpretation scores badly however the arithmetic is done.">
Capital gearing = (Fixed-return capital ÷ Total long-term capital) × 100

Fixed-return capital = debentures + preference share capital
Total long-term capital = fixed-return capital + ordinary share capital + reserves
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example">

Ordinary share capital $300,000; reserves $370,000; 8% debentures $250,000; 6% preference shares $80,000.

`Fixed-return capital = 250,000 + 80,000 = $330,000`

`Total long-term capital = 330,000 + 300,000 + 370,000 = $1,000,000`

`Capital gearing = (330,000 ÷ 1,000,000) × 100 = 33%`

**Interpretation:** a third of long-term capital carries an obligatory return. Annual fixed charges are (250,000 × 8%) + (80,000 × 6%) = 20,000 + 4,800 = **$24,800**, which must be met before any ordinary dividend.

</Worked>

**Implications of high gearing.** Fixed charges must be paid in bad years, so profit available to ordinary shareholders is volatile and the risk of default rises. Lenders see less security and charge more. **Against that:** debt is cheaper than equity, interest is tax deductible, control is not diluted, and in good years the surplus above the interest all belongs to the ordinary shareholders. Low gearing is safer and may mean the company is failing to use cheap finance.

## Provisions

A provision is recognised where there is a **present obligation**, it is **probable** that payment will be required, and the amount can be **reliably estimated**. Creating one debits an expense and credits a liability, so it reduces profit in the year the obligation arises rather than the year the cash is paid. That is the matching concept at work.

**Features:** the timing or amount is uncertain, which is what distinguishes a provision from an accrual, where both are known. A provision is a liability; an allowance for doubtful debts, by contrast, is offset against receivables.

## Auditors, directors and disclosure

**The auditor** is independent of the company and gives an opinion on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view and comply with the standards. The auditor **does not** prepare the statements, guarantee the figures or certify that no fraud exists.

**The Auditors' Report** states the scope of the audit and the opinion. An **unqualified** opinion means no material reservations. A **qualified** opinion signals a problem and is taken very seriously by lenders and investors.

**The Directors' Report** is prepared by the directors and covers the review of the business, principal risks, dividends recommended, directors' names and interests, and future developments. It is narrative and gives the context the figures lack.

**The role in corporate governance** is to provide the external check that makes directors accountable to shareholders, which addresses the separation of ownership from control. Its limits are real: the auditor is paid by the company, works on a sample, and reports after the year end.

**Continuing and discontinued activities** must be shown separately, because a user forecasting future performance needs to know which profits will recur. **Exceptional items** are of unusual size or incidence, such as a large restructuring cost or a one-off disposal profit. Disclosing them separately lets users see underlying performance. If they were buried in operating expenses, the trend would be misleading.

## Purchase and merger of limited companies

<Chunk title="The accounts and the sequence">

**In the books of the company being taken over:**

- **Revaluation account.** Assets and liabilities are restated to the values agreed. Gains are credited, losses debited, and the balance is transferred to the shareholders.
- **Realisation account.** Assets are transferred in at book value, the purchase consideration is credited, and the balance is the profit or loss on realisation, taken to the shareholders.
- **Sundry shareholders' account.** Brings together share capital, reserves and the realisation result, and is settled by the shares or cash received from the purchaser.

**In the books of the purchasing company:**

- **Acquisition account.** Debited with the purchase price, credited with the fair value of each asset taken over and debited with each liability assumed. **The balance is goodwill.**

</Chunk>

<Formula name="Goodwill" note="Use the AGREED or revalued figures, never book value. Using book value is the classic error in this topic.">
Goodwill = Purchase price − Fair value of net assets acquired

where Net assets = Assets taken over − Liabilities assumed
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: purchase and goodwill">

Company B is bought by Company A. Agreed values of B's assets and liabilities:

| | $ |
| --- | --- |
| Property (revalued) | 420,000 |
| Equipment | 95,000 |
| Inventory | 60,000 |
| Trade receivables | 48,000 |
| Trade payables | (37,000) |
| **Net assets at fair value** | **586,000** |

Company A pays with **500,000** shares of $1 issued at **$1.30**, plus **$70,000** cash.

`Purchase price = (500,000 × $1.30) + $70,000 = 650,000 + 70,000 = $720,000`

`Goodwill = 720,000 − 586,000 = $134,000`

**Journal in A's books:**

Dr each asset at fair value, Cr each liability assumed, Dr Goodwill $134,000, Cr Share capital $500,000, Cr Share premium $150,000, Cr Bank $70,000.

**Note the split of the share consideration:** $500,000 to share capital at nominal value and $150,000 to share premium. Putting the whole $650,000 into share capital is the most frequently penalised mistake in this topic.

**If the purchase price had been $560,000**, the result would be negative goodwill of $26,000, meaning A bought the net assets for less than their fair value, usually because the business is loss-making or the sale was forced.

</Worked>

**Treatment of goodwill.** It is a non-current intangible asset. It is not amortised on a straight-line basis under current standards; instead it is reviewed for impairment and written down if its value has fallen. **Purchased goodwill** may be recognised because a price was paid for it; **internally generated goodwill** may not, because no reliable cost exists.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** It is the most likely Section A 55-mark question. Typical parts: prepare the statement of comprehensive income (12–15 marks), prepare the statement of changes in equity (8–10), prepare the statement of financial position (12–15), calculate gearing or goodwill (4–6), then an evaluation of 8–12 marks, for example on whether to finance expansion by debentures or a rights issue.

**Method that protects marks:**

1. **Read every note under the trial balance first** and mark which statement each affects. Most notes affect two.
2. **Work through the adjustments before writing anything out:** depreciation, accruals, prepayments, the tax charge, any revaluation.
3. **Use the standard layout and the IAS headings.** Marks are given for correct presentation and subtotals.
4. **Show your workings separately and label them W1, W2.** A wrong figure with a visible method still earns method marks.
5. **Cross-check:** total assets equals total equity and liabilities; the closing retained earnings in the changes-in-equity statement matches the figure in the position statement; bonus issue and reserve transfer rows total nil.
6. **For the written part, use the figures you have just calculated.** Quoting your own gearing percentage is what separates a good answer from a generic one.

**Getting evaluation marks on a financing question:**

- Compare the **interest cost** with the **return expected** on the investment.
- Quote the **gearing before and after** each option.
- Note that debentures **must** be paid in bad years while dividends need not.
- Note that a rights issue avoids fixed charges and **dilutes control** if not all shareholders take it up.
- Recommend, and state the condition: the answer depends on the stability of profits and on the existing level of gearing.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Putting dividends in the statement of comprehensive income.** They are an appropriation and belong in the statement of changes in equity.
- **Treating debenture interest as an administrative expense.** It is a finance cost, shown after operating profit.
- **Crediting the whole proceeds of a share issue to share capital.** Split it between nominal value and share premium.
- **Giving a bonus issue a total in the changes-in-equity statement.** It must net to nil.
- **Paying a dividend out of a capital reserve.** Capital reserves are not distributable in cash.
- **Calculating goodwill from book values** rather than the agreed or revalued figures.
- **Forgetting to deduct liabilities assumed** when calculating net assets acquired.
- **Quoting a gearing percentage without interpreting it.** The number alone earns almost nothing.
- **Omitting the revaluation gain from other comprehensive income**, or including it in profit for the year.
- **Showing authorised share capital on the face of the statement of financial position.** It is a disclosure note.

</Mistakes>
