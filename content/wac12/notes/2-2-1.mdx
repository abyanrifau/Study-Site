## Getting the inputs right

Three rules settle most of the arithmetic:

1. **Number of shares, not the value of share capital.** 500,000 shares of $0.25 is **500,000** shares, not 125,000. Find the number by dividing the share capital figure by the nominal value per share.
2. **Deduct the preference dividend from profit after tax first.** Preference shareholders are paid before ordinary shareholders, so the earnings *available to ordinary shareholders* is what the per-share ratios use.
3. **Market price is given, never calculated.** If the question does not give it, you cannot compute P/E or dividend yield, and saying so is the correct answer.

<Worked title="Worked example: all six ratios from one set of data">

| | $ |
| --- | --- |
| Operating profit | 340,000 |
| Finance costs | (40,000) |
| Profit before tax | 300,000 |
| Taxation | (60,000) |
| **Profit after tax** | **240,000** |
| Ordinary share capital: 800,000 shares of $0.50 | 400,000 |
| 8% preference shares of $1 | 150,000 |
| Reserves | 510,000 |
| 10% debentures | 400,000 |
| Ordinary dividend paid for the year | 96,000 |

Market price per ordinary share: **$2.40**.

**Step 1 — earnings available to ordinary shareholders.**

`Preference dividend = 150,000 × 8% = $12,000`

`Earnings available = 240,000 − 12,000 = $228,000`

**Step 2 — the six ratios.**

`EPS = 228,000 ÷ 800,000 = $0.285, or 28.5 cents`

`P/E = 2.40 ÷ 0.285 = 8.4 times`

`Dividend per share = 96,000 ÷ 800,000 = $0.12, or 12 cents`

`Dividend yield = (0.12 ÷ 2.40) × 100 = 5.0%`

`Dividend cover = 228,000 ÷ 96,000 = 2.4 times`

`Capital employed = (400,000 + 150,000 + 510,000) + 400,000 = $1,460,000`

`ROCE = (340,000 ÷ 1,460,000) × 100 = 23.3%`

**Two checks worth making.** Dividend cover can also be found as EPS ÷ DPS = 0.285 ÷ 0.12 = 2.4 times, which confirms both figures. And the retention ratio is 1 − (1 ÷ 2.4) = 58%, so the company is keeping well over half its earnings for reinvestment.

</Worked>

## What each ratio actually tells you

<Chunk title="The six ratios interpreted" open>

**Earnings per share.** The profit earned for each ordinary share. It is the cleanest measure of performance per unit of ownership, and it is the input to P/E. It rises if profit rises **or** if the share count falls, so a change in EPS always needs both parts checked.

**Price earnings ratio.** How many years of current earnings the market is paying for one share. A **high P/E** means the market expects earnings to grow, or that the share is overpriced. A **low P/E** means low expectations, or an undervalued share. The ratio cannot tell you which, so the honest interpretation is always conditional.

**Dividend per share.** The cash income per share. Useful, and it says nothing about affordability on its own.

**Dividend yield.** The income return on the current market price. It is what an investor seeking income compares with the return on a bank deposit or a bond. A yield that is very high relative to the sector often means the market expects the dividend to be cut, which is why an unusually high yield is a warning rather than an opportunity.

**Dividend cover.** How many times the dividend could have been paid out of available earnings. **Above 2 times** is generally comfortable. **Around 1 time** means the whole of the year's earnings is being paid out, leaving nothing for reinvestment. **Below 1 time** means the dividend is being paid partly out of past retained earnings, which cannot continue indefinitely.

**Return on capital employed.** The return the business earns on all the long-term finance used to earn it. It is the primary measure of how well management uses the resources it has been given, and the key comparison is with **the cost of that capital**. A ROCE of 23.3% against debentures at 10% means borrowing to invest has added value.

</Chunk>

## The tension between the two investor types

The examinable insight is that **dividend cover and dividend yield pull in opposite directions**.

- An **income investor** wants a high yield: cash now.
- A **growth investor** wants high cover and a high retention ratio: profits reinvested so that earnings and the share price grow.

A company cannot maximise both from the same profit. High cover means a low payout and therefore a lower yield; a high yield means a low cover and therefore less reinvestment. So the answer to "is this a good investment?" depends entirely on what the investor wants, and saying so with reference to both ratios is the strongest evaluative move available in this topic.

## Limitations

- **Historical.** Every ratio uses figures from a period that has ended, while the investment decision concerns the future.
- **One year alone means nothing.** A trend or a competitor comparison is essential.
- **Market price is volatile.** P/E and yield change daily without anything happening inside the company.
- **Accounting policies differ**, particularly on depreciation and asset valuation, so ROCE comparisons between companies need care.
- **Capital employed is a year-end figure** and may not reflect the capital used through the year.
- **Nothing non-financial appears**, yet management quality, order books and reputation drive future earnings.
- **EPS is distorted by share issues.** A bonus issue increases the share count with no new resources, so EPS falls without any deterioration in performance. That point is worth remembering because it links directly to topic 2.1.
- **A dividend can be maintained to reassure the market** even when it is not affordable, which is exactly what a falling cover reveals.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Almost always as part of a larger question, frequently as a Section B 30-mark question: calculate four to six ratios for two years or two companies (10–14 marks), then a written part of 12–16 marks advising a named investor.

**Method:**

1. **Set out a small table** with the ratio, last year and this year, or company A and company B. It is faster to write and easier to mark.
2. **Show the numerator and denominator** for every ratio, so method marks survive an arithmetic slip.
3. **Calculate the preference dividend first** and use the same earnings figure throughout.
4. **State the units.** EPS and DPS in dollars or cents, P/E and cover in times, yield and ROCE as percentages. Marks are lost for a bare number.

**Structuring the advice:**

1. **Identify what the investor wants:** income, growth, or safety of capital.
2. **Use the ratios that answer that question**, not all six indiscriminately.
3. **Explain the movement**, not just its direction. Why did EPS fall? More shares, or less profit?
4. **Compare with the alternative** the question offers, or with the return available elsewhere.
5. **Recommend**, then state the limitations and what further information you would want.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Cover and yield conflict**, so the recommendation depends on the investor's objective.
- **Compare ROCE with the cost of capital**, not with zero.
- **A very high yield can be a warning** that the market expects a cut.
- **A high P/E is either confidence or overvaluation**, and the ratio cannot distinguish them.
- **One year is not a trend**, and non-financial factors are absent.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing by the value of share capital instead of the number of shares.** With shares of $0.50, the number is double the dollar figure.
- **Forgetting to deduct the preference dividend** from profit after tax.
- **Using profit before tax in EPS.** It is profit after tax.
- **Using profit after tax in ROCE.** Use operating profit, because interest-bearing finance sits in the denominator.
- **Omitting non-current liabilities from capital employed.**
- **Inverting the P/E ratio.** It is price divided by earnings.
- **Confusing dividend yield with dividend cover.** Yield uses the market price; cover uses earnings.
- **Giving a number with no unit** and no interpretation.
- **Recommending an investment without asking what the investor wants.**
- **Treating a fall in EPS after a bonus issue as a deterioration in performance.**

</Mistakes>
