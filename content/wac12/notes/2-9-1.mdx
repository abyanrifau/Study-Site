## What ICT is used for

<Chunk title="Spreadsheets" open>

**Uses in accounting:** budgets and cash budgets, break-even models, variance calculations, investment appraisal with discount factors, ratio analysis across several years, depreciation schedules and payroll workings.

**Why they suit budgeting particularly well.** A cash budget is a chain of dependent calculations, and the closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next. In a spreadsheet, changing the sales forecast recalculates every subsequent month instantly. That makes **what-if analysis** practical: raise the sales forecast by 10%, delay the equipment purchase by a month, or assume customers pay a month later, and see the new worst-month balance immediately. Doing the same by hand would mean rewriting the whole budget each time, which is why sensitivity analysis is rarely done manually.

**The specific weakness of spreadsheets.** They have almost no built-in controls. A formula that has been copied to the wrong range, a row inserted outside a SUM range, or a hard-coded number typed over a formula produces a confident, wrong answer with nothing to flag it. Large spreadsheets are notoriously difficult to check, and version control is poor when several people hold copies.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Accounting software packages">

Typical modules: sales invoicing, the receivables list and statements, purchase ledger, payroll, inventory, tax returns, and the nominal ledger producing a trial balance and the financial statements.

**The central benefit is integration.** A single entry of a sales invoice updates the sales account, the customer's account, the receivables total, the inventory records and the tax records at once. That removes duplicate keying and the errors that come with it, and the ledgers cannot disagree with each other because there is only one set of data.

**Other benefits:** documents are produced automatically and look professional; a receivables list with ageing is available instantly, which supports credit control; payroll calculates deductions to current rates; and management reports can be produced at any time rather than only at a period end.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Electronic point of sale">

Each sale is scanned, and the system simultaneously records the revenue, reduces the inventory record and stores the sales data.

**Benefits:** continuous inventory records without manual counting, automatic reordering at a reorder level, immediate identification of slow-moving and fast-moving lines, accurate pricing with no keying error, faster service at the till, and sales analysis by product, time of day and branch that supports both buying decisions and staff rotas. It also makes stock loss visible, because the physical count can be compared with what the system says should be there.

**Limitations:** the equipment and software are expensive to install; the system depends on items being scanned correctly, so mis-scanning and theft still distort the records; a system failure can stop trading altogether; and a physical count is still necessary, because the records show what should be there rather than what is.

</Chunk>

## Advantages and disadvantages

The specification asks for four headings, so use them as the structure of any written answer.

<Chunk title="Audit trail" open>

**Advantages.** Every posting is logged with a date, a user and a reference, so a figure in the financial statements can be traced back to the original transaction. Access rights limit who can post or amend what, and a proper system records amendments rather than allowing them to be overwritten silently. That makes the auditor's work faster and cheaper, improves internal control, and deters fraud because actions are attributable.

**Disadvantages.** The trail is only as good as the system's controls: if users share a single login, attribution is worthless. Errors are propagated instantly and consistently, so one wrong setting produces thousands of consistently wrong entries. Spreadsheets in particular provide almost no trail at all, because a cell can be overwritten with no record of what was there before.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Financial aspects">

**Advantages.** Lower labour costs as routine posting and calculation disappear; faster invoicing, which improves cash flow; better credit control from an instant ageing analysis; fewer errors, and error correction is itself expensive; and better decisions from information that is available in time to act on.

**Disadvantages.** High initial cost of hardware, software and licences; training costs; continuing costs of support, upgrades, backup and security; productivity falls during implementation; and the risk that the money is not recovered if the system is poorly chosen or underused. For a very small business the savings may simply not justify the cost, which is the sharpest evaluative point available here.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Technical aspects">

**Advantages.** Speed and accuracy of calculation; large volumes handled without extra staff; integration between modules; automatic backup; remote and multi-user access; and scalability as the business grows.

**Disadvantages.** Dependence on the system, so a failure can halt the accounting function and, with EPOS, the trading itself. Data can be lost or corrupted, and backups fail more often than businesses expect. Systems face security threats from unauthorised access and malicious software. Packages must be compatible with each other and with the bank's and customers' systems. Software becomes obsolete and must be replaced, and data migration is a substantial risk in itself.

**And the principle that overrides all the technical advantages:** garbage in, garbage out. The system calculates faithfully whatever it is given, so an error in the input data produces an error in the output, delivered quickly and with an unjustified appearance of authority.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Human aspects">

**Advantages.** Routine, tedious work is removed, so staff can be redeployed to analysis and interpretation, which is more satisfying and more valuable. Information is easier for non-specialists to read. Skills gained raise employability.

**Disadvantages.** Resistance to change, particularly from long-serving staff who fear the new system or the loss of their role. Retraining costs time and money, and some staff will not adapt. Jobs may be lost, which damages morale among those who remain. Over-reliance on the output can erode the professional scepticism that spots a figure that cannot be right. And responsibility can become diffuse: "the system produced it" is not an acceptable explanation, but it is a common one.

</Chunk>

## Balanced judgement

**When ICT is clearly worth it:** high transaction volumes, several sites, inventory of many lines, a need for frequent management information, or a growing business where manual systems are already straining.

**When it may not be:** a very small business with few transactions, where the cost and training outweigh the saving; a business whose staff cannot be retrained; or an unstable operating environment, where an unreliable power or internet supply makes dependence on a system a serious operational risk. That last point matters for island and remote operations and is a legitimate consideration rather than an excuse.

**The honest overall position:** ICT changes the nature of accounting work rather than removing the need for judgement. It makes the routine faster and more accurate, and it makes the consequences of poor data, weak controls and misplaced confidence larger and quicker.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** It is the smallest of the nine topics and usually appears as a written part worth 6–12 marks inside a larger Section B question, often alongside budgeting. Occasionally it forms part of an evaluation about whether a company should invest in a new system.

**Structuring the answer:**

1. **Identify the specific use** in the source: spreadsheets for the cash budget, a package for invoicing and payroll, or EPOS for inventory. Answer about that, not about computers in general.
2. **Use the four headings** as your paragraphs: audit trail, financial, technical, human. It guarantees breadth and shows you know the specification.
3. **Give two advantages and two disadvantages under the relevant headings**, each developed rather than listed.
4. **Apply to the business in the question.** Transaction volume, number of sites and current staff skills all change the answer.
5. **Judge**, and give the condition: it depends on the volume of transactions, the cost relative to the saving, and whether the staff can be trained.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Garbage in, garbage out.** Speed and accuracy of calculation do not improve the quality of the input.
- **The cost may exceed the benefit for a small business.**
- **Dependence is a risk**, and a system failure can stop trading.
- **A spreadsheet has almost no audit trail**, which is a real weakness for financial records.
- **It changes the work rather than removing it.** Staff move from posting to interpreting, which requires different skills.
- **It depends on transaction volume**, which is the single most useful condition to state.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Writing generally about computers** instead of about accounting uses.
- **Naming specific software packages.** The specification says you will not be examined on them.
- **Listing advantages with no disadvantages**, or the reverse, in an evaluation question.
- **Ignoring the four specified headings**, which are effectively the mark scheme's structure.
- **Claiming ICT removes errors.** It removes calculation errors and not input errors.
- **Forgetting the human dimension**, which students routinely omit and which carries marks.
- **Treating the audit trail as automatic.** It depends on the controls and the access rights in place.
- **Assuming the investment is always worthwhile**, without reference to the size and volume of the business in the question.

</Mistakes>
