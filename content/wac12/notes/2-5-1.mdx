## Purpose and stages

**Purpose.** A standard cost sets what a unit **should** cost, so actual results can be compared against a benchmark rather than against last year. It supports budget preparation, inventory valuation, pricing decisions and, above all, cost control by exception.

**Stages in establishing a system:**

1. **Study the product and process** to establish the material and labour required per unit.
2. **Set the standard quantity** of material and the standard labour hours, from technical study and past records.
3. **Set the standard price** per unit of material and the standard wage rate per hour, from supplier quotations and wage agreements.
4. **Set overhead absorption rates** from the budgeted overhead and budgeted activity.
5. **Build the standard cost card** for the unit.
6. **Collect actual data from the accounting records:** purchase invoices for prices, stores requisitions for usage, payroll for rates and hours, timesheets for output.
7. **Calculate variances, report them, investigate the significant ones and revise the standards** when conditions have genuinely changed.

**Types of standard.** An **ideal** standard assumes perfect efficiency, so it is almost always missed and can demotivate. An **attainable** standard assumes efficient working with normal allowances, and is the usual choice because it is both a fair benchmark and a credible target. A **basic** standard is left unchanged for years for trend comparison, and becomes useless for control.

## Setting out the variances

<Formula name="The four detailed variances" note="In every formula the STANDARD figure comes first, so a positive answer is FAVOURABLE and a negative answer is ADVERSE. Keeping that order consistently removes almost all sign confusion.">
Material price   = (Std price − Actual price) × Actual quantity
Material usage   = (Std quantity for actual output − Actual quantity) × Std price

Labour rate      = (Std rate − Actual rate) × Actual hours
Labour efficiency = (Std hours for actual output − Actual hours) × Std rate
</Formula>

**The rule that makes this topic manageable:** a **price-type** variance is valued at the **actual quantity**, and a **quantity-type** variance is valued at the **standard price**. Material price uses actual kilograms; material usage uses the standard price per kilogram. Labour rate uses actual hours; labour efficiency uses the standard rate per hour.

**"Standard quantity for actual output" is the phrase to get right.** It is not the budgeted quantity. If the standard is 3 kg per unit and 5,000 units were actually made, the standard quantity is 15,000 kg, whatever the budget said.

<Worked title="Worked example: material and labour variances">

**Standard cost card per unit:** 3 kg of material at **$4.00** per kg = $12.00; 2 hours of labour at **$9.00** per hour = $18.00.

**Actual results:** **5,000** units produced. **16,200 kg** of material bought and used at a total cost of **$61,560**. **9,600** labour hours worked at a total cost of **$89,280**.

**Step 1 — derive the actual prices.**

`Actual price per kg = 61,560 ÷ 16,200 = $3.80`

`Actual rate per hour = 89,280 ÷ 9,600 = $9.30`

**Step 2 — the standards for the actual output.**

`Standard quantity = 5,000 × 3 = 15,000 kg`

`Standard hours = 5,000 × 2 = 10,000 hours`

**Step 3 — material variances.**

`Price = (4.00 − 3.80) × 16,200 = $3,240 favourable`

`Usage = (15,000 − 16,200) × 4.00 = −$4,800, so $4,800 adverse`

`Total material = 3,240 F − 4,800 A = $1,560 adverse`

**Check:** standard cost of material for actual output = 15,000 × 4.00 = $60,000. Actual cost = $61,560. Difference = $1,560 adverse. The check agrees.

**Step 4 — labour variances.**

`Rate = (9.00 − 9.30) × 9,600 = −$2,880, so $2,880 adverse`

`Efficiency = (10,000 − 9,600) × 9.00 = $3,600 favourable`

`Total labour = 2,880 A + 3,600 F = $720 favourable`

**Check:** standard labour cost for actual output = 10,000 × 9.00 = $90,000. Actual = $89,280. Difference = $720 favourable. Agrees.

**Step 5 — read the story, which is where the marks are.**

The company bought material **$0.20 per kg below standard** and used **1,200 kg more than it should have**. The most likely explanation is that a cheaper, poorer-quality material was bought, and it produced more wastage. The usage loss of $4,800 exceeds the price saving of $3,240, so **the purchasing decision cost the company $1,560 overall**.

On labour, the company paid **$0.30 per hour above standard** and used **400 fewer hours than standard**. The likely explanation is that more skilled or more experienced workers were used, and the efficiency gain of $3,600 exceeded the extra wage cost of $2,880, so **that decision earned $720**.

</Worked>

## Overhead variances

Only the **totals** are examined at this level, which makes them the easiest marks in the topic.

<Formula name="Overhead variances">
Total fixed overhead variance = Fixed overhead absorbed − Fixed overhead incurred
  where Absorbed = Actual output × Standard fixed overhead per unit

Total variable overhead variance = (Actual output × Std variable overhead per unit) − Actual variable overhead
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: overheads">

Budgeted fixed overhead **$180,000** for a budgeted output of **4,500** units, so the standard is **$40** per unit. Actual output **5,000** units; actual fixed overhead **$191,000**.

`Absorbed = 5,000 × 40 = $200,000`

`Total fixed overhead variance = 200,000 − 191,000 = $9,000 favourable`

Standard variable overhead **$6** per unit; actual variable overhead **$32,500**.

`Total variable overhead variance = (5,000 × 6) − 32,500 = 30,000 − 32,500 = $2,500 adverse`

**Note why the fixed overhead variance is favourable:** producing 500 units more than budget absorbed $20,000 of extra overhead, which more than covered the $11,000 of overspending. A favourable total here can therefore mean higher output rather than better cost control, which is a useful point in a written answer.

</Worked>

## Reasons for variances

| Variance | Favourable because | Adverse because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Material price** | bulk discount, cheaper supplier, lower-grade material, favourable exchange rate, standard set too high | supplier price rise, urgent small order, higher-grade material bought, weaker exchange rate |
| **Material usage** | better-quality material, less wastage, improved method, more skilled staff | poor-quality material, untrained staff, faulty machinery, theft, over-generous standard |
| **Labour rate** | lower-graded staff used, overtime avoided, pay rise deferred | pay award above standard, overtime premium, higher-graded or agency staff used |
| **Labour efficiency** | skilled or experienced staff, better machinery, good-quality material, high morale | untrained staff, machine breakdown, poor-quality material, low morale, idle time |
| **Fixed overhead** | output above budget, overhead spending controlled | output below budget, rent or insurance increase, unexpected repairs |
| **Variable overhead** | lower power or consumable usage | higher power costs, more consumables per unit |

**The general point worth stating in any written answer:** a variance can arise because performance differed from the standard, **or** because the standard itself was wrong. Investigating a variance therefore means checking the standard as well as the performance.

## Interrelationships

This is where the higher marks are, and the question often asks about it directly.

- **Cheap material causes wastage.** A favourable material price variance with an adverse usage variance is the classic pair, as in the worked example. Compare the two amounts to say whether the decision was worthwhile.
- **Poor material slows production.** The same cheap material also produces an **adverse labour efficiency** variance, because workers spend time on rework and rejects. So one purchasing decision can produce three variances.
- **Skilled labour costs more and works faster.** An adverse rate variance with a favourable efficiency variance, again comparable in money terms.
- **Overtime cuts both ways.** It creates an adverse rate variance through the premium and may create a favourable fixed overhead variance by raising output.
- **Machine breakdown** creates adverse labour efficiency through idle time and adverse fixed overhead through repairs.
- **Cutting maintenance** produces a favourable overhead variance now and adverse efficiency variances later.

**The single most valuable sentence available here:** a favourable variance is not automatically good news, because it may have been bought at the cost of a larger adverse variance elsewhere, or by sacrificing quality that will cost the company later.

## Management by exception

Reporting every variance overwhelms managers and wastes investigation time on noise. **Management by exception** sets a materiality threshold, in money or as a percentage of standard, and investigates only variances above it.

**Advantages:** it concentrates scarce management time where it can make a difference, it speeds up the response to real problems, and it avoids demotivating managers over trivial differences.

**Limitations:** a small variance that recurs every month may matter more than one large one-off; the threshold is a judgement and a wrongly set one hides real problems; and favourable variances are often left uninvestigated even though they may indicate a wrong standard or a quality shortcut. **A well-run system investigates significant variances in both directions.**

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Usually a Section B 30-mark question, occasionally a Section A part. Typical parts: calculate the material and labour variances (8–12 marks), calculate the total overhead variances (4–6 marks), give possible reasons (4–6 marks), then evaluate the usefulness of standard costing or advise on the purchasing decision (8–12 marks).

**Method that protects marks:**

1. **Write out the standard cost card** and the actual results before calculating anything.
2. **Derive actual price per kg and actual rate per hour** by dividing the totals. Questions usually give totals, not rates.
3. **Calculate the standard quantity and standard hours for the ACTUAL output**, and label that working clearly.
4. **Keep the standard first in every formula**, so a positive result is favourable.
5. **Label every answer F or A.** An unlabelled number cannot be given full marks.
6. **Check each pair against the total**, as in the worked example. The check takes ten seconds and catches most errors.

**Structuring the reasons and evaluation:**

1. **Pair the variances up** and state the likely single cause.
2. **Compare the amounts** to say whether the decision gained or lost money overall.
3. **Consider whether the standard itself is wrong**, especially if the same variance recurs.
4. **Bring in the quality dimension:** a cheap material saves cost now and may cost customers later.
5. **Judge**, and say what further information you would want, such as the wastage rate or the supplier's quality record.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **A favourable variance is not automatically good news.**
- **Variances interrelate**, so one decision can cause several.
- **The standard may be wrong**, not the performance.
- **Standard costing is historical** and reports after the event.
- **It suits repetitive production** and fits a varied, service or bespoke operation poorly.
- **Investigate both directions**, because a favourable variance can signal a shortcut.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using the budgeted output instead of the actual output** when calculating the standard quantity or standard hours.
- **Valuing the usage variance at the actual price** instead of the standard price.
- **Valuing the price variance at the standard quantity** instead of the actual quantity.
- **Omitting the F or A label.**
- **Reversing the sign** by putting the actual figure first.
- **Calculating a total variance that does not equal the sum of its parts**, and not noticing.
- **Treating every favourable variance as good performance.**
- **Giving reasons that are just restatements.** "The price variance is favourable because the price was lower" earns nothing; name a cause.
- **Discussing variances in isolation** when the question asks about interrelationships.
- **Forgetting that fixed overhead absorbed depends on actual output**, so a volume change alone creates a variance.

</Mistakes>
