## The core idea

Every unit sold brings in its price and costs its variable cost. The difference **contributes** first to covering fixed costs, and then, once those are covered, entirely to profit.

<Formula name="The three formulas" note="Note that fixed costs appear only at the last step. That is why contribution is the right tool for short-term decisions.">
Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit

Total contribution = Contribution per unit × Units sold

Profit = Total contribution − Fixed costs
</Formula>

## Why fixed costs are usually irrelevant to a short-term decision

This is the whole reason contribution exists as a decision-making technique.

Fixed costs are paid whether or not you accept a particular order. They are therefore **not relevant** to the decision, because they do not change as a result of it. Only the variable costs change, so only contribution matters.

That leads to the decision rule: **accept any additional order whose price exceeds its variable cost**, because it adds contribution, and any contribution is better than none.

<Worked title="Worked example: a special order">

A business sells at **$40** a unit with variable costs of **$22**. Fixed costs are **$180,000** a year and it currently sells **15,000** units.

`Contribution per unit = 40 − 22 = $18`

`Total contribution = 15,000 × 18 = $270,000`

`Profit = 270,000 − 180,000 = $90,000`

**A customer now offers to buy 2,000 units at $28 each**, well below the normal $40. The business has spare capacity.

**The wrong approach:** total cost per unit is (180,000 ÷ 15,000) + 22 = $12 + $22 = $34, so $28 is below cost and the order should be refused.

**The right approach:** the fixed costs are already covered by existing sales and do not change. So:

`Contribution from the order = 2,000 × (28 − 22) = 2,000 × $6 = $12,000`

`New profit = 90,000 + 12,000 = $102,000`

**Accept the order.** It adds $12,000 of profit, because the $180,000 of fixed costs is paid either way.

**But now evaluate, which is where the marks are:**

- **Is there really spare capacity?** If accepting this order displaces full-price sales, the business loses $18 of contribution to gain $6. That reverses the decision entirely.
- **Will existing customers find out?** Selling at $28 to one customer and $40 to others invites everyone to demand $28.
- **Is it a one-off?** A recurring order at $28 becomes the price, and then the fixed costs are no longer covered.
- **Does it lead anywhere?** A low-price order that opens a long-term relationship may be worth more than its contribution.

</Worked>

## Contribution when a resource is scarce

If a resource is limited, machine hours, labour hours, a scarce material, the most profitable product is **not** the one with the highest contribution per unit. It is the one with the highest contribution per unit of the scarce resource.

<Worked title="Worked example: limiting factor">

Two products, and machine hours are limited.

| | Product X | Product Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contribution per unit | $30 | $20 |
| Machine hours per unit | 3 | 1 |
| **Contribution per machine hour** | **$10** | **$20** |

X has the higher contribution per unit, and Y generates twice as much contribution from each scarce machine hour. **Make Y.**

Ranking by contribution per unit rather than per limiting factor is a classic trap.

</Worked>

## Other uses

- **Break-even analysis** (WBS12 **2.3.2.3**), where break-even output is fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
- **Deciding whether to drop a product.** A product covering its variable costs is contributing; dropping it means the fixed costs it was helping to cover fall on everything else.
- **Make or buy decisions**, comparing the variable cost of making with the price of buying in.
- **Pricing in the short run**, where a price above variable cost is better than no sale at all.

## Limitations

- **It is a short-term tool.** In the long run every cost is variable, and fixed costs must be covered or the business fails.
- **It assumes fixed costs genuinely do not change.** A large extra order may push the business past its capacity and require new fixed costs.
- **It assumes spare capacity exists.** Without it, the relevant comparison is what the order displaces.
- **It ignores the effect on normal pricing** and on customer relationships.
- **It ignores quality and reputation.**

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) contribution or profit, Assess (12) whether to accept a special order or drop a product.

**Answering a special order question:**

1. Calculate the contribution per unit of the order: price minus variable cost.
2. Multiply by the units to get the total contribution added.
3. **State clearly that fixed costs are unchanged**, so the decision rests on contribution alone. This is the mark most candidates miss.
4. Give the recommendation.
5. **Then evaluate:** spare capacity, displaced full-price sales, effect on existing customers, whether it is recurring.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Does spare capacity exist?** Without it the answer can reverse.
- **What does it do to normal prices?** Other customers will want the same deal.
- **One-off or recurring?** Fixed costs must be covered in the long run.
- **With a scarce resource, rank by contribution per limiting factor.**
- **Non-financial factors:** reputation, relationship, quality.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using total cost per unit instead of variable cost** in a short-term decision. This is the error the topic exists to correct.
- **Deducting fixed costs from the special order's contribution.** They are already paid.
- **Ranking products by contribution per unit when a resource is scarce.** Use contribution per limiting factor.
- **Forgetting to deduct fixed costs** when calculating overall profit.
- **Accepting a low-price order with no mention of spare capacity.**
- **Ignoring the effect on existing customers' prices.**
- **Treating contribution as profit.** Contribution becomes profit only after fixed costs are covered.

</Mistakes>
