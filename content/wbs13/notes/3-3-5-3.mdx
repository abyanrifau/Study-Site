## The four calculations

<Formula name="All four" note="All three of turnover, retention and absenteeism are percentages. Read carefully whether the question gives you the number employed at the start, the end, or the average.">
Labour productivity = Total output ÷ Number of employees

Labour turnover = (Staff leaving ÷ Average number employed) × 100

Labour retention = (Staff employed throughout ÷ Average number employed) × 100

Absenteeism = (Staff days absent ÷ Total staff days possible) × 100
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example">

A business employs an average of **80** staff. During the year **18** left. **62** were employed for the whole year. Staff were absent for **640** days out of a possible 80 × 250 = **20,000** staff days.

`Labour turnover = (18 ÷ 80) × 100 = 22.5%`

`Labour retention = (62 ÷ 80) × 100 = 77.5%`

`Absenteeism = (640 ÷ 20,000) × 100 = 3.2%`

**Notice turnover and retention total 100%**, which is a useful check on your arithmetic.

**Now cost the turnover, which is what turns the calculation into analysis.** If replacing one employee costs $2,000 in advertising, management time, induction and lost productivity:

`Annual cost of turnover = 18 × $2,000 = $36,000`

Cutting turnover from 22.5% to 12% would mean about 8 fewer leavers, saving roughly **$16,000 a year**. That figure is what you compare against the cost of a pay rise or a training programme, and it is where the evaluation marks are.

</Worked>

## Interpreting the figures

**High labour turnover** may mean low pay, poor management, no progression, boring work, or better opportunities elsewhere. It costs money directly, and it also means a permanently inexperienced workforce, which reduces productivity and quality.

**Some turnover is healthy.** Zero turnover means no new ideas, no fresh skills and no way to move on poor performers. The optimum is low, not nil.

**Turnover varies enormously by sector.** Hospitality and retail run at rates that would be alarming in manufacturing. Judge against the industry, not against zero.

**High absenteeism** may indicate low morale, poor working conditions, stress, or weak management. It may also indicate a genuine health problem or an unusually long-serving, older workforce.

**Productivity** links back to WBS12 **2.3.4.1**, and the point to make is that all these measures interact: high turnover produces an inexperienced workforce, which lowers productivity and raises costs.

## Strategies to improve

The specification names four.

- **Financial rewards.** Pay rises, bonuses, performance-related pay. Fast and visible, and in Herzberg's terms pay is a hygiene factor, so it stops dissatisfaction rather than creating motivation. It is also the most expensive option and easily matched by a competitor.
- **Employee share ownership.** Staff own part of the business, so they share in its success and have a reason to stay. It aligns interests over the long term, and an individual's effort barely moves the share price, so the link to daily behaviour is weak.
- **Consultation strategies.** Asking employees before deciding. Cheap, and it addresses Mayo's social needs directly. It must be genuine: consulting and then ignoring the answer is worse than not asking.
- **Empowerment strategies.** Giving authority over their own work. Strongly motivating in Herzberg's terms because it delivers responsibility. It needs training and clear boundaries, and it does not suit every role.

**The pattern to notice:** the financial route treats a hygiene factor; the other three deliver motivators. So if the problem is that work is boring and staff feel powerless, a pay rise buys a short reprieve and does not fix it.

## Limitations of these measures

- **They show a symptom, not a cause.** A turnover figure of 22.5% tells you there is a problem, not what it is. You need exit interviews and staff surveys.
- **Averages hide distribution.** 22.5% overall may be 5% among managers and 60% among frontline staff, which are completely different problems.
- **No industry context** in the number itself.
- **Definitions vary.** Does turnover include retirements, redundancies and fixed-term endings? Comparisons need care.
- **Some turnover and absence is unavoidable** and even desirable.
- **They are historical.**

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) any of the four measures, Explain (4) on one strategy, Evaluate (20) on how a business should improve its human resource performance.

**Structuring the big question:**

1. **Calculate the relevant measures** and compare with the previous year or the industry figure given.
2. **Cost the problem.** Turnover multiplied by replacement cost turns a percentage into money.
3. **Diagnose before prescribing.** What does the source suggest is causing it: pay, management, boredom, progression?
4. **Recommend two strategies**, one financial and one non-financial, and chain each to the effect.
5. **Compare the cost of the strategy with the cost of the problem.**
6. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The figures show a symptom, not a cause.** Say what further information you would want.
- **Some turnover is healthy.** Zero is not the target.
- **Compare with the industry**, because sector norms differ hugely.
- **Herzberg:** a pay rise treats a hygiene factor and may not address the real cause.
- **Cost the remedy against the cost of the problem**, using the figures.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Dividing by the number of leavers** instead of the average number employed.
- **Forgetting to multiply by 100.**
- **Confusing turnover with retention.** They are complements and should total roughly 100%.
- **Getting the absenteeism denominator wrong.** It is employees multiplied by working days, not just employees.
- **Assuming zero turnover is ideal.**
- **Comparing turnover across industries** without noting that norms differ.
- **Recommending a pay rise with no reference to Herzberg** or to the actual cause.
- **Reporting the percentage without costing it.** The money figure is what persuades.

</Mistakes>
