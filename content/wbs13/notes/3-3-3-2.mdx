## The data you are given

Investment appraisal questions always give you a table like this:

| Year | Net cash flow ($000) | Discount factor (10%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | (400) | 1.00 |
| 1 | 120 | 0.91 |
| 2 | 160 | 0.83 |
| 3 | 180 | 0.75 |
| 4 | 140 | 0.68 |

Year 0 is the initial investment, shown as a negative. The discount factors are always provided; you never calculate them.

## Simple payback

<Formula name="Payback period" note="Work down the cumulative column until it turns positive. Then convert the part-year into months.">
Cumulative net cash flow, year by year, until it turns positive

Part year = (Amount still to recover ÷ That year's cash flow) × 12
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: payback">

| Year | Cash flow | Cumulative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | (400) | (400) |
| 1 | 120 | (280) |
| 2 | 160 | (120) |
| 3 | 180 | +60 |

After year 2 the business is still $120,000 short. Year 3 brings in $180,000.

`Part year = (120 ÷ 180) × 12 = 8 months`

**Payback = 2 years and 8 months.**

</Worked>

**For:** simple, quick, easy to explain, and it focuses on risk. A business short of cash cares most about how soon it gets its money back.

**Against:** it **ignores everything after payback**. A project paying back in three years then earning nothing looks better than one paying back in four years then earning for a decade. It also ignores the time value of money and gives no measure of profitability.

## Average rate of return

<Formula name="ARR" note="The most common error is forgetting to deduct the initial investment before averaging. Total profit is total inflows MINUS the investment.">
Total net profit = Total cash inflows − Initial investment

Average annual profit = Total net profit ÷ Number of years

ARR = (Average annual profit ÷ Initial investment) × 100
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: ARR">

Total inflows over four years: `120 + 160 + 180 + 140 = $600,000`

`Total net profit = 600 − 400 = $200,000`

`Average annual profit = 200 ÷ 4 = $50,000`

`ARR = (50 ÷ 400) × 100 = 12.5%`

**Interpreting it:** 12.5% a year on average. The decision depends on comparison: against the criterion rate the business has set, against the interest rate it could earn or must pay, and against alternative projects. If the business borrows at 9%, a 12.5% return is worthwhile; if it borrows at 14%, it is not.

</Worked>

**For:** gives a percentage, so it is directly comparable with interest rates and with other projects. Uses all the cash flows, not just those up to payback.

**Against:** ignores the **timing** of the cash flows entirely, so a project earning most of its money in year one scores the same as one earning it in year four. It also ignores the time value of money.

## Net present value

The idea: $100 received in three years is worth less than $100 today, because today's $100 could be invested, and because of inflation and risk. So future cash flows are **discounted** back to present value.

<Formula name="NPV" note="Multiply each year's cash flow by its discount factor, add them up, then subtract the initial investment. Positive NPV means accept.">
NPV = Σ (Net cash flow × Discount factor) − Initial investment
</Formula>

<Worked title="Worked example: NPV at 10%">

| Year | Cash flow | Factor | Present value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 120 | 0.91 | 109.2 |
| 2 | 160 | 0.83 | 132.8 |
| 3 | 180 | 0.75 | 135.0 |
| 4 | 140 | 0.68 | 95.2 |
| | | **Total PV** | **472.2** |

`NPV = 472.2 − 400 = +$72,200`

**Interpreting it:** positive, so the project returns more than the 10% cost of capital used to discount it. Accept it.

**Notice what discounting did.** The undiscounted surplus was $200,000; the discounted surplus is $72,200. Most of that difference is the year 3 and 4 money, which is worth far less in today's terms. A project whose returns come late is hurt most by discounting, and that is exactly the weakness the other two methods miss.

</Worked>

**For:** the only method that accounts for the time value of money. Uses all cash flows. Gives a clear decision rule: positive means accept.

**Against:** the answer depends entirely on the **discount rate chosen**, and choosing it is a judgement. It is harder to explain to non-financial managers, and it still relies on forecast cash flows that may be wrong.

## Using all three together

| | Payback | ARR | NPV |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measures | risk and liquidity | profitability | value in today's money |
| Uses all cash flows | no | yes | yes |
| Accounts for timing | no | no | yes |
| Gives a percentage | no | yes | no |

They answer different questions, so a good business uses more than one. A project can have a short payback and a negative NPV, or a high ARR and a dangerously long payback. Where two methods disagree, say so and explain which matters more given this business's situation.

## The limitation that applies to all three

**They all depend on forecast cash flows.** Those forecasts are estimates, often optimistic, and the further out they go the less reliable they are. A precise NPV calculated from guesses is still a guess.

**And they are all purely financial.** They ignore staff, reputation, environmental impact, strategic fit and the effect on existing products. A project with the best NPV can still be the wrong decision.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse considering a $400,000 room extension faces exactly this trade-off. Payback matters enormously, because the loan has to be serviced through the wet season, so a four-year payback is risky in a way it would not be for a business with steady income.

NPV matters because the returns are spread over many years and the discount rate should reflect real risk: tourism demand is volatile and exposed to external shocks. A discount rate that ignores that understates the risk and overstates the NPV.

So the sensible approach is to calculate all three, weight payback heavily because of the seasonal cash flow, and test the NPV at a higher discount rate to see whether the project still stands up. That sensitivity point is a strong evaluation move.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) very often, and Evaluate (20) on whether to proceed with an investment.

**Method for the calculations:**

- **Payback:** build the cumulative column, then convert the part-year to months. Give the answer in years and months.
- **ARR:** deduct the investment from total inflows FIRST, then average, then express as a percentage of the initial investment.
- **NPV:** multiply each year by its factor, total the present values, subtract the investment. State whether it is positive and therefore acceptable.

**Structuring the Evaluate answer:**

1. Calculate whichever methods the data allows, and show the workings.
2. **Interpret each one**, not just report it. What does a 12.5% ARR mean against the cost of borrowing?
3. Note where the methods agree or disagree.
4. **Evaluate the forecasts**: how reliable are the cash flows, and what happens if they are 20% lower?
5. Bring in the non-financial factors: strategic fit, staff, capacity, reputation.
6. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **All three rest on forecast cash flows**, which are estimates.
- **The discount rate is a judgement**, and the NPV is sensitive to it.
- **Payback ignores everything after payback.**
- **ARR ignores timing.**
- **Non-financial factors can outweigh the numbers.**
- **What is the opportunity cost** of the $400,000?

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Forgetting to deduct the initial investment** before averaging in ARR. The single most common error.
- **Dividing by the wrong figure in ARR.** The denominator is the initial investment, not total profit.
- **Giving payback as a decimal of a year** when months are expected. Convert it.
- **Trying to calculate discount factors.** They are always given.
- **Forgetting to subtract the initial investment** at the end of the NPV.
- **Saying a positive NPV means the project is profitable in cash terms only.** It means it beats the discount rate used.
- **Reporting the numbers with no interpretation.** The comparison is the analysis.
- **Treating the forecasts as facts.**

</Mistakes>
