## What is being planned for

- **Natural events:** flood, storm, earthquake, drought.
- **Operational failure:** fire, machinery breakdown, IT failure, cyber attack, data loss.
- **Supply chain failure:** a key supplier collapsing, a port closing, a shipping route disrupted.
- **Loss of key people:** the founder, a key designer, a relationship manager.
- **Financial shocks:** a sharp exchange rate movement, an interest rate rise, a large customer not paying.
- **Reputational crisis:** a product recall, a scandal, a viral complaint.
- **External shocks:** recession, a change in regulation, a pandemic.

## The risk management process

<Chunk title="Four steps" open>

1. **Identify** the risks the business faces.
2. **Assess** each one by **likelihood** and **impact**. This is the step that turns a long list into a usable plan.
3. **Decide the response.** The four standard options:
   - **Avoid** the activity that creates the risk.
   - **Reduce** the likelihood or impact, for example with backups, training or dual suppliers.
   - **Transfer** it, usually through insurance or a contract clause.
   - **Accept** it, where the cost of doing anything exceeds the expected loss.
4. **Review**, because the risks change.

**The judgement to make:** plan properly for **high impact** risks, even unlikely ones, because a low-probability event that would close the business is worth insuring against. Do not spend heavily on high-likelihood, low-impact risks; absorb them.

</Chunk>

## Business continuity and succession

**Business continuity planning** asks what the business must keep doing whatever happens, and how. Typical elements: off-site data backups, an alternative site, second suppliers, emergency contact lists, named decision-makers, cash reserves or an agreed overdraft facility, and a rehearsal so the plan has been tested rather than just written.

**Succession planning** asks who could do each key job tomorrow. In a small business the answer is often nobody, which makes the founder a single point of failure. It links to the entrepreneur-to-leader transition in WBS11 **1.3.4.5** and to the power culture in **3.3.4.1**: the more decisions run through one person, the greater the risk of losing them.

## Costs and benefits

**Benefits:** a much faster response when the event happens, lower losses, reassurance for lenders, insurers, customers and staff, and the planning process itself often reveals weaknesses worth fixing anyway. The best-known benefit is speed: in a crisis, the business with a plan acts while the business without one holds meetings.

**Costs:** management time, holding spare capacity, inventory or cash that earns nothing, insurance premiums, and the risk of planning for the wrong event. A plan can also create false confidence, because a document in a drawer that nobody has rehearsed is not a capability.

**The core trade-off:** contingency planning is paying a certain small cost now to avoid an uncertain large cost later. That framing, taken from risk and uncertainty in WBS11 **1.3.5.3**, is the cleanest way to structure the evaluation.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

The sector shows why high-impact planning matters more than high-likelihood planning. A resort is a single island dependent on imported food and fuel, seaplane or speedboat transfers, desalinated water and a generator. A single point of failure in any of those stops the whole operation, with guests already on site.

So continuity planning concentrates on duplication: a second generator, a water reserve, a fuel buffer, a second transfer operator. Each costs money continuously for an event that may never happen, and the alternative is guests stranded without water, which is both a safety issue and a reputational one that outlasts the incident by years. The wider economic version of the same problem is the lack of diversification in **WEC14**: a country whose export earnings depend on one sector has no contingency plan at national level.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one benefit or cost of contingency planning; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business should invest in a plan for a specific risk.

**Structuring the answer:**

1. **Identify the specific risk** from the source.
2. **Place it on likelihood and impact.** High impact justifies planning even when unlikely.
3. **Set out the cost of planning**, with figures if given.
4. **Set out the cost of the event happening unprepared**, again with figures.
5. **Compare.** If the expected loss exceeds the cost of the plan, plan.
6. **Judge**, and note what else the money could do.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Plan for impact, not just likelihood.**
- **Opportunity cost:** money tied up in spare capacity is not earning.
- **An untested plan is not a capability.**
- **The planning process has value even if the event never happens**, because it exposes weaknesses.
- **You cannot plan for everything**, so general resilience such as cash reserves and flexibility may beat specific plans.
- **Certain small cost now against uncertain large cost later** is the framing to use.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing risks without assessing likelihood and impact.**
- **Claiming a business should plan for everything.** It is unaffordable, and opportunity cost is a valid argument.
- **Ignoring the opportunity cost** of spare capacity, insurance and inventory.
- **Confusing contingency planning with crisis management.** Planning happens before; crisis management happens during.
- **Forgetting succession planning**, which the specification includes and students routinely omit.
- **Not using the figures** in the source to compare the cost of the plan with the cost of the event.
- **Assuming insurance solves it.** Insurance transfers financial loss and does not restore lost customers or reputation.

</Mistakes>
