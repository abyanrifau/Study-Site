## The five ratios

Two you already know from WBS12 (**2.3.3.1** and **2.3.3.2**), plus two new ones that matter most at this level.

<Formula name="All the formulas" note="Margins and ROCE are percentages. Liquidity ratios are written as a ratio, e.g. 1.8:1.">
Gross profit margin = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Profit for the year margin = (Profit for the year ÷ Revenue) × 100

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities

Acid test = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities

Gearing = (Non-current liabilities ÷ Capital employed) × 100

ROCE = (Operating profit ÷ Capital employed) × 100
</Formula>

## Gearing

Gearing measures how much of the long-term finance is **borrowed** rather than owned.

**Capital employed = total equity + non-current liabilities.**

| Gearing | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Below 25% | low geared, financed mainly by equity |
| 25 to 50% | moderate |
| Above 50% | highly geared, heavily dependent on debt |

**High gearing is risky:** interest must be paid whatever happens, so a fall in profit is amplified. It also makes further borrowing harder and more expensive, and leaves the business exposed to interest rate rises.

**High gearing is not automatically bad.** Debt is usually cheaper than equity, interest is a fixed cost so all the upside belongs to the owners, and no control is given away. A profitable business with stable cash flows can carry substantial debt sensibly. A business with volatile or seasonal income cannot.

## Return on capital employed

<Worked title="Worked example">

A business has operating profit of **$126,000**. Its equity is **$500,000** and its non-current liabilities are **$200,000**.

`Capital employed = 500,000 + 200,000 = $700,000`

`ROCE = (126,000 ÷ 700,000) × 100 = 18%`

`Gearing = (200,000 ÷ 700,000) × 100 = 28.6%`

**Interpreting them together, which is what the marks are for.**

An 18% ROCE means every $100 of long-term finance generates $18 of operating profit. **Compare it with the cost of that finance.** If the business borrows at 8%, an 18% return comfortably exceeds it, so borrowing to invest more is sensible. If it borrowed at 20%, the business would be destroying value.

Gearing of 28.6% is moderate, so there is capacity to borrow more if a good project exists. And because ROCE exceeds the interest rate, additional borrowing would raise the return to shareholders.

**That combination, ROCE against the cost of debt, plus gearing to show capacity, is the strongest use of ratios in this unit.**

</Worked>

ROCE is often called the primary efficiency ratio because it captures both profitability and how much capital was needed to produce it. A business with a high margin and a huge asset base can have a poor ROCE.

## Using ratios properly

**A single ratio in isolation is meaningless.** You need a comparison:

- **Over time.** Is it improving or deteriorating? The trend usually matters more than the level.
- **Against a competitor.** Benchmarking against a similar business in the same industry.
- **Against a target** the business set itself.
- **Against the relevant external rate**, such as comparing ROCE with the cost of borrowing.

**And look at ratios together.** A business can improve its current ratio by borrowing short term, which worsens its position. Rising margins with falling ROCE means the capital base has grown faster than profit.

## Limitations of ratio analysis

- **Historical.** Published accounts describe the past, not the future.
- **A snapshot.** The statement of financial position is one date, which a business can arrange to flatter, known as window dressing.
- **Ignores everything non-financial:** staff, brand, customer satisfaction, innovation, reputation.
- **Comparisons need care.** Different accounting policies, different year ends and different industries make figures less comparable than they look.
- **No context.** A falling margin during deliberate expansion is not the same as a falling margin from losing control of costs.
- **Rules of thumb are sector-specific.** A supermarket and a shipbuilder cannot be judged by the same liquidity standard.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Calculate (4) any ratio, and Evaluate (20) on a business's performance from a set of accounts. This is one of the most heavily examined topics in WBS13.

**How to answer "assess the performance of this business":**

1. **Calculate a spread of ratios**, at least one profitability, one liquidity, plus gearing and ROCE.
2. **Compare each with the previous year or the competitor given.** State the direction.
3. **Interpret, do not just report.** ROCE of 18% against borrowing at 8% means something; "ROCE is 18%" does not.
4. **Look for the story linking them.** Margins holding but ROCE falling means the capital base grew. Liquidity improving while gearing rises means short-term comfort bought with long-term risk.
5. **Evaluate:** limitations of the data, what non-financial information you would want.
6. Judge overall.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Compare ROCE with the cost of borrowing.** Very few candidates do this and it is the single sharpest point available.
- **High gearing is risky and not automatically bad.** It depends on the stability of cash flows.
- **Ratios are historical**, and past performance does not guarantee future performance.
- **What is missing?** Staff, brand and customer data are absent from the accounts.
- **Is the comparison fair?** Same industry, same year end, same accounting policies?

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using profit for the year in ROCE.** Use **operating** profit.
- **Forgetting that capital employed = equity + non-current liabilities.**
- **Confusing gearing with liquidity.** Gearing is long-term debt; liquidity is short-term.
- **Quoting a ratio with no comparison.** Always compare over time or against something.
- **Saying high gearing is simply bad**, with no reference to cash flow stability.
- **Reporting numbers without interpretation.** The interpretation is the analysis.
- **Applying one industry's rule of thumb to another.**
- **Forgetting the % sign on margins, gearing and ROCE**, or writing liquidity ratios as percentages.

</Mistakes>
