## Who the stakeholders are, and what they want

| Stakeholder | Main objectives |
| --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** | dividends, rising share price, profit |
| **Employees** | secure jobs, good pay and conditions, development, fair treatment |
| **Managers** | performance, bonuses, status, career progression |
| **Customers** | quality, low prices, reliability, good service |
| **Suppliers** | prompt payment, regular orders, fair terms |
| **Lenders** | interest paid, capital repaid, low risk |
| **Government** | tax revenue, employment, compliance with law |
| **Local community** | employment, minimal pollution and disruption |
| **Pressure groups** | ethical and environmental behaviour |

## The two models

**Shareholder model.** The business exists to generate returns for its owners. Managers are agents acting on the shareholders' behalf, so decisions should be judged by their effect on share price and dividends. Anything else is spending other people's money.

- **For:** clear and measurable, gives a single objective, and shareholders bear the risk so they should get the reward. Capital flows to businesses that use it well.
- **Against:** encourages short-termism, because share price responds to quarterly results. It can justify cutting wages, training, quality and environmental spending, and it externalises costs onto communities and the environment.

**Stakeholder model.** The business should consider everyone affected. Employees, customers, suppliers and the community all contribute to and are affected by its success.

- **For:** better long-term relationships, higher staff motivation and retention, stronger reputation, lower risk of scandal or regulatory intervention, and more sustainable performance.
- **Against:** stakeholders' objectives conflict, so the business cannot satisfy all of them. It gives managers a vague objective and therefore wide discretion, which is hard to hold them to account for. Someone still has to decide whose interests come first.

## The conflicts

This is what Assess questions are really about.

- **Shareholders against employees.** Higher wages reduce profit and dividends.
- **Shareholders against customers.** Higher prices raise profit and reduce value for money.
- **Shareholders against the community.** Environmental spending reduces returns.
- **Employees against customers.** Higher wages may mean higher prices.
- **Short term against long term.** Cutting training and maintenance raises this year's profit and damages future capability. This is the most examinable conflict of all.
- **Managers against shareholders.** Managers may pursue growth or acquisitions for status rather than returns, which is why incentive schemes exist.

## The convergence argument

The best answers make this point: **over a long enough horizon the two models converge.**

- Mistreated employees leave, which raises costs and reduces quality, which reduces returns.
- Cheated suppliers stop offering credit or priority, which raises costs.
- Disappointed customers do not return, and in an online market they say so publicly.
- A community or regulator that loses patience imposes costs the business cannot control.

So treating stakeholders well is often the route to shareholder value rather than a sacrifice of it. **Where the two genuinely conflict is in the short run**, and that is where the difficult decisions sit.

## Stakeholder mapping

A practical tool: assess each stakeholder by **power** and **interest**.

- **High power, high interest** → manage closely. Major shareholders, key lenders, regulators.
- **High power, low interest** → keep satisfied. A large institutional investor not currently engaged.
- **Low power, high interest** → keep informed. Local community groups, small suppliers.
- **Low power, low interest** → monitor.

It tells you who a business will *actually* listen to, which is often the honest answer to "will they consider this stakeholder?"

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A resort development shows the conflict concretely. Shareholders want the fastest, cheapest construction and the highest occupancy. The local community wants employment, access to fishing grounds and minimal disruption. Employees want decent accommodation and rest days on a remote site. The government wants tax revenue and environmental compliance. Environmental groups want the reef protected.

The convergence argument applies with unusual force here, because the reef **is** the product. Damaging it to save construction cost destroys the asset the shareholders own. So in this case the environmental stakeholder's interest and the shareholder's long-term interest are the same thing, and only the short-run cost pulls the other way. That makes it an excellent example for the evaluation.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one conflict; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business should adopt a stakeholder or shareholder approach, or on how it should handle a decision affecting several stakeholders.

**Structuring the answer:**

1. **Identify the stakeholders in the source** and what each wants.
2. **Name the specific conflict** the decision creates.
3. **Argue the shareholder case** for the decision, with figures if given.
4. **Argue the stakeholder case** against it.
5. **Make the convergence point:** what does the short-term saving cost in the long run?
6. **Judge**, and say which stakeholder's interest should come first and why. Using stakeholder power to explain who the business will actually listen to is a sophisticated move.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The two models converge in the long run**, and conflict in the short run.
- **Stakeholders have unequal power**, so "considering all stakeholders" in practice means considering the powerful ones.
- **The stakeholder model gives managers a vague objective**, which makes accountability harder.
- **It depends on the time horizon**, which is the single most useful distinction here.
- **It depends on the business's objectives and ownership.** A family business and a listed company answer differently.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing stakeholder and shareholder.** Shareholders own shares; stakeholders are anyone affected.
- **Listing stakeholders without their objectives** or the conflicts between them.
- **Saying the stakeholder model is simply better.** It is vague and stakeholders conflict.
- **Missing the time dimension**, which is where the real distinction lies.
- **Assuming all stakeholders have equal influence.**
- **Forgetting managers are stakeholders** with their own interests, which may diverge from shareholders'.
- **Not applying to the source.** The decision in the question determines which conflict matters.

</Mistakes>
