## Diseconomies of scale

Average cost per unit falls as a business grows, up to a point. Beyond the **optimum size** it starts to rise again, and the three standard causes are all human rather than technical.

<Chunk title="The three causes" open>

**Communication problems.** More layers of hierarchy means a longer chain of command, so messages travel slowly and get distorted. Decisions take longer, the people at the front no longer know what the strategy is, and information from customers no longer reaches the people who could act on it.

**Coordination difficulties.** More departments, sites and products means more to keep aligned. Duplication appears, departments pursue conflicting objectives, and the management effort required to hold it together becomes a cost in itself. More managers are needed, and managers are expensive.

**Falling motivation.** In a large organisation an individual feels remote from the outcome. The work is more specialised and less varied, the manager is one of many reporting to a stretched supervisor, and the connection between effort and result disappears. In Herzberg's terms the motivators, achievement, recognition, responsibility, all weaken as scale grows. Turnover and absenteeism rise, productivity falls, and unit costs rise with it.

</Chunk>

**Why it matters for the exam:** whenever a question proposes growth, diseconomies are the counter-argument, and naming the mechanism is what earns the mark rather than the phrase itself.

## Internal communication

The specification lists this separately, so treat it as its own problem.

As a business grows: the chain of command lengthens, informal communication that worked with twelve people fails with three hundred, formal systems and reporting have to replace conversation, and information is filtered as it passes up, so bad news travels slowly.

**Consequences:** slower decisions, duplicated work, mistakes from misunderstanding, and staff who feel uninformed and therefore uncommitted.

**Responses:** flatter structures and delayering (WBS11 **1.3.4.3**), decentralising authority, better systems, and deliberately keeping units small within a large business.

## Overtrading

Growing faster than the working capital allows. Covered in WBS12 **2.3.3.3** and directly examinable here as a problem of growth.

**The mechanism, which is what to write:**

> The business wins larger orders → it must buy stock and hire staff to fulfil them → it pays for those now → the customer pays 60 days later → the gap between paying out and being paid widens as sales grow → cash runs out → it cannot pay suppliers or wages → it fails, while profitable and growing.

**Why it catches good businesses:** it is caused by success. The faster the growth, the wider the cash gap.

**Responses:** forecast cash monthly, arrange finance before it is needed rather than during the crisis, take deposits, shorten customer credit terms, negotiate longer supplier credit, use factoring, or simply grow more slowly.

## The optimum size

Putting the ideas together: economies of scale pull average cost down, diseconomies push it back up, and the lowest point is the optimum size.

Three implications worth stating:

- **Bigger is not automatically better.** There is a size beyond which growth destroys value.
- **The optimum differs by industry.** Car manufacturing has a very large optimum; a restaurant has a small one, which is why restaurant chains replicate small units rather than building one enormous one.
- **Large businesses try to keep the benefits of being small** by decentralising, creating semi-autonomous divisions and keeping individual units small.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse operator growing from one property to four gets genuine economies. Growing to twenty properties across a dozen islands would hit every one of the three diseconomies hard.

**Communication:** the owner cannot visit twenty islands, so standards are transmitted through managers who transmit them through staff, and the personal service the reviews were built on is exactly what gets lost.

**Coordination:** twenty properties with different transfer arrangements, suppliers and staffing needs require a head office, which is a new fixed cost that did not exist before.

**Motivation:** a manager on a distant island with no autonomy and little contact is far less motivated than the owner was.

That is why the growth-against-control trade-off is so sharp in this sector, and why many successful operators deliberately stop at a handful of properties.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on diseconomies or overtrading; Explain (4) on one cause; and as the counter-argument in almost any Evaluate question about growth.

**Using it as a counter-argument:** do not simply assert that diseconomies might occur. Name which one, explain the mechanism, and apply it to the business. "Coordination costs would rise because a head office would be needed to manage twenty sites, which is a new fixed cost" is worth far more than "there may be diseconomies of scale".

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **There is an optimum size**, and it differs by industry.
- **Diseconomies are human, not technical.** They are about communication, coordination and motivation.
- **Overtrading is caused by success**, which makes it counter-intuitive and easy to overlook.
- **The problems are manageable**, through decentralisation and cash forecasting, but managing them costs money.
- **Growth has an opportunity cost** in the owner's attention as well as in capital.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Saying "diseconomies of scale" without naming a cause.** Communication, coordination or motivation.
- **Confusing diseconomies with a fall in demand.** Diseconomies raise cost per unit; they are not about revenue.
- **Confusing overtrading with trading at a loss.** Overtrading businesses are profitable and growing.
- **Assuming diseconomies are inevitable.** They can be managed by decentralising and keeping units small.
- **Forgetting the optimum size varies by industry.**
- **Not linking growth problems to the structure topic** in WBS11, where the solutions live.

</Mistakes>
