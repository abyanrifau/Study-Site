## Ansoff's Matrix

</Figure>

<Chunk title="The four strategies" open>

**Market penetration** — existing product, existing market. Sell more to the customers you have, or take share from rivals. Lowest risk, because you know both the product and the market. Achieved through promotion, price, loyalty schemes and wider distribution. Its limit is that the market may be saturated, so growth is capped.

**Product development** — new product, existing market. You know the customers; the product is new. Moderate risk. Needs research and development spending, and it can fail even with a well-understood market.

**Market development** — existing product, new market. You know the product; the customers are new. Moderate risk. New segment, new region or new country. The risk is cultural, regulatory and competitive unfamiliarity, and the product may need adapting.

**Diversification** — new product, new market. Highest risk, because nothing is familiar. Its attraction is spreading risk so the business does not depend on one market. Related diversification, into something adjacent, is less risky than unrelated.

</Chunk>

**How to use it in an answer:** classify the proposed strategy, state its risk level, then argue whether the business has the resources and capability for that level of risk. Do not just label it.

## Porter's Strategic Matrix

<Figure caption="Two decisions: compete on cost or on difference, and across a broad market or a narrow one. Porter's argument is that you must choose.">
  <PorterStrategyDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Cost leadership** — lowest cost in a broad market, so you can charge the lowest price and still profit. Needs scale, efficiency and tight cost control.
- **Differentiation** — broad market, but distinct enough to charge more. Needs branding, design, quality or service.
- **Cost focus** — lowest cost within a narrow segment.
- **Differentiation focus** — distinct within a narrow segment. The classic niche strategy.

**Stuck in the middle** is Porter's central claim: a business that is neither clearly cheapest nor clearly different has no reason for customers to choose it. It cannot win on price against the cost leader or on quality against the differentiator.

**The evaluation point:** the theory is criticised because some businesses have successfully done both, combining efficient operations with a strong brand. So treat "stuck in the middle" as a warning rather than a law, and say so.

## Portfolio analysis

The aim is **balance** across the whole range of products: cash generators funding growth products, some new bets being tested, weak products pruned. The Boston Matrix from WBS11 **1.3.3.1** is the standard tool.

A business that judges each product in isolation ends up either with no future growth or with nothing generating cash.

## Strategic and tactical decisions

| | Strategic | Tactical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time frame | long term | short term |
| Taken by | senior management or the board | middle or junior management |
| Risk | high | lower |
| Reversibility | difficult and costly | usually easy |
| Example | entering a new country | running a promotion this month |

The specification asks about the effect of these decisions on **human, physical and financial resources**:

- **Human** — strategic change needs new skills, recruitment, training, and it creates resistance. Tactical change rarely does.
- **Physical** — strategic change often needs new premises, equipment or systems, committed years ahead.
- **Financial** — strategic decisions commit large sums with a long and uncertain payback, which raises gearing and reduces flexibility.

That last point is why strategic decisions are hard to reverse: the money has already been spent on assets that cannot easily be sold.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse operator's options map onto Ansoff neatly.

**Penetration:** more marketing and better reviews to fill existing rooms on the existing island. Cheapest, lowest risk, and limited by the island's total demand.

**Product development:** add diving, excursions or a restaurant for the same guests. Moderate risk, and it uses the customer knowledge already held.

**Market development:** target a new source market, perhaps shifting from European to regional Asian visitors. The product is the same; the marketing, language and expectations are not.

**Diversification:** buy a boat charter business serving a different customer entirely. Highest risk, and it spreads dependence away from room nights alone.

For a small operator with limited finance, the sensible sequence is penetration first, then product development, precisely because risk rises with distance from what is already known.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Very heavily, often as the 20-mark essay. Define (2); Explain (4) on one strategy; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on which growth strategy a business should pursue.

**Structuring the big question:**

1. **Classify the options using Ansoff**, and state the risk of each.
2. **Argue for one**, chaining the reasoning to this business's resources, finance and capability.
3. **Argue against the alternative**, using its risk level.
4. **Bring in Porter** if relevant: does the proposed move fit its existing cost or differentiation position? A cost-focused business moving to a premium product is changing strategy, not just adding one.
5. **Evaluate:** finance available, the owner's objectives, how reversible it is, what the opportunity cost is.
6. Judge, and sequence it if you can. "Penetration first, then product development" is a more sophisticated answer than picking one.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Risk against reward.** Diversification spreads risk and takes on more of it in the short run.
- **Capability.** Does the business have the skills and cash for this move?
- **Stuck in the middle is contested**, so use it as a warning rather than a rule.
- **Strategic decisions are hard to reverse**, so the cost of being wrong is high.
- **Name the opportunity cost** of the option chosen.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Mixing up product development and market development.** Product development is a NEW product to EXISTING customers. Market development is an EXISTING product to NEW customers.
- **Labelling a strategy without judging whether the business can do it.**
- **Treating diversification as simply bad.** It is risky and it spreads risk, which is its purpose.
- **Confusing Ansoff with Porter.** Ansoff is about growth direction; Porter is about the basis of competitive advantage.
- **Stating "stuck in the middle" as an absolute law.** It is contested.
- **Confusing strategic and tactical decisions**, especially in a question about resources.
- **Ignoring finance.** Most growth strategies fail on cash rather than logic.

</Mistakes>
