## The hierarchy

> **Mission** (why we exist) → **Aims** (where we are going) → **Corporate objectives** (measurable targets for the business) → **Functional objectives** (targets for each department) → **Tactics** (what we do this month)

Each level should follow from the one above. If a corporate objective is to raise market share by 5%, the marketing objective might be to raise brand awareness to 60%, the operations objective to raise capacity by 20%, and the HR objective to recruit and train 30 staff. All three serve the same corporate objective.

**Why the hierarchy matters:** without it, departments optimise separately and pull against each other. Operations cuts cost by standardising while marketing promises customisation. Aligning objectives is what stops that.

## Mission statements

A mission statement answers: what do we do, for whom, and why does it matter?

**What a good one does:**

- Gives employees a shared sense of purpose, which is motivating.
- Guides decisions when no rule covers the situation.
- Signals to customers and investors what the business stands for.
- Provides the reference point for setting aims and objectives.

**Critical appraisal**, which the specification explicitly asks for:

- **Most are too vague to constrain anything.** "To delight our customers through world-class service" could belong to any business in any industry. If a rival could adopt it unchanged, it says nothing.
- **They are often PR rather than intent.** A statement about valuing people means little in a business with high turnover and low pay.
- **They are rarely measurable**, so nobody can tell whether the mission is being achieved.
- **Employees frequently cannot recall them**, which tells you how much they guide behaviour.
- **They can become a liability** if behaviour visibly contradicts them, because the gap becomes the story.

**The balanced view:** a specific, honest, believed mission statement genuinely aligns decisions. A generic one printed on a wall is decoration. Whether the business acts consistently with it is the test, and that is the judgement an Assess question wants.

## Corporate objectives

Common corporate objectives: profit, growth, market share, survival, diversification, return on capital, shareholder value, and increasingly social or environmental goals.

**SMART** is the standard test:

- **Specific** — which market, which product?
- **Measurable** — a number.
- **Achievable** — within the resources available.
- **Realistic** — given the market and competition.
- **Time-bound** — a deadline.

"Grow the business" fails every test. "Raise market share in the premium segment from 6% to 9% by December 2027" passes all five.

## Where objectives come from and why they change

They depend on the stage of the business, the state of the market, the owners' motives and pressure from stakeholders. They change when the environment changes, which is why a recession sees growth objectives replaced by survival ones almost overnight.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**A mission that constrains.** Businesses whose mission is explicitly built around a single idea, such as a low-cost airline committing to being the cheapest, find the mission genuinely useful: it rules options out. Anything that raises cost per seat is off the table, and that clarity is what makes the strategy coherent.

**A mission that does not.** Statements promising to be "the leading provider of innovative solutions" rule nothing out, which is exactly the criticism in the specification.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on the purpose of a mission statement or an objective; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on the usefulness of a mission statement, or on whether objectives are appropriate.

**Answering "how useful is this mission statement?"** Use the appraisal points properly:

1. **Quote it and test it.** Could a competitor adopt it unchanged? If yes, say so.
2. **Is it measurable?** Usually not, which limits its use as a target.
3. **Does the business's behaviour match it?** Use the source. A gap is the strongest criticism available.
4. **But grant the benefits:** shared purpose, decision guidance, external signalling.
5. Judge: useful if specific and believed, decorative otherwise.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Test the statement against the source's own evidence** of how the business behaves.
- **Distinguish mission, aims and objectives properly.** Many candidates use them interchangeably, and the hierarchy is examinable.
- **An objective that cannot be measured cannot be managed.**
- **Objectives conflict**, so pursuing one has a cost in another.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Using mission, aim and objective interchangeably.** The mission is purpose, aims are broad goals, objectives are measurable targets.
- **Accepting a mission statement at face value.** The specification asks for a critical appraisal.
- **Listing SMART without applying it** to the actual objective in the source.
- **Saying mission statements are useless.** The balanced answer is that a specific and believed one is genuinely useful.
- **Forgetting the hierarchy**, so functional objectives are discussed with no link to the corporate ones.
- **Ignoring that objectives change** with the stage of the business and the state of the market.

</Mistakes>
