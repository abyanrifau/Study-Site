## Strong and weak cultures

**A strong culture** means values are widely shared and genuinely held. Staff know how to behave without being told, decisions are consistent, and there is less need for supervision and rules. Recruits either fit or leave quickly.

- **Advantages:** consistency, motivation, lower supervision costs, a clear identity customers recognise.
- **Disadvantages:** resistance to change, groupthink, and difficulty absorbing people who think differently. A strong culture that is pointing the wrong way is worse than a weak one.

**A weak culture** means behaviour is driven by rules and supervision rather than belief. It is more flexible and easier to change, and it produces inconsistency and needs more management.

## Handy's four classifications

<Chunk title="The four types" open>

**Power culture.** Authority concentrated in one person or a small group, often the founder. Decisions are very fast, and everything depends on that individual being right. Common in small owner-managed businesses, and it becomes the constraint on growth, which is the entrepreneur-to-leader problem in WBS11 **1.3.4.5**.

**Role culture.** Built on clear job descriptions, procedures and hierarchy. Authority comes from your position rather than your expertise. Predictable, consistent and fair, and slow, bureaucratic and poor at responding to change. Common in large established organisations and the public sector.

**Task culture.** Organised around project teams assembled for a specific purpose, and disbanded afterwards. Expertise matters more than rank. Flexible, motivating and good at solving problems, and it needs resources and can produce unclear accountability. This is the culture that fits a matrix structure.

**Person culture.** Individuals regard themselves as more important than the organisation, which exists mainly to support them. Common among professionals such as doctors, lawyers or consultants sharing premises. Very hard to manage, because there is little shared purpose.

</Chunk>

## How culture is formed

- **The founder.** Their values, priorities and behaviour set the original pattern, and it persists long after they leave.
- **History and past success.** What worked before becomes "how we do things".
- **Who gets rewarded and promoted.** This is the strongest signal of all. A business that says it values teamwork and promotes the most aggressive individual has a culture of individual aggression, whatever the wall says.
- **Recruitment and selection.** Hiring for cultural fit reproduces the culture, and reduces diversity of thought.
- **Leadership style**, from WBS11 **1.3.4.5**.
- **The industry and the national context.** Safety-critical industries develop procedural cultures; creative industries develop looser ones.

**The point worth making:** culture is shaped far more by what a business *does*, particularly who it rewards, than by what it *says*. That is the sharpest way to criticise a mission statement in **3.3.1.1**.

## Why changing culture is so difficult

- **It is unwritten**, so there is nothing to amend. You cannot issue a memo changing beliefs.
- **The people are the culture.** Changing it means changing behaviour, and often the people.
- **Past success justifies it.** "This is how we became successful" is a powerful argument against change.
- **Fear and loss.** Change threatens status, routines and relationships.
- **Middle management resistance.** Those whose authority came from the old way have most to lose.
- **It takes years**, while senior managers are usually judged in quarters.
- **Announcing a change is not making one.** Staff watch whether behaviour and rewards actually change.

**What actually works:** changing what gets rewarded and promoted, visible consistent leadership behaviour, recruiting differently, restructuring so the old power bases go, and patience.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Culture clash in acquisitions.** Cross-border mergers repeatedly underperform because the acquirer assumes procedures transfer. Two workforces with different attitudes to hierarchy, working hours and decision-making do not merge because a contract was signed. It is the most common single cause of acquisition failure, which is why it comes up in **3.3.2.3**.

**Culture as a constraint on strategy.** A business with a deeply procedural role culture attempting a fast, innovative strategy usually fails, not because the strategy is wrong but because the organisation cannot execute it. Strategy has to fit the culture, or the culture has to change first.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2) on culture or one of Handy's types; Explain (4) on how culture is formed or why it is hard to change; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business can change its culture, or on whether a proposed strategy fits it.

**Answering "can they change their culture?"**

1. **Identify the current culture** from the source, using Handy's classification and the evidence.
2. **Say why change is being proposed** and what the gap is.
3. **Argue the difficulties**, using the reasons above and the specifics in the source.
4. **Say what would actually have to change:** rewards, promotions, leadership behaviour, possibly people.
5. **Judge:** possible but slow, and give the condition on which it depends.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Culture follows rewards, not statements.** The strongest point available.
- **It takes years**, and managers are judged in quarters.
- **A strong culture is an asset or a liability** depending on whether it points the right way.
- **Strategy must fit the culture**, or the culture has to change first, which takes longer than the strategy allows.
- **Whose culture wins** in an acquisition is rarely decided deliberately.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Describing culture as just "the atmosphere".** Define it as shared values, beliefs and ways of working.
- **Confusing power and role culture.** Power is concentrated in a person; role is based on position and procedure.
- **Saying a strong culture is always good.** It resists change and can produce groupthink.
- **Assuming culture can be changed by announcement.**
- **Ignoring who gets rewarded**, which is what actually forms culture.
- **Forgetting the link to acquisitions**, where culture clash is the main cause of failure.
- **Not classifying the culture in the source** before discussing changing it.

</Mistakes>
