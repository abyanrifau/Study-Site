## Why businesses grow

The specification names four objectives, and you should be able to develop each rather than list it.

<Chunk title="Economies of scale" open>

Average cost per unit falls as output rises. The mechanism is the same as in WBS12 **2.3.2.1**: fixed costs spread over more units, plus additional savings that only large scale allows.

**Internal economies** come from the business itself growing:

- **Purchasing** — bulk buying gets a lower price per unit.
- **Technical** — large specialised machinery is only worth buying at high volume, and larger plant is often proportionately cheaper to run.
- **Managerial** — specialists can be employed, a finance director, a marketing director, whose cost is spread over a much larger output.
- **Financial** — large businesses borrow more cheaply, because lenders see less risk.
- **Marketing** — the cost of a campaign is spread over far more units.

**External economies** come from the whole industry growing in one place: a pool of trained local labour, specialist suppliers nearby, better transport links, industry-specific training courses. The individual business benefits without having grown itself.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Market power over customers and suppliers">

**Over suppliers:** a large buyer can demand lower prices, longer credit and priority supply. In Porter's terms, growth reduces supplier power.

**Over customers:** fewer alternatives means a firm can hold prices higher and demand becomes less price elastic.

This is often the real motive behind growth, and it is also why competition policy exists (WBS12 **2.3.5.2**).

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Market share and brand recognition">

A larger share brings recognition, retailer willingness to stock you, and a platform for future price rises. It is also self-reinforcing: visibility drives sales, which drives visibility.

</Chunk>

<Chunk title="Increased profitability">

The intended result of the other three: lower unit costs plus the ability to charge more equals a wider margin, applied to a larger volume.

**The caution:** it does not always follow. Growth can raise profit in total while reducing it per unit, or raise revenue while diseconomies of scale push costs up faster. Higher revenue is not higher profit, and saying so is a reliable evaluation point.

</Chunk>

## Organic against inorganic

| | Organic | Inorganic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | slow | immediate |
| Cost | lower, funded from profits | high, often needs debt or shares |
| Risk | lower, incremental and controllable | high, most acquisitions disappoint |
| Control | retained | may be diluted if shares are issued |
| Culture | preserved | two cultures have to be merged |
| Finance | often internal | usually external |

**The one-line summary:** organic growth is slower and safer; inorganic growth buys time and takes on risk.

## Growth is a means, not an end

The point that lifts an answer. Growth is pursued because of what it delivers, lower costs, market power, profit, not for its own sake. So:

- **Growth that does not reduce unit costs** achieves nothing on the cost side.
- **Growth that outruns the cash** causes overtrading, as in WBS12 **2.3.3.3**.
- **Growth beyond the optimum** produces diseconomies of scale (**3.3.2.4**).
- **The owner may not want it.** Profit satisficing is a legitimate objective, from WBS11 **1.3.5.2**.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse operator growing from one property to four gets real economies: one booking system, one marketing effort, bulk purchasing of food and supplies, and a manager whose cost is spread across four properties instead of one.

The external economies matter too. As the industry on an island grows, boat transfers become more frequent, trained staff become available locally, and suppliers begin delivering regularly. A single guesthouse benefits from all of that without having grown itself at all, which is a clean example of an external economy.

The limit is also visible. Purchasing economies on imported food are real but capped by the size of the local market, and the biggest cost, the property itself, does not get cheaper per room with scale. So the economies are genuine and smaller than in manufacturing.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one objective of growth or one economy of scale; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business should grow, and how.

**Answering "should this business grow?"**

1. **What would growth deliver here?** Name the specific economies or market power available, using the source.
2. **Can it be financed?** This is where most growth plans fail. Check the cash.
3. **Organic or inorganic**, and why.
4. **What are the risks?** Diseconomies, overtrading, loss of control, culture.
5. **What does the owner want?** If the source shows satisficing, growth may be the wrong recommendation entirely.
6. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Growth is a means, not an end.** Ask what it is for.
- **Distinguish internal from external economies**, and note the business only controls the internal ones.
- **Higher revenue is not higher profit.**
- **There is an optimum size**, beyond which costs rise again.
- **Name the opportunity cost** of the capital committed to growth.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing internal and external economies.** Internal: the firm grew. External: the industry grew.
- **Saying "economies of scale" with no mechanism.** Name which one: purchasing, technical, managerial, financial or marketing.
- **Assuming growth raises profit automatically.**
- **Treating bigger as better.** There is an optimum, and the owner may not want to reach it.
- **Ignoring finance.** Growth consumes cash before it generates it.
- **Confusing market power over suppliers with power over customers.**
- **Forgetting the link to overtrading**, which is the standard way growth kills a business.

</Mistakes>
