## What each statement shows

**Statement of comprehensive income — performance over a period.**

Key information: revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit, interest, tax, profit for the year. Read over two years it shows whether the business is growing, and whether costs are under control.

**Statement of financial position — position at one date.**

Key information: non-current assets, current assets, current and non-current liabilities, and equity. It shows what the business owns, what it owes, how it is financed, and whether it can meet its short-term obligations.

**The distinction to state:** one is a film of the year, the other a photograph of one day. That is why a business can show a healthy profit and a weak position, or the reverse.

## Stakeholder interest

The specification asks specifically what different stakeholders look for, and this is where the marks are rather than in describing the statements.

| Stakeholder | What they look for | Which statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shareholders** | profit, dividends, return on their investment, growth in value | mainly income statement, plus equity |
| **Lenders and banks** | ability to repay: gearing, liquidity, interest cover, assets as security | mainly financial position |
| **Suppliers** | will we be paid? liquidity and trade payables | mainly financial position |
| **Employees and unions** | job security, ability to pay higher wages, profit available to share | mainly income statement |
| **Managers** | performance against targets, where costs are rising, what to fix | both |
| **Government** | tax due, employment, compliance | both |
| **Customers** | will the business survive to honour warranties and supply? | mainly financial position |
| **Local community** | stability, employment, expansion plans | both |

**The exam-relevant insight:** the same figures support opposite conclusions depending on who is reading. A large profit pleases shareholders, prompts a union pay claim, and attracts government tax attention. A big investment in non-current assets reassures a lender about security and worries a supplier about the cash consumed. Making that point explicitly is a strong evaluation move.

## Limitations

- **Historical.** They report what happened, and stakeholders want to know what will happen.
- **The position statement is one day.** It can be arranged to look better than the year as a whole, which is window dressing.
- **Nothing non-financial.** Staff morale, brand strength, customer satisfaction, innovation and reputation are all absent, and all affect competitiveness.
- **Accounting policies differ**, so two businesses' figures may not be directly comparable.
- **No context.** A fall in profit during deliberate expansion is not the same as a fall from losing control of costs.
- **Aggregated.** Total figures hide which product or region is performing.

**So a proper assessment of competitiveness needs the statements plus the human resource measures in 3.3.5.3 plus market information such as share and customer satisfaction.** That completeness point is worth making in any question about assessing competitiveness.

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Usually as part of a larger question: Analyse (6) what the statements show, or Evaluate (20) on the business's performance, where the ratios in **3.3.5.2** do the calculating and this topic supplies the interpretation and the stakeholder angle.

**Answering "what would stakeholder X look for?"** Name the stakeholder's interest, say which figures or ratios address it, and say what conclusion they would draw. A bank looks at gearing and liquidity because it wants to know the loan will be serviced; it cares far less about revenue growth than a shareholder does.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The same figures support different conclusions** for different stakeholders.
- **The statements are historical**, and competitiveness is about the future.
- **Non-financial information is missing** and may matter more.
- **One date can be arranged to flatter.**
- **What else would you want to see?** Naming it shows you understand the limits of the data.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing the two statements.** Income statement: performance over a period. Financial position: one date.
- **Describing the statements instead of interpreting them.**
- **Treating all stakeholders as wanting the same thing.**
- **Forgetting the non-financial dimension** of competitiveness.
- **Assuming a profitable business is financially healthy.** Check liquidity and gearing too.
- **Using the old names** without also using the current ones. The assessments use International Accounting Standards terminology.

</Mistakes>
