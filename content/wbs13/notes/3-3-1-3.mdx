## The four boxes

<Figure caption="The top row is about the business itself. The bottom row is about the world it operates in. Getting an item in the wrong row is the most common error.">
  <SwotDiagram />
</Figure>

**Internal (things the business controls or owns):** brand strength, staff skills, cash reserves, patents, location, cost base, reputation, product range, management quality.

**External (things happening to it):** a growing market, a new technology, a competitor's failure, a change in the law, a recession, a new entrant, a shift in tastes, an exchange rate movement.

**The test:** can the business change it directly? If yes, it is internal. If it would still be true whether or not this business existed, it is external.

## Using it properly

A list of four boxes is worth very little. The value comes from the connections between them, and the strongest answers make those connections explicit:

- **Strength + opportunity** → the obvious move. Use what you are good at to exploit what is happening. This is where strategy usually comes from.
- **Weakness + opportunity** → the opportunity exists and you cannot currently take it. Either fix the weakness or partner with someone who has that capability.
- **Strength + threat** → use the strength to defend. A strong brand and cash reserves are what let a business survive a price war.
- **Weakness + threat** → the danger zone. This is where businesses fail, and it is where a SWOT earns its keep by identifying the combination early.

So in an exam, do not just classify. Say: **this strength, applied to this opportunity, suggests this strategy.**

## Limitations

The specification expects a critical view.

- **Subjective.** Whoever writes it decides what counts. Managers overstate strengths and understate weaknesses.
- **A snapshot.** It describes one moment and the environment moves.
- **It lists without prioritising.** Twenty items with no weighting is not a strategy. Which threat actually matters?
- **No guidance on action.** Identifying an opportunity does not tell you whether you can profitably take it.
- **Items can be ambiguous.** The same fact can be a strength or a weakness depending on context. A specialised, highly efficient factory is a strength while demand holds and a weakness when the market shifts, because it cannot make anything else. Saying this is a high-level point.
- **It can be used to justify a decision already made**, by selecting the items that support it.

## Making it useful

- Be specific and evidenced rather than general. "Strong brand" is weak; "highest review score on the island at 9.1" is usable.
- Prioritise, so the two or three items that matter most are identified.
- Keep it short. A long SWOT is a sign nobody has decided anything.
- Follow it with a decision, and revisit it when the environment changes.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

A guesthouse SWOT, done properly:

**Strengths:** highest review score on the island, owner-managed so service is personal, no debt.
**Weaknesses:** only eight rooms so capacity is capped, entirely dependent on one booking platform, no marketing in languages other than English.
**Opportunities:** growth in regional Asian visitor numbers, direct booking through its own site to avoid platform commission.
**Threats:** three new guesthouses opening on the island, platform commission increases, weather disruption to transfers.

**Now the connections, which is the actual analysis.** The strong review score (strength) plus growing regional demand (opportunity) suggests translating the site and targeting that market. But the single-platform dependence (weakness) against rising commission (threat) is the danger combination, and it argues for building direct bookings as the priority. That is a strategy, and it came out of pairing the boxes rather than listing them.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on the difference between a weakness and a threat; and very often as part of a larger Assess or Evaluate question about strategy, where you are expected to use a SWOT rather than describe one.

**If asked to conduct a SWOT from a source:** take items directly from the source, classify them correctly, and then pair them. Do not invent items the source does not support.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **It is subjective**, and the person writing it has an interest in the answer.
- **It does not prioritise**, so it cannot tell you what to do first.
- **The same item can be both**, depending on circumstances.
- **It dates quickly**, so a SWOT from last year may be misleading now.
- **The value is in the pairings**, not the lists. Say so and demonstrate it.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Putting an external factor in the internal row.** A competitor opening nearby is a threat, not a weakness. The weakness would be having no differentiation to defend with.
- **Confusing weaknesses and threats.** Weaknesses are yours; threats are out there.
- **Listing without analysing.** The classification earns little; the pairings earn the marks.
- **Being vague.** "Good staff" against "staff with dive qualifications nobody else on the island has".
- **Producing a long list** with no sense of which items matter.
- **Inventing items** not supported by the source.
- **Treating the SWOT as the answer.** It is the analysis that leads to an answer.

</Mistakes>
