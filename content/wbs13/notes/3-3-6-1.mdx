## Causes of change

**Internal:** new leadership, growth, a new strategy, poor performance, a merger, new technology adopted.

**External:** competitor action, recession, new regulation, technological disruption, exchange rate movements, changing tastes. These are the PESTLE factors from **3.3.1.4**.

The useful distinction is **planned against imposed change**. Planned change is chosen, timed and resourced. Imposed change arrives whether the business is ready or not, and is far harder to manage because there is no time to prepare people. A business that is already flexible converts imposed change into something closer to planned change, which is the whole argument for flexibility.

## Types of organisational change

<Chunk title="Four kinds the specification names" open>

**Flexible workforces.** Part-time, temporary and zero-hour contracts, multi-skilling, flexible hours, outsourcing and homeworking. They let the business match capacity to demand and redeploy people as needs change.

- **For:** lower fixed costs, quicker response, and multi-skilled staff cover absence and bottlenecks.
- **Against:** insecurity damages motivation and loyalty, temporary staff are less committed and less trained, and the ethical objections in **3.3.4.3** apply.

**Delayering.** Removing management levels.

- **For:** lower costs, faster communication and decisions, and more empowerment for those below.
- **Against:** wider spans of control stretch the remaining managers, lost expertise, fewer promotion steps so career paths disappear, and the fear generated during the process damages productivity.

**Restructuring.** Changing the shape of the organisation, for example from functional to divisional as the business grows internationally.

- **For:** fits the structure to the strategy, clarifies accountability, and can bring decisions closer to customers.
- **Against:** it is disruptive, duplicates functions, and breaks informal networks that were doing real work.

**Changing organisational culture.** Covered in **3.3.4.1**, and the slowest and hardest of the four.

</Chunk>

## Managing information

The specification highlights this, and it is where most change programmes fail.

- **Say why, not just what.** People accept change they understand the reason for.
- **Communicate early.** In the absence of information, staff invent worse versions than the truth.
- **Two-way, not broadcast.** Consultation surfaces practical objections that managers have not thought of, and it converts resistance into ownership.
- **Be honest about losses.** Pretending nobody loses anything destroys trust for the rest of the programme.
- **Repeat it.** One announcement is not communication.
- **Match words with behaviour.** Staff judge the change by what managers actually do, especially who is rewarded and promoted.

## Resistance to change, and why it is rational

- **Fear of the unknown**, of job losses, of not coping with new methods.
- **Loss of status or power**, which is why middle managers resist delayering hardest.
- **Habit and comfort** with existing routines.
- **Loss of relationships** when teams are broken up.
- **Distrust of management**, particularly where previous changes went badly.
- **Genuine disagreement.** Sometimes the people doing the work can see that the plan will not work, and dismissing that as resistance is a serious management error.

**Reducing it:** communication and consultation, involvement in designing the change, training so people can cope with new demands, phasing rather than imposing everything at once, incentives, and visible senior commitment. Force is available and leaves lasting damage.

## Force field analysis

<Chunk title="How to use it">

Lewin's technique:

1. State the proposed change.
2. List the **driving forces** pushing for it, and score each for strength, typically 1 to 5.
3. List the **restraining forces** opposing it, and score each.
4. Total both sides.

If driving forces outweigh restraining forces, the change can proceed. If not, it will stall.

**The insight that earns marks:** you can make change happen either by strengthening the drivers or by weakening the restrainers, and **weakening the restrainers is usually cheaper and more effective**. Adding more pressure to a resisting organisation raises the tension on both sides; removing a specific fear through training or a guarantee removes the resistance itself.

**Limitations:** the scores are subjective, it is a snapshot, and it lists forces without telling you how they interact.

</Chunk>

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Delayering and the missing middle.** Businesses that removed a management layer to cut costs often found later that the layer had been doing coordination, coaching and informal problem-solving that never appeared in any job description. The saving was visible immediately and the cost appeared later as overstretched senior managers and no succession pipeline.

**Technology-driven change.** Retail banks moving to app-based service had to change branch roles, staff skills and the culture of the business at the same time as the technology. The technology was the easy part; the people were not.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4) on one cause of resistance or one benefit of a flexible workforce; Analyse (6); Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on how a business should manage a specific change, or whether a proposed change will succeed.

**Structuring the big question:**

1. **Identify the change** and whether it is planned or imposed.
2. **Identify the driving and restraining forces** in the source, which is a natural use of force field analysis.
3. **Name the specific resistance** you would expect, and from whom.
4. **Recommend how to manage it:** communication, consultation, training, phasing.
5. **Judge whether it will succeed**, and give the condition it depends on.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Weakening restrainers beats strengthening drivers.**
- **Resistance is often rational**, and may contain information the plan needs.
- **Planned change is manageable; imposed change is not**, so flexibility is the real protection.
- **Culture change takes years**, longer than the strategy usually allows.
- **Communication is necessary and not sufficient.** People watch behaviour and rewards.
- **It depends on the speed required**, because a phased change is better managed and may arrive too late.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing types of change without evaluating them.**
- **Treating resistance as irrational.** It has causes, and sometimes good ones.
- **Assuming communication alone solves resistance.**
- **Confusing delayering with downsizing.** Delayering removes a level of management; downsizing reduces the total workforce.
- **Forgetting the cost of a flexible workforce** in motivation and loyalty.
- **Using force field analysis without scoring** the forces or drawing a conclusion from the totals.
- **Ignoring the time available.** A well-managed slow change can still be the wrong answer.

</Mistakes>
