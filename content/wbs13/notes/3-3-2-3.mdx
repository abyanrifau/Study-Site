## Merger or takeover

**Merger** — agreed, usually between businesses of similar size, forming a new combined entity. Friendlier, and it raises the question of who is really in charge.

**Takeover** — one business buys a controlling stake in another. Can be agreed or **hostile**, where the target's board resists. The acquirer is clearly in control.

In practice the distinction blurs, and many "mergers" are takeovers presented politely. In an exam, use the terms precisely.

## Types of integration

| Type | What it is | Main reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Horizontal** | same industry, same stage | market share, economies of scale, fewer competitors |
| **Backward vertical** | acquire a supplier | secure supply, control input costs and quality |
| **Forward vertical** | acquire a distributor or retailer | control the route to market and the margin on it |
| **Conglomerate** | unrelated industry | spread risk, diversify |

<Chunk title="What each achieves, and its risk" open>

**Horizontal.** Immediate market share, economies of scale, and one fewer competitor. It is the most likely type to be blocked by competition authorities, and the most likely to produce duplication that has to be cut, which means redundancies and resistance.

**Backward vertical.** Secures supply and takes the supplier's margin. Reduces supplier power in Porter's terms. The risk is running a business you do not understand, and losing the flexibility to switch supplier when a better one appears.

**Forward vertical.** Controls how the product is presented and sold, and captures the retail margin. The risk is the same: retailing is a different skill from manufacturing, and you may end up competing with the other retailers who stock you.

**Conglomerate.** Spreads risk across unrelated markets, so a downturn in one is cushioned by another. The problem is that there is no synergy, no shared cost base, no shared customers, and management has no expertise in the new industry. The evaluation point is that shareholders could diversify their own portfolios more cheaply than the company can diversify for them.

</Chunk>

## Reasons for mergers and takeovers

Speed above all: instant scale, market share, capability, brands and market access. Plus:

- **Synergy**, through cost savings or cross-selling.
- **Acquiring capability** the business could not build quickly: skills, patents, technology, a brand.
- **Market access**, including entry to a new country with an established local operation.
- **Reducing competition** by buying a rival.
- **Asset stripping**, buying an undervalued business to sell its parts for more than the whole.
- **Management ego**, which sounds flippant and is a documented reason large acquisitions happen.

## Financial risks and rewards

**Rewards:** immediate revenue and market share, cost savings from removing duplication, a higher combined valuation if synergies are real.

**Risks:**

- **Overpayment.** The acquirer pays a premium for control, and the premium often exceeds the synergies. This is the single most common reason acquisitions fail.
- **Debt.** Acquisitions are frequently debt-funded, which raises gearing and interest costs and reduces resilience.
- **Synergies are overestimated.** They are easy to promise and hard to deliver.
- **Hidden liabilities**, which is what due diligence is for and does not always find.
- **Dilution.** If shares are issued to pay for it, existing shareholders own less.

## Advantages and disadvantages overall

**For:** speed, instant market share, capability bought rather than built, economies of scale, one fewer competitor, and access to new markets.

**Against:** high cost and often a premium price, integration difficulty, **culture clash**, redundancies and resistance, distraction of senior management, regulatory intervention, and the well-documented fact that most acquisitions do not create value for the acquirer.

**Culture clash deserves emphasis** because it is the answer examiners look for. Two workforces with different ways of working, different pay structures and different loyalties do not merge because a contract was signed. Integration failure is more common than financial failure.

<RealWorld where="Across countries">

**Horizontal integration.** Airline mergers consolidate routes and fleets and repeatedly attract competition authority scrutiny, because fewer carriers on a route means higher fares.

**Vertical integration.** Streaming services buying production studios is backward integration: securing the content supply rather than bidding for it against rivals.

**Conglomerates.** Large diversified groups across Asia and Latin America span unrelated industries. They spread risk effectively and are frequently valued at a discount, because investors doubt that one management team can run banking, cement and retail well.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Very heavily, often as the 20-mark essay. Define (2) on merger, takeover or an integration type; Explain (4) on one reason or risk; Evaluate (20) on whether a business should acquire another.

**Structuring the 20-mark answer:**

1. **Classify the proposed deal.** Horizontal, vertical or conglomerate, and say what that type is normally for.
2. **Argue for it**, using the specific synergies available in the source.
3. **Argue against it**, leading with overpayment and culture.
4. **Bring in the finance.** How is it being paid for, and what does that do to gearing?
5. **Evaluate:** is the price justified, can the two be integrated, is there a cheaper organic alternative?
6. Judge.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Most acquisitions fail to create value for the acquirer.** State it and give the reasons: overpayment and integration.
- **Synergies are promised before the deal and measured after it**, if at all.
- **Culture clash is the usual cause of failure**, not the numbers.
- **Conglomerate diversification can be done more cheaply by shareholders themselves.**
- **Compare with organic growth.** Would building it be slower and safer, and is speed actually necessary here?

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing forward and backward vertical integration.** Backward goes towards the supplier; forward goes towards the customer.
- **Confusing horizontal with vertical.** Horizontal is the same stage; vertical is a different stage.
- **Assuming synergy will be achieved.** It is a forecast, not a fact.
- **Ignoring the price paid.** A good business bought at a bad price is a bad acquisition.
- **Leaving out culture**, which is the most common cause of failure.
- **Forgetting competition policy**, especially for horizontal deals.
- **Treating conglomerate diversification as obviously sensible.** There is no synergy and no management expertise.
- **Not comparing with the organic alternative.**

</Mistakes>
