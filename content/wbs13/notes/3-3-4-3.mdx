## Ethics beyond the law

The law is the floor, not the standard. A decision can be entirely legal and still be criticised as wrong: paying the legal minimum to staff who cannot live on it, using tax structures that are legal and widely seen as avoidance, marketing to children, sourcing from suppliers with poor conditions where local law permits it.

**The examinable tension:** ethics costs money in the short run. Paying suppliers fairly, sourcing responsibly, refusing profitable but questionable business, all reduce this year's profit. That is the trade-off, and pretending it does not exist produces weak answers.

## Ethics of strategic decisions

Where the profit-against-ethics trade-off usually appears:

- **Sourcing.** Cheaper suppliers with worse pay, conditions or environmental standards.
- **Location.** Producing where labour is cheapest and regulation lightest.
- **Marketing.** What is claimed, to whom, and how honestly.
- **Tax.** Legal minimisation against paying a share seen as fair.
- **Redundancy.** How people are treated when a site closes.
- **Environment.** Spending on emissions and waste that no law requires.

**The argument for prioritising profit:** the business's duty is to its owners, competitors that cut costs will undercut you, and jobs depend on remaining viable. A business that prices itself out of the market helps nobody.

**The argument against:** reputational damage is expensive and slow to repair, scandals destroy revenue faster than cost savings created it, staff do not want to work for a business they are ashamed of, and regulation usually follows public anger, so getting ahead of it is cheaper than being forced.

## Pay and rewards

The specification names this specifically.

- **Executive pay ratios.** When the chief executive earns hundreds of times the median employee, it raises questions of fairness and affects morale. Defenders point to the market for talent and the scale of responsibility.
- **Low pay.** Paying the legal minimum to staff who cannot meet living costs is legal and widely criticised. Some businesses adopt a voluntary living wage.
- **Gender and ethnicity pay gaps.** Increasingly reported and scrutinised.
- **Zero-hour contracts.** Flexible for the employer, insecure for the worker, from WBS11 **1.3.4.1**.
- **Performance incentives.** Bonuses tied to short-term targets can encourage exactly the behaviour that causes scandals.

That last point is the sharpest: **you get the behaviour you reward**, so an unethical outcome is often the predictable result of an incentive scheme rather than of bad individuals.

## Corporate social responsibility

CSR means going beyond legal obligation: environmental commitments, community investment, ethical sourcing, charitable giving, reporting on social and environmental performance.

**The case for:**

- **Reputation and differentiation.** It can be a genuine competitive advantage where customers care.
- **Staff attraction and retention**, particularly among younger employees.
- **Risk reduction.** It heads off regulation, boycotts and scandal.
- **Access to markets.** Large customers increasingly demand standards from their suppliers.
- **Genuine cost savings.** Using less energy, material and waste is cheaper, so sustainability and efficiency often point the same way.

**The case against:**

- **It costs money**, and shareholders may not have agreed to it.
- **Competitors who do not do it have lower costs.**
- **Customers frequently say they will pay more and then do not**, which is the crucial gap to notice.
- **It can be greenwashing**, in which case the reputational risk is worse than doing nothing, because the gap between claim and behaviour becomes the story.
- **It can be a distraction** from running the business well.

## The convergence point

As in **3.3.4.2**, the honest position is that **ethics and profit conflict in the short run and align over a long enough horizon** for most businesses. Where they genuinely and permanently conflict, the business is making a values choice, and that is a legitimate thing for an owner to do, as in WBS11 **1.3.5.2**.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Two ethical issues are unavoidable in the sector.

**Environmental.** Reef damage from construction, waste disposal and sewage. Here ethics and long-term profit align almost perfectly, because the reef is the product. Damaging it to save money destroys the asset, so an environmental stance is also a commercial one.

**Labour.** Staff on remote island properties may work long seasons far from family, living in staff accommodation. Legal standards exist; the ethical questions concern rest days, accommodation quality, family contact and pay relative to the rates guests pay. This one is a genuine short-run cost with a long-run return through retention, and retention matters enormously where a departing dive instructor cannot be replaced mid-season.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Define (2); Explain (4) on one benefit or cost of CSR; Assess (12) or Evaluate (20) on whether a business should adopt a particular ethical position, or on the trade-off between profit and ethics.

**Structuring the answer:**

1. **Identify the specific ethical issue** in the source, not ethics in general.
2. **Quantify the short-run cost** if the figures allow it.
3. **Argue the commercial case for behaving ethically:** reputation, retention, risk, market access.
4. **Argue the commercial case against:** cost, competitor advantage, and the gap between what customers say and do.
5. **Distinguish short run from long run**, which is the central analytical move.
6. **Judge**, and note that it depends on the customers, the visibility of the issue and the owner's objectives.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **The trade-off is real in the short run** and often disappears over the long run.
- **Customers say they will pay more for ethics and frequently do not.** Naming that gap is a genuinely sharp point.
- **You get the behaviour you reward**, so incentive schemes cause unethical outcomes.
- **Greenwashing is worse than silence**, because the gap becomes the story.
- **Where the environment is the product**, ethics and profit align.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Confusing ethics with law.** Ethics is what is right, which goes beyond what is required.
- **Claiming ethics always costs money.** Waste reduction and retention often save it.
- **Claiming ethics always pays.** In the short run it usually costs, and the honest answer acknowledges the trade-off.
- **Assuming customers will pay a premium** without evidence from the source.
- **Discussing ethics in general** rather than the specific issue in the question.
- **Ignoring competitor behaviour.** An ethical stance in a market where nobody else has one carries a cost disadvantage.
- **Forgetting the incentive link.** Unethical behaviour is often the predictable result of how people are paid.

</Mistakes>
