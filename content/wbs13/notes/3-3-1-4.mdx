## PESTLE

<Figure caption="Six categories of external influence. The value is in identifying which two or three actually matter for this business, not in listing all six.">
  <PestleDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Political** — government policy, stability, trade policy, subsidies, public spending.
- **Economic** — growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, incomes, unemployment. Covered in WBS12 **2.3.5.1**.
- **Social** — demographics, tastes, values, lifestyles, education levels, attitudes to work.
- **Technological** — automation, new products, new channels, data, and the obsolescence of what exists.
- **Legal** — employment, consumer, environmental, competition and intellectual property law. Covered in WBS12 **2.3.5.2**.
- **Environmental** — climate, resource availability, waste, weather, and pressure to be sustainable.

**How to use it well:** a full PESTLE listing six factors earns little. Identify the **two or three that genuinely matter** for this business and develop those into chains. An examiner would far rather see one developed technological threat than six labelled headings.

## The changing competitive environment

Industries do not stand still. What changes competition:

- **New entrants**, especially where technology has lowered barriers to entry.
- **Technological disruption**, which can remove an industry rather than compete with it.
- **Consolidation**, as mergers reduce the number of players and increase their size.
- **Globalisation**, bringing overseas competitors into what was a local market.
- **Changing customer power**, particularly through online price comparison and reviews.

## Porter's five forces

<Figure caption="Five forces determine how much profit there is in an industry. Strong forces mean thin profits for everyone in it.">
  <FiveForcesDiagram />
</Figure>

The framework explains **industry profitability**, not one firm's performance. That distinction matters.

<Chunk title="What makes each force strong" open>

**Competitive rivalry — strong when:** many similar-sized competitors, slow market growth, little differentiation, high fixed costs pushing firms to fill capacity, high exit barriers.

**Supplier power — strong when:** few suppliers, no substitutes for their input, switching is costly, the supplier's product is critical to your quality, or the supplier could integrate forwards into your market.

**Buyer power — strong when:** few large buyers, the product is undifferentiated, switching costs are low, buyers are price sensitive, or buyers could produce it themselves.

**Threat of new entrants — strong when:** low capital requirements, no licensing, easy access to distribution, no brand loyalty, no economies of scale to overcome. In short, low barriers to entry.

**Threat of substitutes — strong when:** other ways of meeting the same need exist, are cheap, and are easy to switch to. Note this means *different* products meeting the same need, not rival brands of the same product.

</Chunk>

**The conclusion:** an industry where all five forces are strong is a bad industry to be in, because the profit is competed away. Where the forces are weak, profits are high and sustainable.

**What a business does about it:** build barriers to entry through brand and scale, reduce supplier power by dual sourcing or backward integration, reduce buyer power by differentiating so comparison is harder, and avoid the most crowded parts of the industry.

## Limitations of both frameworks

- **They describe, they do not predict.** They organise what is happening; they do not tell you what happens next.
- **They are snapshots**, and both date quickly.
- **They encourage listing.** Six PESTLE headings or five forces named and not developed is a weak answer.
- **Five forces was designed for manufacturing industries** and fits digital markets less neatly, where network effects and platforms behave differently.
- **Neither weighs the factors.** Which force or influence actually matters most is a judgement the framework does not make for you.

<RealWorld where="Maldives">

Five forces applied to the guesthouse industry explains why margins are thinner than the headline rates suggest.

**Rivalry: high.** Many similar small properties on the same island, little real differentiation, and all visible side by side online.

**Buyer power: high.** Guests compare twenty properties in seconds and switch for a small saving. The booking platforms themselves are also powerful buyers of inventory, taking commission of roughly 15 to 20%.

**Supplier power: moderate to high.** Imported goods come through few channels, and transfer boat operators on a given island may be the only option.

**New entrants: high.** Low capital requirements relative to a resort, and no brand needed to appear on a platform.

**Substitutes: high.** A guest could choose a resort, a liveaboard, or a different country entirely.

Four or five strong forces means the industry competes profit away, which is exactly why differentiation, diving, food, hosting, local knowledge, is the only sensible strategy. That conclusion came from the framework rather than from intuition, which is what using it properly looks like.

</RealWorld>

<Tip>

**How this topic is tested.** Explain (4); Analyse (6); and frequently as part of a 20-mark Evaluate on strategy, where the framework is the tool rather than the subject.

**Using five forces in an answer:**

1. Assess each force as strong, moderate or weak, **with a reason from the source**.
2. Conclude on industry profitability.
3. Say what the business should do about the strongest force specifically.

That third step is what most candidates omit, and it is where the marks are.

**Using PESTLE in an answer:** pick the two or three that matter, develop each into a chain, and say which is most significant and why.

**Getting evaluation marks:**

- **Which force or factor matters most?** Rank them.
- **Both frameworks describe rather than predict.**
- **Five forces analyses the industry, not the firm.** A strong firm can do well in a bad industry.
- **They date quickly**, particularly where technology is changing barriers to entry.
- **Some factors interact.** Low barriers to entry plus high buyer power is far worse than either alone.

</Tip>

<Mistakes>

- **Listing six PESTLE factors with no development.** Two developed beats six labelled.
- **Confusing substitutes with rivals.** A substitute meets the same need a different way; a rival sells a similar product.
- **Confusing supplier power with buyer power.** Suppliers sell to you; buyers buy from you.
- **Treating five forces as a measure of one firm's strength.** It measures industry profitability.
- **Naming the forces without saying strong or weak, and why.**
- **Forgetting to say what the business should do** about the forces identified.
- **Ignoring that these are snapshots** of a moving environment.

</Mistakes>
